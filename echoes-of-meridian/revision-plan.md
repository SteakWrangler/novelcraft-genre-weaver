## MUST FIX #1: Give Hadley a Real Argument

### Problem
Hadley functions as a speed bump. He locks Lena down, Kai walks to a public terminal and emails everyone, done. His authority evaporates instantly. The story raises the stakes of unmanaged disclosure and then suffers zero consequences from it. The reader never has to seriously weigh Hadley's position.

### Fix 1A: Add consequences to the leak (Ch5, after "The world found out in stages")
**Location:** Ch5, lines ~917-928. The current "Reuters moved the story first" passage covers the leak's spread in a single paragraph and then immediately cuts to Hadley showing up impotently.

**Currently exists:** A clean, triumphant sequence -- Reuters runs it, universities post it, labs verify it, Hadley's memo is exposed, the Secretary-General calls a session. No friction. No cost.

**Replace with:** A longer sequence (approx. 400 words) showing real fallout alongside the verification. Include:
- Financial markets: a flash crash triggered by algorithmic trading reacting to the headline. Trading halts on three exchanges. The Dow drops 2,200 points in forty minutes before circuit breakers kick in. It recovers partially but the damage to pension funds and savings is real.
- Religious reaction: Vatican issues an emergency statement within hours. An evangelical megachurch pastor in Texas declares the data Satanic on a livestream watched by 11 million. Riots outside a mosque in Lahore where a rumor circulates that the Ketharan are anti-Islamic. Two people are hospitalized.
- Political fracture: Brazil's president denounces the leak as "American information warfare." China's foreign ministry demands the genetic data be shared through WHO, not Western universities. India recalls its UNOOSA ambassador for consultations.
- A single vivid personal detail: Lena sees a clip of a woman in Seoul holding a candle in a crowd, weeping, and she doesn't know if the woman is grieving or celebrating, and that ambiguity is the point.

Then Hadley arrives -- and his line "You understand that what you've done constitutes--" now carries genuine weight. He was right that people would panic. Some did. Lena's choice was correct, but it was not clean, and the reader should feel that.

**Word count impact:** +400 words.

### Fix 1B: Give Hadley a specific, defensible counterargument (Ch5, Hadley confrontation in Lena's quarters)
**Location:** Ch5, lines ~836-868. The scene where Hadley revokes her clearance.

**Currently exists:** Hadley says "people will panic, governments will fracture, markets will collapse." These are generic warnings. He sounds like a bureaucrat reciting a checklist.

**Replace the dialogue exchange with:** Hadley citing a specific historical precedent he personally lived through. For example: "In 2011, I was deputy liaison when the WikiLeaks cables hit. A CIA asset in Islamabad was named in a cable about drone strikes. His family was dead within seventy-two hours. That's what unmanaged disclosure looks like. Not abstractions -- a man and his two daughters." This makes his fear concrete and earned. He should also articulate why Sable's terms are less dangerous than they appear: "The census is voluntary in the protocol. The advisory placements require host-nation consent. I negotiated those terms. The memorandum you found is a first draft, not a fait accompli. If you leak it without that context, you hand every authoritarian government on Earth a justification to militarize their response."

Lena should not be able to fully rebut this. She should acknowledge the logic and choose to proceed anyway -- not because Hadley is wrong about the risks, but because she believes the alternative (one man controlling the narrative for 8 billion people) is worse. The reader should feel the genuine moral tension.

**Word count impact:** +150 words net (some current dialogue trimmed).

### Fix 1C: Hadley is not simply irrelevant in Ch6
**Location:** Ch6, lines ~1171. The paragraph beginning "Hadley was still at his desk in Washington."

**Currently exists:** Lena dismisses him in a single paragraph. "She found she didn't need him to be punished. She just needed him to be irrelevant, and he was."

**Replace with:** A more nuanced resolution (approx. 150 words). Hadley's regulatory frameworks -- the ones no one asked for -- should be described as genuinely useful. The open-exchange framework is messy and slow, and quietly, Hadley's procedural documents are being cited by three delegations as starting templates. Lena should recognize this with discomfort: the man who tried to control the story is now, inadvertently, providing the scaffolding for the story's next chapter. His instinct to build structure was never the problem. His instinct to build it alone was. This makes him a tragic figure rather than a cardboard antagonist.

**Word count impact:** +80 words net.

**Total for Must Fix #1:** +630 words.

## MUST FIX #2: Dramatize Sable's Transformation

### Problem
Sable shifts from custodial diplomat to humbled equal entirely offscreen. We see an argument through a visual-only feed (Ch5, lines ~932-945). We hear the result in a speech (Ch6, lines ~1039-1058). We never witness the actual moment their worldview cracks.

### Fix 2A: Add a Sable POV scene or direct confrontation (new scene, Ch6, before the General Assembly speech)
**Location:** Insert between the current Ch6 opening (the cafeteria runs out of coffee) and the General Assembly session. Approximately line 1031.

**Currently exists:** A direct cut from "Lena slept nine hours" to the Assembly hall being reconfigured.

**Add a scene (approx. 500 words) in which Lena has a private conversation with Sable via holo-link before the Assembly session.** The scene should:

- Open with Sable requesting the call, not Lena. This matters -- Sable is choosing to be vulnerable.
- Show Sable struggling with a specific realization, not a generic one. The trigger: they have been reviewing Earth's genetic data (the data Lena released) and have discovered that the "colonial drift" they expected to find -- degradation, accumulated errors, loss of designed traits -- is not there. Instead, the drift is adaptive. Humans developed lactose tolerance, altitude adaptation, sickle-cell resistance to malaria. The "errors" are improvements. The colony didn't degrade. It innovated. And the Continuance's own genome, by comparison, shows signs of over-curation -- genetic diversity has been managed so carefully that certain adaptive pathways have been pruned. Sable's people are, in a measurable sense, less resilient than their "lost" colony.
- Sable should express this not as a clinical observation but as a personal crisis. They have spent nineteen years in transit believing they were coming to rescue. The data says the rescue was unnecessary. The data says the colony may have surpassed the parent. They don't know what their purpose is now.
- Lena's response should be human and imperfect. She doesn't comfort Sable. She doesn't have a speech ready. She says something like: "I spent three years after my paper was retracted thinking I'd wasted my career. Then I found the alloy again and realized the work had been right all along. The years weren't wasted. They just weren't what I thought they were." It's not a perfect analogy. Sable recognizes this. But it's enough.
- End with Sable saying they have something to tell the Assembly, and Lena saying she'll listen.

This scene does the work the editor asked for: it puts us in the room when Sable's worldview cracks. It also grounds Sable's Assembly speech in a specific emotional experience rather than having it arrive as a polished performance.

**Word count impact:** +500 words.

### Fix 2B: Trim Sable's Assembly speech
**Location:** Ch6, lines ~1039-1058.

**Currently exists:** The speech is 19 lines and somewhat redundant with the new private scene.

**Trim by ~150 words.** Sable no longer needs to explain the full arc of their realization in the speech -- the reader has already witnessed it. The speech can be shorter, more direct, more emotional. Keep: "I was wrong." Keep: the withdrawal of the reunification protocol. Cut: the explanatory middle paragraphs where Sable narrates their own change of heart (lines ~1047-1051), since we've now seen it happen.

**Word count impact:** -150 words.

**Total for Must Fix #2:** +350 words.

## MUST FIX #3: Fix the Leak Sequence

### Problem
Kai walks to a public terminal in the JSC visitor center and emails 47 addresses. This is not plausible for a facility under a national security classification program. The terminal would be on a monitored network. The email would be flagged. Kai would be stopped before he hit send.

### Fix 3: Replace the transmission method (Ch5, lines ~877-908)
**Location:** Ch5, the entire "They had nine hours" sequence.

**Currently exists:** Kai walks to a public terminal, downloads the report from his email drafts, attaches files, and sends to 47 addresses. Total implausibility: a JAXA pilot on a security-flagged campus using a public terminal to email classified material.

**Replace with a more formidable security environment and a cleverer circumvention (approx. same word count, ~350 words):**

The new method:
1. Establish that Hadley has locked down external communications from JSC. All outgoing traffic from the campus network (including the visitor center) is being filtered through MERIDIAN monitoring. Kai confirms this -- he tested it. The public terminals are useless.
2. Kai's solution uses his JAXA credentials in a way Hadley's American security apparatus can't easily block. JAXA maintains an independent satellite uplink for their ISS operations -- a dedicated ground station antenna on the JSC campus that routes through Tsukuba, not through American networks. Kai has legitimate access because he's the JAXA liaison. The uplink is technically for telemetry data, not email, but Yuki (still on the ISS at this point) can receive a data package through the station's JAXA module and relay it to the JAXA cloud from orbit -- outside American jurisdiction entirely.
3. The chain: Kai uploads Lena's report + attachments to the JAXA uplink disguised as a routine telemetry package. Yuki receives it on the ISS, strips it out, and pushes it to the JAXA cloud server. From there, an automated script (which Kai set up the previous night using his personal JAXA credentials) distributes it to the 47 addresses. The email originates from a Japanese server. By the time Hadley's monitoring flags the content, it's already in 47 inboxes across 14 countries.
4. The cost: Yuki knows this will end her career at JAXA. The moment is not glossed over. She says something like: "My mother is going to kill me." Kai says: "Get in line." This keeps the human stakes real.
5. Kai does not casually stroll to a visitor center. He goes to the JAXA liaison office (which is already established as outside Hadley's direct authority) and does this from behind a closed door, sweating, checking timestamps, knowing that if the upload is flagged before Yuki can relay it, all three of them are done.

This version respects the security environment, gives Kai and Yuki more agency, and maintains the tension.

**Word count impact:** Net zero (replacing ~350 words with ~350 words).

### Fix 10: Em-dash parenthetical addiction
**Location:** Throughout Ch3-6. The manuscript uses mid-sentence em-dash asides on nearly every page.

**Fix:** Do a pass through Ch3-6 and convert approximately half of the em-dash parentheticals to one of:
- A separate sentence (where the aside is substantive enough to stand alone)
- Deletion (where the aside is redundant)

Target: reduce em-dash parentheticals from ~40 instances in Ch3-6 to ~20. Prioritize cutting those that interrupt the subject-verb core of a sentence.

### Fix 11: "Physical detail + what it reveals" descriptive formula
**Location:** Character introductions throughout.

**Examples of the pattern:**
- Ch1, line 73: Reyes: "He had deep-set eyes, silver hair cropped close, and the calloused hands of someone who still spent time in trenches." (Physical + reveal)
- Ch2, line 205-207: Kai first appearance: physical description + "He was the calmest person in the room. Not performing calm..." (Physical + psychological read)
- Ch3, line 399: Sable: physical description + "their fingers wrapped around the metal in a way that made Lena think of ivy on a fence post." (Physical + simile/reveal)

**Fix:** This pattern works well for major character introductions (Kai, Sable) but becomes formulaic on repetition. For secondary characters and re-introductions, vary the approach:
- Sometimes lead with action or speech, let the reader infer the physical.
- Sometimes give the physical detail without the "what it reveals" clause.

Specific cuts:
- Ch2, line 205-208: The Kai introduction is in Ch2 (protected). Leave it.
- Ch3, line 399: Sable's introduction is the major one. Leave the ivy simile -- it's earned.
- Ch4, line 667: Achebe in the hallway: "leaning against the wall near the elevator bank with one hand pressed flat to his chest. His satchel hung from his opposite shoulder, pulling his frame into an asymmetry that made him look thinner than he was." -- Cut "pulling his frame into an asymmetry that made him look thinner than he was." The hand on chest is the important detail. We don't need the diagnostic reading of his silhouette.

**Word count impact:** -30 words.

## CHARACTER WORK

### Fix 12: Give Kai his own beliefs about the revelation
**Location:** Ch3 (the ISS scenes) and Ch5 (the decision to leak).

**Problem:** Kai functions as Lena's emotional counterweight and logistics enabler. He has no stated position on the Ketharan revelation itself.

**Fix 12A:** In Ch3, during the late-night data extraction scene (lines ~539-591), add 3-4 lines where Kai offers his own perspective. After Lena says "We're the colony," Kai should respond with something that reveals his worldview -- not agreement or disagreement, but his own angle. Suggestion: Kai's perspective as a pilot. He has spent his career in machines that were designed, tested, iterated. He understands engineered systems. His reaction to learning humanity was engineered is not existential dread but something more like professional recognition: "Makes sense, actually. Everything about us looks like a design that got field-tested and modified. We're not a prototype. We're a production model that's been running so long nobody remembers the factory." This gives him a distinct intellectual identity -- pragmatic, systems-oriented, unsentimental.

**Word count impact:** +50 words.

**Fix 12B:** In Ch5, before Kai hits send (lines ~898-908), add a beat where he pauses and the reader gets his internal reason for doing this. Not a speech. One or two sentences. He's not doing this for Lena (though that's part of it). He's doing it because he's a pilot and pilots don't fly with bad instruments. Hadley is a bad instrument. The world needs accurate data to navigate what comes next.

**Word count impact:** +40 words.

**Location:** Ch3, lines ~448-486 (the phone call) and Ch5, lines ~888-894 (the ISS relay).

**Problem:** Yuki speaks only in exposition. Her dialogue in Ch3 is compressed summary. In Ch5, she gets two lines.
