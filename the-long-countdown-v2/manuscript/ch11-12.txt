## Chapter 11: The Letters We Don't Send

The attic smells like mouse droppings and old paper and the particular kind of dust that only accumulates in spaces people avoid. I'm twenty-one years old and I'm helping my mother carry boxes down a pull-down ladder because she decided, on a Tuesday in November, that it was time to "get organized up there." Which means she watched a show about decluttering and felt guilty about the state of things overhead.

"Careful with that one," she says from below, hands raised like she's going to catch a box that weighs thirty pounds. "That's got your grandmother's sewing kit."

I bring it down one-handed, bracing the other against the ladder frame. The box is water-stained on one corner. Inside, wrapped in a flour-sack towel that's older than I am, there's a Singer sewing kit in a leather case, the kind with the fold-out trays and tiny compartments for bobbins.

"She made your christening gown with that," Diane says, opening the case with the reverence people reserve for relics. "Hand-stitched the lace. Took her three months." She touches a spool of white thread, faded to ivory. "You screamed through the whole ceremony. Father Morrissey said you had opinions."

"Still do."

She laughs. It's a real one. That's been happening more often—the real laughs, not the careful ones she used to deploy like shields. I've been sober two years. I've been present. The math is simple: show up consistently and people stop bracing for your absence.

We work through the morning. She tells me about each object like she's narrating a museum tour for an audience of one. Frank's old bowling trophy from the league he quit in '94. Sophie's first pair of shoes, bronze-coated, one of them missing the heel tip. A box of Christmas ornaments Diane's mother painted by hand—cardinals and snow scenes on wooden discs, the paint cracking now along the grain.

"Keep or donate?" I ask about a set of curtains still in their packaging.

"Oh, keep. Those are for the guest room when I redo it."

She's been going to redo the guest room for six years. I don't say that. I just stack the curtains with the keep pile and reach for the next box.

It's up near the eave, shoved behind a garment bag full of bridesmaid dresses from the eighties—teal satin, enormous shoulders. The box is small. Shoebox-sized. No label. I pull it toward me and the top shifts, revealing notebooks. Three of them, the kind with marbled covers you get at the drugstore. I recognize Diane's handwriting on the first page of the top one before I register what I'm reading.

*November 2008*

I should put it back. I know I should put it back.

"Mom, what do you want me to do with—" I lean toward the ladder opening but she's gone. I hear water running in the kitchen. She's making tea. She'll be back in five minutes with two mugs and an opinion about the bridesmaid dresses.

I open the notebook.

Her handwriting is the same rounded cursive she uses for grocery lists and birthday cards, but the lines slant more here, as if she was writing fast or writing angry or writing both. I flip through. It's not a diary in the traditional sense—there are no "Dear Diary" headers, no dates on every page. It's more like a collection of letters she never sent. To Frank. To her sister. To herself.

To me.

I find it near the middle of the second notebook. The date is March 2009. I was nineteen. I was drunk most of the time and lying about the rest.

*I'm losing him. I watch him choose the easy thing every time and I don't know how to stop it. Am I the reason? Did I make it too comfortable to fail? Frank says leave him alone, let him figure it out, but Frank didn't see him last Thursday. I found the bottles under his bed when I was putting laundry away and I just stood there. I just stood there holding his clean shirts and looking at the bottles and I thought: I did this. Somehow I did this. He was such a bright kid. Everyone said so. And now I'm losing him and I don't know which part was the mistake—the loving too much or the not-enough.*

The dust catches the light from the attic's single bulb. Motes hang suspended, moving so slowly they seem frozen. I'm standing with my head bent under the roofline and my chest feels like someone's got a hand inside it, squeezing.

There's more. The next page:

*I read an article about enabling. The signs. I have all of them. Every single one. I make his excuses. I soften his consequences. I tell Frank not to push too hard. What if pushing is what he needs? What if the soft place I keep being is the very thing that's killing him?*

I close the notebook. I place it back in the box. I align the box behind the garment bag, exactly where it was. I adjust the angle so the dust pattern isn't disturbed. These are the skills of a former addict—we know how to make things look untouched. We know how to erase our presence from a scene.

"Nate? Tea's ready!"

"Coming down," I call. My voice sounds normal. That's another skill.

I descend the ladder. Diane hands me a mug—Earl Grey, too much sugar, the way she always makes it. Her face is open and happy. She's enjoying this. The organizing. The togetherness. The son who shows up on a Tuesday to help with boxes.

"Find anything interesting up there?"

"Bunch of bridesmaid dresses. Teal."

She groans. "Oh God. Karen's wedding. 1987. I looked like a teal refrigerator."

"You should keep them. Halloween potential."

She swats my arm. We drink our tea. She tells me about Karen's wedding—the best man who got drunk and fell into the cake, the flower girl who refused to walk and had to be carried. Normal stories. The kind of stories mothers tell their present, sober, accounted-for sons.

I don't tell her I read the journal. I will never tell her I read the journal. She will never know that I know about the guilt she carries—that she thinks she made it too comfortable for me to fail, that she sees my old destruction as her architecture. She's wrong, of course. The drinking was mine. The lying was mine. The choosing easy was mine.

But I can't tell her she's wrong without telling her I read it.

So instead I carry boxes down the ladder and laugh at the teal dresses and drink too-sweet tea and try to be the answer to a letter she never sent.

---

Three weeks later, the ice storm hits.

It starts as freezing rain on a Thursday night in December and doesn't stop for thirty-one hours. By Friday afternoon, half of Black Hawk County is without power. By Saturday morning, it's three counties—Black Hawk, Bremer, Butler. The weight of the ice brings down power lines and tree limbs and, in one case on Highway 218, an entire billboard advertising a personal injury lawyer. The irony writes itself.

I'm in my apartment when it starts. By the time it's clear this isn't a normal winter storm, I'm already on the phone. This is what NEORAN was built for. It's barely operational—we have a filing with the state, a checking account with eight hundred dollars in it, and a website I built myself that looks like it was designed in 2003. But we have a phone tree. We have a list of community centers with backup generators. We have relationships with the county emergency management office because I've been showing up to their meetings for six months, the youngest person in the room by three decades, taking notes.

Elena picks up on the second ring.

"You watching this?" I ask.

"Already lost power at my building. I'm at the library."

"I need you at Covenant Church on College Street. They've got a generator and they just agreed to open as a warming center. Can you get there?"

"Give me twenty minutes."

She doesn't ask why. She doesn't ask what the plan is. She trusts that I have one. That trust is earned—we've been working together for five months now, every Thursday and some weekends, building out NEORAN's volunteer roster and resource maps. She's seen my binder. The binder is impressive in a way that makes normal people uncomfortable.

By Saturday evening I haven't slept. The roads are coated in an inch of ice and the temperature's dropping. We have three warming centers open—Covenant, the VFW on Seerley Boulevard, and the community room at Bartlett Hall that UNI let us use after I spent forty minutes on the phone with the facilities director. Elena is running Covenant. I'm floating between all three, coordinating supply drops and tracking capacity.

The phone trees work. That's the thing—they work. Mrs. Patterson from the emergency management meetings calls twelve seniors on her block. Nine of them need rides. We get them rides. Tom Birch, who I met at the volunteer event where I met Elena, has a truck with chains on the tires. He makes six trips. Everything connects. Every piece we put in place over the past months has a function, and the functions combine into something that actually helps.

I'm at Bartlett at two in the morning, Saturday bleeding into Sunday, when Elena calls.

"We've got forty-three people at Covenant and we're running low on blankets. Also, Mrs. Henderson needs her oxygen. Her backup tanks are at her house on Tremont."

"I'll send Tom."

"Tom's truck died. He's stuck on University Avenue."

I rub my eyes. "Okay. I'll figure it out. How are you?"

"I'm fine. How are you?"

"Fine."

"Nate. When did you last sleep?"

I have to think about it. "Thursday night? I think? What day is it?"

"It's Sunday now. Technically."

"Then Thursday night. But I'm—"

"That's not heroic, that's stupid. Sleep."

"I can't. Mrs. Henderson—"

"I'll handle the oxygen tanks. Jake Morgan has a Subaru with all-wheel drive, he's here. Sleep. I'm not asking."

She hangs up. I stare at the phone. In my experience, people who tell you to sleep don't usually arrange the logistics to make it possible first. Elena does both. She issues the order and removes the obstacle in the same breath.

I find a cot in the supply room at Bartlett. Someone—I don't know who—puts a blanket over me at some point because I wake up three hours later with one draped across my chest that wasn't there when I laid down. My phone has four missed calls. I answer them all in sequence. The ice is still falling.

The storm breaks Sunday afternoon. The power stays out for another two days in most areas, three in some. The warming centers stay open until Tuesday. By the time we close down operations, we've sheltered a hundred and seventeen people, coordinated eighteen supply runs, and facilitated transport for thirty-four elderly residents who would have otherwise been alone in freezing houses.

The county emergency management director, Bill Travers, shakes my hand on Tuesday afternoon outside Covenant Church. "You've got a future in this," he says. He's sixty-two, face like a topographical map of worry.

"That's the plan."

"I mean it. What you built here—the phone trees, the resource lists, the way you had volunteers pre-assigned to roles before the first call went out. That's not amateur hour. That's infrastructure."

I want to tell him that I built it because I know what's coming. Not this storm—this storm was luck, or maybe inevitability, or maybe just Iowa being Iowa in December. But there will be worse. There will be so much worse. And the difference between a community that survives and one that doesn't is exactly this: pre-assigned roles, resource lists, phone trees. The boring architecture of preparedness that nobody funds because it doesn't photograph well.

Instead I say, "Thank you, sir. Means a lot."

Elena is inside, sweeping the church basement. She's been here since Thursday night, same as me. Her hair is in a braid that's come half undone. There's a smear of something—peanut butter, maybe, from the sandwiches—on her jacket.

"Bill Travers just told me I have a future in this," I say.

"No shit." She keeps sweeping. "You also have a present in this. Which involves helping me stack these chairs."

I stack chairs. We work in the particular silence of people who've been through something together—not the silence of nothing-to-say but the silence of not-needing-to. When we finish, the basement is clean and empty and the fluorescent lights buzz in the quiet.

"Thank you," I say. "For all of it. The whole weekend."

She leans the broom against the wall. Looks at me straight on—Elena always looks at you straight on, never sideways, never from angles. It's disorienting if you're used to people who communicate obliquely.

"You don't have to thank me. I volunteered. That's what volunteering means."

"Still."

"Still nothing. But you're welcome." She pulls on her coat. "I'm going home to shower for approximately nine years. Same time Thursday?"

"Same time Thursday."

She leaves. I stand in the clean, empty basement of Covenant Church and I feel something I don't have a word for. Not pride exactly. Not satisfaction. Something closer to recognition—like looking at a blueprint and then looking at the building and seeing that they match. This is what I came back to do. Not just the disaster response, but the being-here. The showing up when the power goes out and the roads are sheets of ice and Mrs. Henderson needs her oxygen tanks. This is what I choose instead of the easy thing.

Anyway. The point is—

---

Marcus calls on a Wednesday in January. It's become routine—Wednesday nights, nine o'clock his time, eight mine. He's in his senior year at Iowa State and he sounds tired in the way that engineering students always sound tired: caffeinated, slightly manic, proud of their suffering.

"So the bridge project," he says. "We've been modeling load distributions for three weeks and Professor Kim keeps finding errors in our finite element analysis. She's relentless. It's like having a very polite assassin review your homework."

"What kind of errors?"

"Boundary conditions, mostly. We keep over-constraining the model and getting artificially stiff results. The real structure would flex more than what we're predicting, which means our safety factors are inflated."

I understand maybe forty percent of this. But I ask questions because Marcus lights up when he talks about it—I can hear it in his voice, the gap-toothed enthusiasm that makes him sound fourteen again, showing me his sketches of impossible buildings.

"There's also a girl," he says, and his tone shifts. Careful. Almost shy.

"Yeah?"

"Her name's Priya. She's in my thermodynamics section. She's—I don't know. She's really smart. And she has this laugh that's like—" He stops. "This is stupid."

"It's not stupid. Tell me about the laugh."

"It's just—she laughs with her whole body. Like the joy goes all the way through her. I've never seen anyone do that."

I'm sitting in my apartment, phone pressed to my ear, and I'm smiling at the wall. Marcus in love is Marcus at his most genuine—no performance, no cool, just wonder.

"Have you asked her out?"

"We've gotten coffee twice. But I don't know if she thinks they're dates or just study sessions. We did study. Both times. Thermodynamics."

"Marcus. You have to tell her."

"I know. I know. I'm working up to it." A pause. "How about you? Anything happening with the volunteer girl?"

"Elena. And no. We're just working together."

"Uh-huh."

"We are."

"Nate. You talk about her every time we're on the phone."

"Because we work together. On the nonprofit. Which is a major part of my life right now."

"Sure. And you describe what she was wearing."

I open my mouth. Close it. He's not wrong. Last week I told him she'd switched from a green jacket to a blue one and it was strange how much it changed her face. That's—okay. That's a data point.

"Anyway," Marcus says, and I can hear the grin. "The point is. Ask her out."

"The point is you should ask Priya out."

"Deal. Same time next week?"

"Same time next week."

I hang up. The apartment is quiet. Three relationships maintained through presence. Marcus on Wednesdays. Elena on Thursdays. Frank and Diane and Sophie on the irregular schedule of family. I used to think relationships required strategy. Turns out they mostly require showing up.

---

The rain starts Thursday night. By Saturday, the river is over flood stage. The sandbagging starts. And somewhere in the middle of all of it—the mud and the water and the exhaustion—something is going to break open.

But that's the next chapter.

For now: Elena, thirty hours into the ice storm response, falling asleep on my shoulder in the Covenant basement. Both of us finally sitting down. She slept for two hours. My arm went completely numb. I didn't move it. I didn't move at all.

She never mentioned it after. I never mentioned it after. Some things live in the space between what happened and what's acknowledged, and they're more real for the silence.

## Chapter 12: What Mud Reveals

The Cedar River crests eleven feet above flood stage on a Sunday in April, and the city of Cedar Falls learns what it's made of.

I've been awake since Friday. That's not precisely true—I slept two hours Saturday morning on a cot at the EOC, and Elena made me eat a sandwich at some point that I don't remember consuming but must have because the wrapper was in my pocket later. But functionally I've been running on adrenaline and bad coffee for sixty hours, and my body is starting to register its complaints.

The sandbagging is relentless. We have sixty volunteers across three sites—the levee on First Street where the commercial district meets the river, the residential stretch on Rownd Street where the older houses sit too low, and the park district where the storm drains are backing up. Elena's running Rownd Street. I'm coordinating between all three. Tom Birch is on First Street with a crew of twelve, filling bags from a dump truck full of sand that the county delivered Friday.

My radio crackles. Elena's voice, calm and precise: "North end of Rownd, we need ten more bodies. The water's coming over the top."

"Copy. Sending from the park crew—they've got capacity."

"Also, our sandbag supply is getting low."

"Truck's en route. Twenty minutes."

"Make it fifteen."

I make it fourteen. The truck driver, a county employee named Gerald who's been working since Thursday, gives me a look that says *I am too old for this* and also *I respect whatever insanity you're running here* and peels off toward Rownd Street.

Fourteen hours. That's how long Sunday lasts. Fourteen hours of sandbagging and pumping and evacuating and coordinating and watching the river rise and rise and rise until, around nine p.m., it stops. Holds. The crest comes and it's there and it's bad but it's not catastrophic because we built a wall between the water and the town and the wall held.

Not everywhere. Three houses on Rownd Street took water—the ones south of Elena's perimeter, the ones we couldn't reach in time. A business on First Street, a flooring company with a below-grade loading dock, that one was gone by Saturday night. The park is underwater entirely, playground equipment sticking up like the skeleton of a drowned circus.

But it could have been so much worse. Bill Travers says it, the mayor says it, the local news says it: *it could have been so much worse.*

I know. I know exactly how much worse it could have been.

---

The parking lot behind the Rownd Street staging area is mud. Two inches of it, the thick Iowa kind that sucks at your boots with every step. I'm standing in it. Elena is walking toward me from the staging tent—walking slow, the way people walk when they've been on their feet for fourteen hours and their body has run out of the chemical that makes movement feel effortless.

She's covered in mud. Her jacket, her jeans, her boots, her hands. There's a streak of it across her forehead where she's pushed her hair back. Her braid has come completely undone and her hair hangs loose and wet against her neck. She looks like she's been dragged through a swamp. She looks like the most competent person I've ever known.

She stops in front of me. We're under a parking lot light, the kind that turns everything orange-yellow. The rain has stopped but the air is still thick with moisture, the smell of the river—organic, cold, alive.

"We did it," I say.

"We did it," she says.

And then she steps forward, takes my face in both her muddy hands, and kisses me.

It's not gentle. It's not tentative. It's not a question. Her mouth is warm and tastes like the bad coffee we've all been drinking and her hands are cold on my jaw and she kisses me like she's been thinking about it for a long time and decided there's no reason to pretend otherwise.

When she pulls back—not far, just enough to talk—she says: "I've been waiting for you to do that for six months. You were taking too long."

I'm standing in two inches of mud in a parking lot behind a sandbag staging area and my hands are on her waist because at some point during the kiss they found their way there, and I say the only thing I can think of:

"Six months?"

"At least. Possibly longer. You're not very perceptive." She's still holding my face. Her thumbs are on my cheekbones. "I got tired of waiting."

"I was going to—"

"When? When were you going to?"

"I don't know. Eventually."

"Eventually is too long. I did it now." She lets go of my face. Steps back. Studies me with that straight-on look. "So?"

"So what?"

"So is this happening? Are we doing this?"

"Yes," I say. "God. Yes."

"Good." She nods once, decisive, the same way she nods when we confirm a supply delivery or approve a volunteer schedule. Then she smiles—rare with Elena, and devastating for the rarity—and says, "You look terrible."

"You have mud on your face."

"You have mud everywhere."

"Fair."

We stand in the parking lot. The river holds at crest. The pumps hum. Somewhere on First Street, Tom Birch is probably asleep against a sandbag. The town is battered but intact. And Elena kissed me in the mud after fourteen hours because she got tired of waiting for me to be brave.

---

Tomorrow is not different.

Elena calls me at noon on Monday. "Are we together? Yes or no."

"Yes."

"Good. Don't waste my time."

That's it. That's the entire negotiation. Elena doesn't do ambiguity. She doesn't do the slow approach, the undefined period, the what-are-we phase. She identifies what she wants, confirms it's mutual, and proceeds.

Dating Elena is not like anything I imagined dating would be. She's practical about it. She builds us into her schedule the way she builds everything into her schedule—with intention and without apology. Tuesday evenings and weekends are ours. Thursdays and Saturdays are still NEORAN. She maintains the distinction because she's seen couples who merge their work and personal lives and lose both.

"We need boundaries," she says, two weeks in. "When we're working, we're working. When we're not working, we're not working. Otherwise it all becomes one thing and one thing is fragile."

---

May. Dinner at the Callahans'.

Frank monopolizes her immediately. They're in the garage within fifteen minutes of arrival, looking at his workbench setup. I can hear them through the kitchen window—something about a leaking spigot and whether to replace the whole assembly or just the washer.

"PVC solvent cement on the joint," Elena is saying. "But you'd want to cut below the crack. No point patching over compromised material."

"That's what I said." Frank's voice has the warmth he usually reserves for particularly well-made tools.

I'm at the kitchen counter with Diane.

"She's lovely," Diane says.

"She's my colleague."

"Mmhmm." Diane puts a dish away. "She's lovely and you're watching her through the window like she's on television."

Sophie materializes. "She's way too good for you, by the way."

"I know," I say.

Diane touches my arm. Brief, light. "No she's not."

Dinner is chicken and rice. We sit at the kitchen table—the same table where I ate cereal a thousand times, where Frank told me he was proud, where Diane set down plates for years while I was disappearing. Elena compliments the chicken. Frank asks about the flood response. Sophie corners Elena in the living room afterward—I can't hear what they're talking about but Sophie is leaning forward, which means she's interested.

This is it. This is the life I came back for.

---

June. Elena and I are on the couch in my apartment. Her feet are in my lap. She's reading a large animal anatomy textbook.

"Hey," she says, not looking up. "Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Why disaster prep? Most guys our age are trying to get into finance."

"I think it matters."

"That's not an answer."

She's right. It's not.

"I just—I grew up here. I've seen what happens when storms hit and nobody's ready."

Elena studies me. Weighing my words on her precise scale.

"Okay," she says. "That's part of it. But it's not all of it." She picks up her textbook. "You'll tell me the rest when you're ready."

She returns to her diagrams. I sit with my hand on her ankle and my heart in my throat and the knowledge that Elena Vasquez is smarter than every deflection I've ever built.

---

NEORAN grows. First paid staff member—Jamie Ostrowski, twenty-four, just out of the Army. We apply for a bigger grant. Federal this time—FEMA's Community Preparedness program. $50,000. The application is sixty pages.

The grant comes through in September. Thirty-seven thousand. Enough to hire Jamie full-time and lease dedicated office space.

Elena and I celebrate at the diner on Main Street. Breakfast at nine p.m.—our thing. She eats pancakes. I eat eggs. We're twenty-two and twenty-three and we're building something that matters and we're together and for the first time in either of my lives I feel like the present tense is enough.

---

November. Late. A Tuesday. We're in bed. The room is dark except for streetlight through the blinds.

Elena is on her side, facing away, breathing starting to even out.

"You're going to tell me eventually," she says. Sleep-thick, barely a murmur. "Whatever it is you're not saying."

I don't respond. I stare at the ceiling.

"I'm not in a hurry," she says, quieter still. "But I know there's something. And you're going to tell me. Because that's what this is. This is the kind where you tell each other things."

Her breathing deepens. She's asleep.

She's right. She's already right. She's been right since the parking lot, since she took my face in her hands and closed the distance I was too scared to close.

I'm going to have to tell her. Not everything—maybe not ever everything—but something. Some version of the truth.

But not tonight.

Tonight I lie beside her and listen to her breathe and feel the particular terror of being known. She's pointed at my truth like a compass needle and she's not wavering.

The countdown continues. The storms will come—bigger, worse. And between now and then there's this: a woman asleep beside me who refuses to accept half-truths.

I close my eyes. Elena shifts in her sleep, her hand finding my arm. The radiator ticks.

Anyway. The point is—

She's already asleep. She's already right.
