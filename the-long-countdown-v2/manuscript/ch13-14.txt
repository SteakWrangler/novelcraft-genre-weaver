## Chapter 13: Bridges and Thresholds

Marcus shows up on a Friday in July with a six-pack of root beer and a sunburn that makes him look like he fell asleep inside a rotisserie chicken. He's driving a used Civic now, bought with his first real paychecks from Kiewit Engineering, and he parallel parks it on the street outside my apartment with the careful precision of someone who's spent four years solving structural load problems and can't turn it off.

I watch from the window. He gets out, stretches, checks his phone, then looks up at my building like he's assessing its foundation. Old habits.

"You look like a lobster," I tell him at the door.

"Field survey in Ames. Four hours in a hardhat with no sunscreen." He holds up the root beer. "Housewarming gift. Even though you've lived here for a year."

"It's the thought that counts."

"The thought was: Nate probably doesn't have anything good to drink."

"I have water."

"Exactly."

The apartment is small — one bedroom, a kitchen that's really just a counter with ambitions, a living room where I've set up a desk and a whiteboard covered in NEORAN project timelines. Marcus scans the whiteboard the way he always scans things: methodically, left to right, top to bottom. Taking inventory.

"You're busier than last time I was here," he says.

"We got the FEMA partnership." I crack open a root beer and hand it to him. "Three county emergency management offices using our volunteer coordination platform now."

"That's real."

"It's real."

He settles onto my secondhand couch, which protests with a sound like a cat being stepped on. Marcus doesn't weigh much — the couch is just dramatic. He takes a long pull of root beer and looks at me with those steady eyes. Six years I've known this version of Marcus. Six years of weekly phone calls, of him showing up, of the kind of friendship that doesn't require spectacle. He just keeps appearing. Keeps being present. It's the most radical thing anyone's ever done for me, and he doesn't even know it.

"So," he says. "Elena."

"What about her?"

"You've been together almost a year and I still haven't met her properly. The volunteer event doesn't count — there were forty people and a collapsed staging area."

"She's at the clinic until six. You'll meet her at dinner."

"Good." He takes another drink. "Your mom says she's wonderful."

"My mom thinks anyone who's not actively on fire is wonderful."

"Your mom told me Elena talked to your dad about French drains for forty-five minutes."

"She did."

"Nate." Marcus leans forward, gap-toothed grin widening. "She talked to Frank Callahan about drainage. Voluntarily. That woman is a keeper."

I can't argue with that. Elena sat on the Callahan back deck — the deck I built with Frank — and discussed the relative merits of interior versus exterior waterproofing with the intensity of someone defending a thesis. Frank had looked at me afterward with an expression I'd never seen from him before. Something close to relief. Like he'd been waiting to see if I could find someone who'd stay, and here was evidence.

"She is," I say. Just that. No deflection, no joke. Some things don't need armor.

---

Elena joins us for dinner at a Thai place downtown. She's still in scrubs from the vet clinic, her dark hair pulled back, a smudge of something I don't want identified on her collar. She shakes Marcus's hand with the grip of someone who regularly restrains seventy-pound dogs and says, "You're the one who calls every Tuesday."

"Wednesday," Marcus corrects.

"He talks about you every Wednesday night," she tells him. "I time my showers around it."

Marcus laughs — a real one, surprised out of him. Elena does that. She takes people off-guard by being exactly who she is, immediately, without warm-up or pretense.

Over pad thai and spring rolls, I watch them find each other. Marcus talks infrastructure; Elena talks about the time she splinted a horse's leg in a thunderstorm. They discover a shared love of disaster — the managed kind, the kind where you show up and fix things.

"He's not as put-together as he looks," Elena tells Marcus, gesturing at me with a spring roll. "He forgets to eat. He stays up until three working on grant proposals. He runs at five AM regardless."

"He's always been like that," Marcus says. "Minus the running."

"The running is non-negotiable," I say.

"The eating should also be non-negotiable," Elena says.

Marcus is grinning at both of us. The gap in his teeth catching the restaurant light. He looks happy — genuinely happy to see me here, in this life, with this person who argues with me about nutrition.

---

Later. Back at the apartment. Elena's gone to bed — she has a six AM shift — and Marcus and I are on the tiny balcony. It's not the deck I built with Frank, but it overlooks a parking lot and a strip of trees, and if you squint, you can pretend the streetlights are stars.

"She's good for you," Marcus says. Not a question.

"Yeah."

"You're good for her too. In case you were about to deflect."

I was. He knows me well enough to preempt it now.

We sit in the kind of silence that only works between people who've logged enough hours together. The air is heavy with Iowa July — humid, alive, smelling of cut grass and asphalt that hasn't cooled yet.

"You're better than you were," Marcus says eventually. His voice is quiet. Not careful exactly — Marcus is never careful with me, which is why I trust him — but measured. "Way better. But there's a room in you I'm not allowed into."

I don't say anything.

"I'm not asking," he continues. "I'm just saying I see it. There's something you're carrying. You don't have to tell me. But I see it."

The thing about Marcus is that he's never once pushed. Not in six years. He's noted things — my predictions, my certainty about events before they happen, the way I sometimes look at the sky like I'm expecting something. He said it once, years ago: "There's something you're not saying." And I said: "When I can tell you, I will." And he let it sit. He's letting it sit now.

"When I can tell you," I say, "I will."

"I know." He takes a sip of root beer. "That's not what this is about. I'm just saying — whatever it is, it hasn't broken you. Whatever you're carrying, you're carrying it well. That's worth something."

I look at the parking lot. The trees. The streetlights that aren't stars.

"Thank you," I say.

"Don't thank me. Just keep doing whatever you're doing." He pauses. "And eat lunch."

---

The Cedar Falls Planning Commission meets on the third Thursday of every month in a conference room that smells like burned coffee and old carpet. I'm here because NEORAN has community interest in the proposed development along Dry Run Creek. Flood plain management, drainage infrastructure, emergency access routes.

I don't know Derek is involved until he walks in.

He's wearing a suit. Not a college-kid-playing-dress-up suit — a real one, fitted, charcoal, no tie because he's not trying that hard. He looks like money. He looks like confidence that's been professionally laundered.

Now he's at the front of the room, shaking hands with the commission chair, and I realize with a slow sinking feeling that the Dry Run Creek development — the one NEORAN submitted concerns about — is his.

Holt Development Group. I'd read the name on the proposal documents. Hadn't connected it. Derek Holt, right there in the name, and I missed it because I wasn't looking for him.

He sees me. His expression doesn't change — it just gains an additional layer. Recognition. Amusement. Something else.

The meeting proceeds. Derek presents his development plan with the polish of someone who's done this many times. Seventy-two single-family homes. Mixed-use commercial space. He uses phrases like "community-integrated design" and "sustainable growth corridor."

When public comment opens, I stand up.

"Nate Callahan, Northeast Regional Awareness Network. We submitted written comments regarding flood plain encroachment and emergency access limitations for the western parcels."

I lay out the issues: hundred-year flood plain maps, inadequate drainage proposals, single-access road. I cite FEMA data. I reference the 2008 flood. I'm not attacking his project — I'm raising legitimate concerns.

Derek watches me from his seat at the front. His face is perfectly neutral. Attentive.

---

Afterward, in the parking lot, Derek catches up to me.

"Callahan."

"Derek."

"NEORAN." He says it like he's tasting it. "That's yours?"

"Founded it three years ago."

"I know. I looked you up after I saw the comment letter." He tilts his head. "Emergency management nonprofit. Federal partnerships. You've got — what, eight employees?"

"Ten, as of last month."

"Ten." A small smile. "You went from burnout to nonprofit director. That's a hell of a rebrand."

"It's not a rebrand."

"Everything's a rebrand. Every version of ourselves is a rebrand." He says it without malice. Like it's obvious. Like the idea of genuine transformation is a charming fantasy he's too sophisticated to believe in.

"I disagree," I say. "But we don't need to have that argument in a parking lot."

"No, we don't." He looks at me for a long moment. "You want to grab a drink? Talk about this like adults?"

"Sure," I say. "There's a place on Main. I'm buying."

---

The bar is dim and half-empty. Derek orders bourbon, neat. I order club soda with lime. He notices but doesn't comment.

We talk for an hour. About his business — three developments completed, two more in planning. About NEORAN — the growth, the challenges. He asks good questions. Derek's intelligence was never the problem. His ethics were the problem.

"Here's what I'll do," Derek says finally. "I'll hire a real drainage firm. Do it right. Submit a revised plan. It'll cost me four months and probably eighty grand, but it'll be a better project."

"Why?"

"Because you're right and I'd rather spend eighty grand than fight you at meetings for half a year." He slides a card across the table. "Also because I want you to owe me one."

"That's not how it works."

"Everything's how it works if you frame it right."

We stand. At the door, he stops. Turns back to me.

"You know what I like about you, Callahan?" His voice is casual. Almost friendly. "You're the only person I've met who's as strategic as me but pretends not to be."

He walks to his car — something German and black and expensive — and drives away without waiting for a response.

I stand in the doorway with his card in my hand. The night air is warm. Someone's laughing on the street a block away.

The worst part — the part that's going to keep me up tonight — is that Derek isn't wrong.

I am strategic. I've always been strategic. The foreknowledge just gave me something worthy to aim it at. Without it, who would I be? Without the countdown, would I be the one in the tailored suit, cutting corners because profit is the only game that makes sense in a world without meaning?

I don't know. And not knowing is the room Marcus isn't allowed into. Not the foreknowledge itself — but the question of whether I'm good or just well-aimed.

Anyway. The point is—

I drive home. Elena is asleep. I stand in the kitchen for a long time, looking at the whiteboard, and I think about bridges.

Eight years. Maybe nine. The countdown doesn't care about my moral philosophy.

## Chapter 14: The Sealed Envelope

Chen's office smells like coffee and cat. Boltzmann is asleep on a stack of papers that are probably someone's dissertation. The afternoon sun cuts through the blinds in horizontal lines across Chen's desk, across her face as she looks at me with the expression I've come to recognize: she is about to demand something.

"I need more," she says.

"More what?"

"More data. More specifics." She takes off her glasses and rubs the bridge of her nose. "Nate, we've been working together since 2011. I need something I can verify independently. Something with a timeline."

It's July 2015. I'm twenty-four.

"What would satisfy you?" I ask.

"Something astronomical. Something I can observe. Something with a specific time, specific coordinates, that hasn't been predicted by anyone else."

I've been thinking about this. The Pathfinder objects will enter the outer solar system around 2018. Three years from now. Detectable with the right instruments.

"Objects," I say. "Plural. Entering the outer solar system. Around 2018."

Chen goes very still. Boltzmann yawns in his sleep.

"What kind of objects?"

"Artificial. Not natural. Moving on trajectories that aren't consistent with any known orbital mechanics."

"You're talking about..." She doesn't finish.

"I'm talking about contact. Not landing on the White House lawn. Something slower. Something that starts at the edge of the solar system and takes years to resolve. But it starts in 2018."

She stands up. Sits back down. Walks to the window.

"If this is real," she says to the window. "If what you're telling me is real—"

"I know."

"No, you don't." She turns back. "I'm the one who has to decide whether to believe you. I need it documented. Written. Sealed. Time-stamped."

"That's why I'm here."

---

We do it that afternoon. Chen provides university letterhead. I write by hand:

*Objects of artificial origin will enter the detectable range of the outer solar system (beyond Neptune's orbit) between January and June 2018. Approximate approach vector: ecliptic plane, bearing consistent with origin from galactic center direction. Multiple objects. Non-ballistic trajectory corrections will be observable.*

*Written July 14, 2015. Nate Callahan.*

Chen reads it three times. Her lips move.

"Approach vector from the galactic center direction," she repeats. "That's specific."

"It's correct."

She takes the paper. Folds it carefully. Puts it in an envelope. Writes the date. Signs across the seal. Walks to the combination safe behind her desk. The lock clicks. The envelope disappears. The safe closes.

"Three years," Chen says.

"Three years."

"I'm going to need more coffee."

---

The months after become a blur of growth. NEORAN expands — ten employees by early 2016. Elena leaves her vet clinic to come on as operations coordinator. Marcus chairs the technical advisory board. Regional offices in Waterloo, then Dubuque. Federal money flows.

Elena and I move in together fall 2016. A two-bedroom apartment. The second bedroom becomes an office Elena immediately colonizes with veterinary journals and color-coded binders.

She organizes by season. I cook badly. We fight about dishes and reconcile over takeout. We get a dog — a rescue mutt she names Hazard. One ear up, one ear down. He eats three leashes in the first month.

Sophie comes for dinner in April 2017. She's twenty-one. Less prickly. More curious.

"You really did change," she says. "I still don't understand how."

"I had good reasons."

"You still won't tell me what they are."

---

Thanksgiving 2017. The Callahan house.

Frank carves the turkey. Diane orchestrates. Marcus and Elena debate disaster infrastructure — levees versus community preparedness. Sophie argues with everyone. Hazard is under the table performing structural analysis on shoelaces.

I look at this table. Frank and Diane. Sophie. Marcus. Elena. This improbable collection of people who love me. This life I built piece by piece from the rubble of what I was.

I have eight years left.

Frank catches my eye across the table. Nods. The nod that means: this is good, bud. This is right.

I pick up my fork. I eat Diane's turkey. I laugh at Sophie's jokes. I am here, fully here, and I am also counting. I am always counting.

---

New Year's Eve, 2017. Our apartment roof.

It's cold. December-in-Iowa cold. Elena is bundled in a coat that makes her look like a small puffy astronaut. Between us, a thermos of hot chocolate. Below, Cedar Falls prepares for midnight.

But I'm looking at the sky.

I can't help it. Beyond the stratosphere, beyond the Kuiper belt — the objects that Chen and I sealed in an envelope two years ago. Drifting inward. Getting closer. Still invisible, but there. Moving.

Three months. Maybe four. Before the first confirmed observations.

"Hey," Elena says.

I look at her. She's watching me with that expression — the one that means she's seeing something I'm not showing.

"What do you want this year?" she asks.

"More time," I say.

Elena smiles. She thinks I mean us — more time together, more mornings and arguments and the dog pressed between us on the couch. She thinks it's romantic.

And I do mean that. I mean every second of this. I want decades of it.

But I also mean everything else.

More time before the objects arrive. More time before the world learns. More time to build NEORAN into something strong enough. More time to prepare the people I love for something I can't yet explain.

"More time," I say again.

Elena reaches across and takes my hand. Below us, someone sets off a premature firework — a single red burst that fades into smoke.

"We've got plenty," she says.

I don't contradict her. The fireworks begin. Colors bloom overhead, reflected in her eyes. The year turns. 2018 begins.

Seven years left. Maybe.

More time.

She thinks I mean us.

I do.

I also mean everything else.
