## Chapter 22: The Parking Lot

I start wrong. I start with the science.

"There are objects approaching Earth," I say. "Seven of them. They're decelerating — which means they have propulsion. Which means they're not natural. Dr. Chen has published the data. Other researchers have confirmed it. The timeline is approximately eighteen months."

Frank listens. Doesn't move. Arms still crossed, leaning against his truck.

"NEORAN — the nonprofit, the shelters, everything Sophie and I have been building — it's preparation for this. For what happens when they arrive."

He nods. Once. Processing. His face is unreadable.

And I realize — this isn't the thing I need to tell him. This is the easy part. Aliens. Invasion. Data. Chen's papers. These are facts. Facts are simple.

The hard part is the other thing. The thing underneath.

"That's not all," I say.

"Okay."

"Dad, I need you to — I know this is going to sound—"

"Just say it, bud."

The parking lot is quiet. A car pulls out somewhere behind us. The evening light slants long and gold across the asphalt. I can hear birds — starlings, maybe, in the trees along the hospital perimeter.

"I lived another life before this one."

The words land in the air between us. Solid. Irretrievable. I watch Frank's face for the flinch, the doubt, the calculation that his son has lost his mind. It doesn't come. He just watches me. Waits.

"I don't know how. I don't have a mechanism. Chen doesn't either. But I was — I lived to thirty-five. In that life, I was a different person. I drank. I drifted. I burned everything. Everyone." I stop. Breathe. "And then I was seventeen again. Back in my room. Back at the beginning. With everything I knew."

Frank uncrosses his arms. Puts his hands in his jacket pockets. His eyes haven't left mine.

"That was eighteen years ago," I say. "I was seventeen and I remembered being thirty-five and I decided — I decided to do it differently."

"Differently how?" Frank's voice is quiet. Not skeptical. Just asking.

"Everything. School. Work. You. Mom. Sophie." I swallow. "In the first life, I failed at all of it. I dropped out. I drank. I moved away. I didn't come home for years."

The muscle in Frank's jaw moves. Just slightly.

"You and I — we barely spoke. After I left. There were years where I didn't call. Years where you called and I didn't pick up. And then—" My voice cracks. I let it. "And then you died. At sixty-three."

Frank is very still.

"Your heart. Like now. Like what just happened. But in that version, no one was home. Mom was at Aunt Carol's. You were alone in the recliner. And by the time they found you—"

"Nate."

"I was in Waterloo. I was drunk. I flew home for the funeral in a suit that didn't fit and Sophie wouldn't look at me and I held Mom's hand and I thought—" I stop. Because the tears are here now. Not dramatic. Just steady. Rolling down in the late light. "I thought: I built this. Not the heart attack. But the distance. The years of silence. I built all of that."

Frank doesn't speak. He takes his hands out of his pockets. He's looking at the ground now. At the asphalt. At the painted lines of the parking space.

"So I came back. I don't know how. I don't know why. But I came back and I was seventeen and you were forty-seven and you were alive and I thought — okay. Okay. This time I stay."

The silence stretches. A minute. Two. The parking lot lights haven't come on yet — it's that in-between time when the sun is below the treeline but the sensors haven't triggered. Everything is blue-gold. Suspended.

"The drinking," Frank says finally. "In high school. You stopped."

"Yes."

"Overnight. I remember. One week you were — and the next week you weren't."

"That's when it happened. The displacement."

Frank nods slowly. Not agreeing. Processing. Filing.

"And the nonprofit. NEORAN."

"Built for this. For what's coming."

"And Chen."

"She has the data. She believes the data. The time travel part — she sets aside."

"Smart woman."

"Yeah."

Another silence. Frank shifts his weight against the truck. The metal creaks slightly.

"In the first version," he says. And stops. Works his jaw. Tries again. "In the first version. We didn't—"

"We barely spoke. After I was twenty. Fifteen years of almost nothing."

"And I died—"

"At sixty-three. Alone."

"Was I—" He stops again. I see his throat work. "Was I a bad father? In that version?"

The question hits me like a fist.

"No." The word comes out broken. "No, Dad. You were the same. You were exactly the same. You tried. You called. You showed up. I was the one who—" I press the heel of my hand against my eyes. "You were fine. I was the problem. Every time."

Frank is quiet for a long time after that. The parking lot lights come on — sudden, harsh, turning everything flat and white. Neither of us moves.

"So you came back," he says. His voice is rough. Lower than usual. "And you — what. Fixed things?"

"I tried. I stayed in school. Got sober. Went to college. Came home. Started NEORAN. Stayed close." I look at him. "Stayed."

"Eighteen years."

"Yeah."

"You've known for eighteen years. About — all of it. The objects. The first life. And you just—"

"Stayed. Yeah."

Frank turns. Looks out across the parking lot. The lights make his face stark — shadows under his eyes, the lines deep, the jaw set the way it sets when he's feeling something he won't name.

"Why didn't you tell me before?"

I consider the question. The honest answer.

"Because I was afraid you'd think I was crazy. Because the world hasn't ended yet and I have no proof that isn't data and equations. Because—" I stop. "Because what if you looked at me differently. What if you looked at me and saw — a stranger. Someone from somewhere else. Instead of your son."

Frank turns back. Looks at me. The look lasts a long time. Long enough for another car to pull out of the lot. Long enough for the starlings to resettle in the trees. Long enough for the weight of two lifetimes to fill the space between us.

"Nate," he says. And his voice has something in it I've never heard before. Something that sounds like it's being carried up from very deep. "You spent eighteen years—"

He stops. Starts again.

"You could have gone anywhere. Done anything. You had — what, the future? The stock market? All of it?"

"Yes."

"And you came here. And you stayed."

"Yes."

"You fixed your life. You built something. You come to dinner every Sunday." He's looking at me and his eyes are bright and I realize — Frank Callahan is crying. Not openly. Not dramatically. Just a brightness at the edges. A slight catch in his breath. This man who built a deck with a broken finger. Who drove three hours in a snowstorm for Sophie's dance recital. Who says "bud" instead of "I love you" because the words are too big for his mouth.

"You stayed," he says again.

"Yeah, Dad."

"Well."

One word. It takes all the air out of the parking lot.

*Well.*

Like: I hear you. Like: I believe you. Like: Eighteen years of watching you become this person, and now I know why, and it doesn't change anything because you're still my son. Like: I am proud of you in a way I don't have language for. Like: You came back from somewhere impossible and you chose this family and this town and this life and you stayed and that is enough. That is everything.

*Well.*

The parking lot lights buzz. The asphalt is blue-white under them. The evening has gone full dark and neither of us has moved.

"How'd I die?" Frank asks. Quiet. Not afraid. Just — curious. The way you'd ask about a road you didn't take.

"In your recliner. The brown one."

"The La-Z-Boy."

"Yeah."

"Diane hated that chair."

"She still hates it."

"But she hasn't thrown it out."

"No."

Frank makes a sound. Not quite a laugh. "Good chair."

I laugh. Wet and broken and real. "Yeah. Good chair."

Silence again. Comfortable now. The worst has been said. The truth is out — all of it, the time travel and the first life and the drinking and the funeral in the bad suit and the fifteen years of not calling. It's all in the air between us and Frank is still here. Still leaning against his truck. Still looking at me like I'm his kid.

"Your mother," he says.

"Yeah."

"She should know?"

"I — yeah. If you think—"

"Not tonight." He straightens up. Rolls his shoulders. "Tonight she'll have dinner ready and she'll want to hear about the appointment. Good news. Clean labs. Let's give her that tonight."

"Okay."

"But soon."

"Yeah. Soon."

He pulls his keys out. Looks at them in his hand. Looks at me.

"You want me to drive?"

He always drives. His truck, his rules. But he's offering. Something has shifted.

"I'll drive," I say.

He nods. Walks around to the passenger side. Gets in. I get in the driver's seat. Adjust the mirrors. The truck smells like him — coffee and sawdust and Old Spice. It's always smelled like this. In both lifetimes.

I start the engine. Put it in reverse. And then Frank's hand lands on my forearm. Brief. Firm. The same hand that taught me to throw a football, that clapped my shoulder at graduation, that patted my hand in the hospital room three months ago. One squeeze.

"Bud."

"Yeah."

"You did good."

Three words. Thirty-three years in both directions. A whole life of trying — two whole lives of trying — and those three words land like they've been waiting all this time.

I pull out of the parking space. We drive home. The radio plays Tom Petty — "Wildflowers," which isn't classic rock, technically, but Frank likes it anyway. He hums along. Off-key. Comfortable.

We don't talk the rest of the drive. We don't need to.

---

At the house, Diane has dinner ready. Pot roast. The real kind — Frank's cheat day, because good lab results deserve celebration. She takes one look at Frank's face, then mine, and her eyes narrow.

"What happened?"

"Good news," Frank says. "Everything clean." He kisses her cheek. Hangs his jacket. "Where's the pot roast?"

She watches him go. Then turns to me. That mother-look. The one that sees through walls.

"Nate?"

"It's fine, Mom. We just — had a talk."

"What kind of talk?"

"A good one."

She studies me a moment longer. Then nods. Lets it go. Because Diane Callahan also knows when not to push. It's a family trait.

Dinner is normal. Frank eats two helpings. Diane reminds him about portion control. He ignores her lovingly. I sit at the table and eat pot roast and I think about how in the first timeline, I never came to this table after I was twenty. Fifteen years of pot roasts I missed. Sunday dinners I didn't attend. This table, these chairs, this light — I missed all of it. And now I haven't missed it. Not a single one.

After dinner, I help Frank with the dishes. Side by side at the sink. Him washing, me drying. The rhythm is old and comfortable.

"Thursday," he says, low enough that Diane can't hear from the living room.

"What about Thursday?"

"Come by Thursday. We'll tell your mother."

"You sure?"

He hands me a plate. "She's tougher than both of us combined."

"That's true."

"Thursday."

"Okay."

He washes the last dish. Drains the sink. Dries his hands on the towel that's always hung on the oven handle.

"For what it's worth," he says. "I'm glad you came back."

I put the plate away. Close the cabinet.

"Me too, Dad."

He nods. Once. Done. The conversation is over in the way Frank's conversations end — not with a period but with a settling, like a house that's finished shifting on its foundation.

He goes to the living room. Sits in the La-Z-Boy — the brown one, the one he died in once, in another version of this story. Turns on the television. Some documentary about the Great Lakes.

I lean in the kitchen doorway and watch him. Alive. Present. Watching television in his recliner.

"Bud," he says without looking up. "You're staring."

"Sorry."

"Go home. Pet the dog. Tell Elena I said hi."

"Okay."

"Drive the speed limit."

"I will."

He glances at me. A smile — small, private, ours. "Good kid."

I drive home at the speed limit. Every single mile.

---

Thursday. Frank and I tell Diane. Together. In the kitchen, over coffee, at 10 AM.

She listens. Doesn't interrupt. Her hands are wrapped around her mug and they don't shake and her eyes don't leave my face.

When I finish, she's quiet for exactly fourteen seconds. I count.

Then she says: "Well. That explains the socks."

"The — what?"

"The socks. When you were seventeen. One day you didn't care what you wore. The next day you were sorting your laundry and folding your socks like a thirty-year-old." She takes a sip of coffee. "I told your father. I said, 'Something happened to that boy.' And Frank said, 'He's figuring it out.' And I said, 'Overnight? No one figures it out overnight, Frank.'"

She sets her mug down.

"I've been waiting eighteen years for this conversation."

Frank, beside me, makes a sound. "You never said—"

"I didn't know what it was. I just knew something changed." She looks at me. Through me. Into whatever's behind me. "My son went to bed one person and woke up another. A better person. A kinder person. But different." She reaches across the table. Takes my hand. "I've been watching you be different for eighteen years, sweetheart. I'm just glad to finally know why."

I don't cry. I already cried in the parking lot. But I hold her hand and I think: of course. Of course she knew. Mothers always know.

"The invasion part," she says. "That I'll need some time with."

"Take all the time you need."

"I don't have all the time, apparently." She picks her mug back up. Sips. "How long?"

"About a year. Maybe fourteen months."

"And NEORAN is ready?"

"Getting there."

"What do you need?"

I blink. "What?"

"What do you need. From us. To be ready."

Frank nods beside me. Solid. Ready. This is what Callahans do. We get the bad news. We process it. We ask: what do we need to do.

"I need you to be at the Iowa City shelter when it happens. You and Dad. That's all."

"Done."

"And — Sunday dinners. Until then. Every one."

She smiles. Small. Fierce. "Obviously."

---

I drive home. Call Elena.

"They know."

"Both of them?"

"Both. Dad on Tuesday. Mom today."

"And?"

"Dad said 'well.' Mom said she knew something changed when I was seventeen."

Elena is quiet a moment. Then: "Of course she did."

"That's what I said."

"Because she's a mom, Nate."

"Yeah."

"Are you okay?"

I think about it. Really think. Am I okay? The two people who made me know the truth. The full truth — not just the mission, not just the data, but the other life. The first life. The one where I was nothing. They know that person existed. They know I was him. And they're still here.

"Yeah," I say. "I'm okay."

"Come home. Hazard misses you."

"Hazard saw me three hours ago."

"He has a short memory and a big heart. Come home."

I come home.
