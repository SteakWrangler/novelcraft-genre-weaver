## Chapter 25: The Point Is

Three days.

Three days since the sky changed. Three days since the grass died and the trees dropped their leaves and seven harvesting platforms settled over the horizon like mountains that arrived without geologic process. Three days since I turned thirty-five and the world I spent eighteen years preparing for became the world I live in.

It's June 17, 2026. A Tuesday. I know this because Sophie's communications log — handwritten, in a spiral notebook, because she doesn't trust anything with a battery to last — says Tuesday at the top of the page. Otherwise I might not know. The days have a sameness now. The gray light through the high windows doesn't change with the hours. There's no morning gold, no afternoon slant, no evening warmth. Just gray. Constant. The light of a world with something between it and its star.

I wake at 5:14 AM on a cot in the coach's office. I don't remember lying down. The last thing I recall is checking the water pressure at the south station at — when? Midnight? Later? The hours blurred. Elena must have steered me to the cot. She does that now — appears at my elbow when my sentences start to fragment, says *Nate, twenty minutes, I mean it* in the voice that doesn't allow argument. I've been sleeping in twenty-minute blocks. Sometimes forty, if my body overrides my mind. This was a long one — five hours, if the clock on the wall is right. She must have let me go.

I sit up. My back aches in the way of a man who slept on a vinyl-covered cot made for middle schoolers. My mouth tastes stale. My shirt — the same one from three days ago, a gray henley that Elena packed in the go-bag — is wrinkled and smells like institutional coffee and nervous sweat. I haven't showered. Nobody has. The water system is for drinking and cooking. Showers are a luxury that belongs to the world before.

I put my boots on. Stand up. The coach's office is small — a desk, a filing cabinet, the cot, a whiteboard on the wall with a schedule that Marcus wrote in blue marker. *Water check: 0600, 1200, 1800, 0000. Generator fuel: check hourly. Air filtration: visual inspection every four hours.* The routine of survival, reduced to bullet points.

I step out onto the main floor.

---

The gymnasium at 5:14 AM. Two hundred and sixty-three people, as of last night's count. The number has stabilized — not because people stopped coming, but because we've reached the capacity we planned for and Marcus's structural assessments say we hold here. The overflow goes to the mill site on the east side, which has its own systems, its own volunteers, its own version of this. Seventy-one people there as of yesterday.

The floor is a landscape of sleeping bodies. Cots in rows along the east wall. Families clustered together, children tangled in wool blankets. Singles and couples on the folding cots we bought in bulk from a restaurant supply company two years ago, when this was hypothetical. When this was a contingency plan that most people smiled at and moved on from.

Not hypothetical anymore.

I walk the perimeter. This is what I do now — walk the building, check the systems, talk to the people who need talking to. The walking isn't necessary. We have volunteers assigned to every station, competent people on rotations that Sophie designed and that work flawlessly. I walk because it's the thing my body knows how to do when my mind is running too hot to sit still. I walk because the people in this building look up when I pass and something in their faces settles, the way a room settles when a known quantity enters it. I don't know what they see in me. Competence, maybe. Certainty. The appearance of a man who has a plan.

I have a plan for today. And tomorrow. And the day after. I don't have a plan for the year or the decade or the rest of whatever this is. Nobody does. The plan extends as far as the supplies last, as far as the hydroponic systems produce, as far as the generators run. Beyond that is a horizon I can't see past, and I've made peace with that, mostly, in the three days since the horizon became real instead of theoretical.

First stop: the south wall. The water station. Two five-gallon jugs, refilled every eight hours from the recycling system in the basement. The level is fine. Three-quarters full. I check the seal on the tap, check the drainage tray underneath. Fine. Move on.

---

The medical station. 5:22 AM. Elena is already awake — or still awake. I can't tell which. She's at the folding table that serves as her triage desk, writing in a composition notebook by the light of a battery-powered lamp. Her handwriting is small and precise. Medical log. Patient notes. Inventory.

She looks up when I approach. Her face is drawn — thinner than three days ago, or maybe that's the light. Dark circles under her eyes. Her hair is pulled back with a rubber band because she lost her hair tie on day one and hasn't found another. She looks exhausted. She looks beautiful. She looks like a woman doing the thing she decided to do, fully, without reservation.

"Hey," she says.

"Hey. How's inventory?"

"Holding. Ibuprofen is going fast — people have tension headaches from the air pressure changes. I've been rationing to four doses per person per day. Antibiotics are fine. Bandages fine. We're good on saline for another week."

"Anything overnight?"

"Gerald had another panic attack at 2 AM. I got him through it. The Henderson's cat escaped the carrier and someone with a cat allergy had a reaction. Benadryl handled it. Nothing serious."

"How are you?"

She looks at me. A long look. The kind of look that asks whether I'm asking as her partner or as the guy who runs the shelter. The answer is both, and she knows it, and she decides to answer both.

"I'm tired. And I'm okay. And I need you to eat something before you do anything else today."

"I will."

"You said that yesterday. I watched you not do it for six hours."

"I'll eat. I promise."

She holds my gaze for one more second. Then she nods. Returns to her notebook. I put my hand on her shoulder — just briefly, just the weight of it, the way I've learned to touch her in this new context where privacy doesn't exist and intimacy has been reduced to these small gestures, these brief moments of contact that say *I'm here, you're here, we're still us.*

She covers my hand with hers. Two seconds. Then she lets go, and I move on.

---

The basement. 5:31 AM. I take the stairs down — metal, industrial, our footsteps echoing in the concrete stairwell. The basement is cooler than the main floor. The hum of equipment fills the space — the water recycling system, the air filtration units, the backup generator running at low load. The hydroponic grow rooms are through a reinforced door at the end of the corridor. Green light spills from underneath — the LEDs that simulate sunlight for the plants that can no longer get it from the sky.

Marcus is here. Of course he is. He's standing in front of the filtration bank with a clipboard, checking readings against the baseline numbers he established on day one. He's wearing the same clothes he was wearing three days ago. A pencil is behind his ear. His face, when he turns, is calm in the way that Marcus has always been calm — the deep calm of a man whose confidence comes from competence rather than ignorance.

"Morning," he says.

"Morning. How's the building?"

"Same as yesterday. Walls are solid. No new cracks. Foundation is dry. The settlement reading on the north wall hasn't changed in forty-eight hours, which means we're past the adjustment period from the added load. We're stable."

"Filtration?"

"Running at eighty-three percent efficiency. That's above spec. The particulates in the outside air are actually larger than we planned for — easier to filter. Ironic."

"How long can we run like this?"

Marcus sets the clipboard down. Looks at me directly. "You're asking how long the systems last."

"Yes."

"Filtration: indefinitely, as long as we can replace the filters. I have stock for eighteen months. Water recycling: same story. Moving parts wear out eventually, but I've got replacement components for two years. The generator is the bottleneck — fuel. At current consumption, we have four months of diesel. If we cut back to essential systems only, six."

"And the solar?"

"Producing about forty percent of our load during the gray. Not enough to run everything, but enough to extend the diesel significantly. If we're smart about it — maybe a year. Longer if Chen's data about the particulate density holds and it doesn't get worse."

A year. Maybe longer. Maybe not long enough. But longer than most people will have.

"Good work, Marcus."

He nods. Picks up his clipboard. "Go eat something."

"Elena already told me."

"Elena's right."

I go back upstairs.

---

5:48 AM. The concession area. Our food prep station. Three folding tables pushed together, bins of canned goods organized by type, a two-burner propane stove for heating, a coffee percolator that Frank found in a closet on day one and got running through sheer stubbornness.

I eat. A can of peaches, cold from the tin, and a piece of bread from the stock we baked yesterday in the concession oven. The bread is dense and slightly underdone — our baker is a volunteer named Jackie who ran a home bakery before this and is doing her best with limited ingredients and a commercial oven she's still learning. It's food. It's fuel. I eat it standing up, looking out at the main floor, watching the gymnasium come slowly awake.

People stir. A child cries briefly and is quieted. Someone coughs. The murmur of low voices begins — the sound of a community waking into its fourth day of something none of them have a name for yet. Not an emergency, exactly. An emergency implies an end point. This is something else. This is the new shape of things.

I finish the bread. Drink coffee from a paper cup. The coffee is weak — rationed grounds, stretched with chicory that someone brought from home — but it's hot and it's bitter and it's the closest thing to normal that exists in this building.

---

6:00 AM. Sophie's communications station.

My sister is asleep in her chair, headphones around her neck, her head tilted back at an angle that will hurt when she wakes. The satellite link glows green on the laptop beside her. The marine radio hisses softly — static, the sound of the atmosphere between here and everywhere else. Her handwritten log is open on the table, the last entry from 3:47 AM: *Minneapolis node reports stable. All Midwest nodes green. Network holding.*

I don't wake her. I stand there for a moment and I look at her. Sophie. Thirty-one. My sister who stopped speaking to me for a year after Dad's funeral, who said *I'm scared of you* when she was twenty, who drove two hours to a gas station at midnight because she needed someone to sit with. Who is now sleeping in a metal folding chair in a gymnasium with headphones around her neck because she spent four years learning satellite communications so that on this day, this specific day, she would be the person connecting our shelter to the rest of the network.

She didn't have to do any of this. She didn't have to believe me. She didn't have to learn the equipment. She didn't have to be here. She chose it. All of it. The way everyone in this building chose to be here — not because they had to, but because somewhere in the years before this morning, someone offered them a hand and they took it.

I pull a blanket from the supply shelf and drape it over her shoulders. Gently. She shifts but doesn't wake. I step back.

The satellite link pulses green. Eight nodes. Twelve thousand people.

---

7:15 AM. The gymnasium is awake now. People are moving — to the water station, to the food area, to the bathrooms we've set up in the locker rooms. The hum of low conversation fills the space. Not cheerful. Not panicked. Something in between. The muted sound of people who are scared and tired and alive and trying to figure out what that means today.

I'm at the main entrance, checking the seal on the doors — the weather stripping we installed to keep the outside air from seeping in — when Frank appears beside me.

He's carrying two cups of coffee. He holds one out without saying anything. I take it. We stand there side by side, looking at the gymnasium through the small window in the entrance door.

"How many people?" he asks.

"Two sixty-three in here. Seventy-one at the mill."

"Hmm." He sips his coffee. The sound he makes — a small satisfied exhale through the nose — is a sound I've been hearing since I was a child. The sound Frank makes when coffee is hot and available and the morning is what it is.

"Mom's okay?" I ask.

"Your mother's fine. She's back there helping the woman with the twins get organized. Found some baby formula in that stuff I brought." He pauses. "She's in her element."

"Yeah."

"She would've been good in a war. That generation — her mother was in a war. You get it from somewhere."

I look at him. "You too."

"Me?" A short sound. Not quite a laugh. "I'm just carrying things. Anybody can carry things."

That's not true and we both know it. But Frank Callahan has never in his life acknowledged his own value out loud, and he's not going to start on day four of the apocalypse. He just drinks his coffee and looks at the room and he's here. That's the whole thing. He's here.

We stand in silence for a while. The gray light presses against the windows. Somewhere on the main floor, someone laughs — an unexpected sound, bright and brief, and then gone. But it happened.

"You did a good job, bud," Frank says. He's not looking at me. He's looking at the room.

I don't say anything. I can't. My throat has closed around something I don't have a name for. I drink my coffee. The silence holds.

---

The morning passes. The routine of survival, repeated. Water check. Generator check. Medical rounds. Communications update from Sophie, awake now, blanket folded neatly on her chair, voice crisp on the satellite link as she checks in with Des Moines and Omaha and Minneapolis.

I walk the floor. I talk to people. Not speeches. Not reassurance. Just conversation. The Hendersons tell me about their cat — his name is Whiskers, which is the most pedestrian name for a cat in the history of cats, and Mr. Henderson knows this and doesn't care. Jackie the baker wants to try sourdough if we can maintain a starter with the temperature variations in the concession area. A teenager named Marcus — different Marcus, fourteen, quiet — asks me if I think school will start again in the fall.

"I don't know," I tell him. "But there are smart people working on it."

He nods. He looks at me with eyes that are too old for fourteen. Eyes that have processed something in the last three days that no kid should have to process. Then he goes back to his cot and picks up the book he was reading — something with a spaceship on the cover, which feels either perfectly appropriate or perfectly absurd, and maybe those are the same thing.

I walk. I check. I talk. The building holds. The systems run. The people inside it are scared and tired and bored and alive. This is what it looks like. This is what survival looks like on day four. Not triumph. Not resolution. Just continuation. Just the insistent, stubborn, ordinary act of being alive in a building with other people who are also alive, under a sky that has changed in ways none of them fully understand.

---

11:30 AM. Chen's scheduled check-in.

Her face on the laptop is more tired than yesterday. The pixelation of the satellite link makes it hard to read her expression precisely, but I know her well enough to fill in what the resolution doesn't show. Three days of monitoring. Three days of data streaming in from atmospheric sensors and orbital tracking and ground stations and the handful of academic contacts she's maintained around the world who are also awake, also working, also trying to understand.

"Status," she says. No preamble. Chen doesn't do preamble.

"Stable. Two sixty-three here, seventy-one at the mill. Systems nominal. No medical emergencies."

"Good." She pushes her glasses up. "Network update: we have fourteen nodes confirmed active now. Three new ones came online overnight — two in the Southeast, one in California. Approximate total personnel in shelters: eighteen thousand."

Eighteen thousand. Up from twelve. People are finding the nodes. The network is growing. Not fast enough. Never fast enough. But growing.

"The vessels," I say. "Any change?"

"No. Position stable. Harvesting activity continues in agricultural and forested zones. Urban areas are experiencing secondary effects — plant die-off, atmospheric density, some structural failures in buildings with vegetation-dependent foundations — but the primary extraction is still focused elsewhere."

"So we're not the target."

"We were never the target, Nate. The biomass is the target. We just happen to be standing in it."

I think about that. Mice in the wheat field. The combine doesn't care about the mice. Doesn't hate them. Doesn't want them dead. Just doesn't factor them into the calculation at all.

"How long?" I ask. The same question I asked three days ago. The same question everyone asks.

"I still don't know. The extraction rate suggests — if it continues at current levels — significant depletion of surface biomass within months. But I don't know their threshold. I don't know when they consider the field harvested and move on. If they move on."

"Will they?"

Chen looks at me through the screen. Her face is still. Her eyes are steady behind her glasses. "I don't know, Nate. But we're here. And we're maintaining. And the network is growing. That's what we control. That's what we focus on."

"Yeah."

"How are you?"

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. You're running on four hours of sleep and institutional coffee and the sheer momentum of eighteen years of preparation. But you're functional, which is what matters right now."

"Thanks, Chen."

"Eat something."

"Everyone keeps telling me that."

"Because you don't eat when you're in mission mode. You've been in mission mode for three days. Eat."

I nod. She nods back. The screen goes dark.

---

Afternoon. The gymnasium finds its rhythm. People settle into routines — routines that are only three days old but already have the weight of habit. The coffee station is busy after meals. Children are in the reading corner that Diane organized, sitting on blankets, listening to Mr. Henderson read from a picture book he brought for reasons no one has questioned. The teenager with the spaceship book has found two others his age and they're sitting together on adjacent cots, not talking much, just being near each other. The particular comfort of proximity when you're fourteen and the world has changed and you don't know what to say about it.

I eat. Twice. Elena watches me do it the second time and nods in a way that means *good* and also *I'm keeping count.* I drink water. I sit down for ten minutes in the coach's office and close my eyes and I don't sleep but I breathe. I breathe and the building hums around me and I let myself exist, for ten minutes, as something other than the man who runs the shelter. As just a man. Tired. Alive. Breathing.

Then I get up and go back to the floor.

---

4:47 PM. Three days and ten hours since the sky changed.

I'm standing in the middle of the gymnasium floor, and I'm looking at it. All of it. The whole room. The cots and the people and the blankets and the medical station and the communications array and the food prep area and the water station and the children and the dogs — Hazard isn't the only one, three other families brought pets — and the elderly couple with their cat and the volunteers moving between stations and the gray light falling through the high windows like something gentler than it is.

I'm looking at it and I'm thinking: this is what I built.

Not alone. Never alone. Chen's mind. Marcus's hands. Sophie's voice on the radio. Elena's steadiness. Frank's truck full of blankets. A hundred volunteers over a decade and a half. A thousand small decisions that accumulated into this moment, this room, these people alive and together under a roof that holds.

But I started it. I'm the one who woke up at seventeen with knowledge no one else had and made a choice about what to do with it. And the choice wasn't perfect — it was corrupted, sometimes, by the old machinery, by the habits of a man who used to manipulate and leverage and control. The choice was compromised by Aldiss in his office, by the years I spent treating people as variables, by every moment I slid backward into the version of myself that the first timeline created.

But the choice held. Imperfectly. With damage. With cost. But it held.

And this room is the evidence.

---

5:30 PM. The light through the windows is dimming again — the gray deepening toward something that isn't darkness exactly, just density. The end of another day that doesn't end the way days used to end, with color and horizon and the slow warmth of sunset. Just the gradual thickening of gray into something that approximates night.

I'm at the south wall, refilling my water cup, when I feel someone behind me. I turn.

Frank.

He's standing ten feet away, by the edge of the sleeping area. He's been walking the floor again — blankets over his arm, though by now everyone who needs one has one. He's walking because that's what he does. The same reason I walk. The need to move through the space. To be visible. To be present in a way that the body requires and the mind can't talk it out of.

He's looking at me.

Not the quick assessment look — the two-second scan for damage. A longer look. The kind of look Frank almost never gives anyone, because Frank communicates in monosyllables and physical presence and the language of showing up, not in sustained eye contact.

He's looking at me and his face is doing something I've never seen it do. Not a smile — Frank doesn't do overt smiles. Something underneath that. Something that lives in the set of his jaw and the stillness of his eyes and the way his shoulders are angled toward me even though his body is facing the room. Something that says — without words, because Frank would never say it in words — something that says: *I see what you did. I see what this is. I see what you became.*

He nods.

That's all. Just a nod. A small downward motion of the chin, a millimeter of movement, the most minimal possible physical gesture a human being can make that still carries meaning. But it's Frank's nod. And I know what it means because I've been learning his language for eighteen years, since I woke up in my childhood bedroom and came downstairs and saw him at the kitchen table with the Cedar Falls Courier and understood — really understood, for the first time in either life — what it meant to be seen by a man who doesn't waste words on things he can say with his presence.

The nod means: *I'm proud of you.*

The nod means: *This is enough.*

The nod means: *You did it, bud.*

I nod back. Same gesture. Same minimal motion. Father and son, across ten feet of gymnasium floor, in the gray light of a world that has changed forever, speaking in the only language they've ever fully shared.

My throat is tight. My eyes sting. I don't look away and neither does he. Five seconds. Maybe less. Then Frank shifts his weight, adjusts the blanket over his arm, and turns back toward the room. Back to work. Back to the thing that needs doing.

I watch him go. My father. Sixty-six years old. A stent in his heart. Blankets over his arm. Walking the floor of a gymnasium shelter on the fourth day of the end of the world because that's what there is to do and he's here and he does what's here.

I stand at the south wall with my cup of water and I breathe.

---

The evening settles over the gymnasium like a weight. Not oppressive — just present. The gray light dims. The overhead fluorescents take over, casting their flat white across the floor. People eat. People talk. Someone plays a guitar in the corner — quietly, a fingerpicking pattern I don't recognize, something gentle and repetitive that fills the space without demanding anything of it.

I sit on a cot at the edge of the floor. Elena is beside me. She's leaning against my shoulder, her eyes closed, not sleeping but resting. My arm is around her. Hazard is at our feet, his chin on my boot, his ears relaxed for the first time in three days.

From here I can see the whole room.

Sophie at her station, headphones on, the green glow of the satellite link reflected on her face. She's talking to someone — Kansas City, maybe, or one of the new Southeast nodes — in the low, clear voice she uses for comms. Professional. Steady. My sister.

Marcus crossing the floor with his clipboard, heading for the basement stairs. His walk is unhurried. Efficient. The walk of a man who knows exactly what he needs to check and exactly where to find it. My friend. My first friend, in this timeline. The one who believed me when I was twenty and had nothing to offer except an impossible story and the desperate hope that someone would listen.

Chen — not here in body, but her presence in the satellite link, in the network, in the data streaming across the laptop in the command center. The mind that took my impossible knowledge and built a framework around it. The scientist who said *I don't need to understand the mechanism to trust the data* and then spent three years proving the data right.

Frank. In his folding chair by the east wall, cup of coffee in hand, watching the room. Diane beside him, knitting something — she brought needles and yarn in her tote bag, because Diane Callahan is the kind of woman who brings knitting to the apocalypse because people will need warm things and warm things don't make themselves.

And Derek Holt, on his cot across the floor. He's lying on his back, hands behind his head, staring at the ceiling. I don't know what he's thinking. I don't need to know. He's here. He's alive. That's enough.

I look at all of them and I think about the question the novel of my life has been asking for eighteen years: *Was Nate Callahan worth saving?*

Not: *Did Nate Callahan save the world?* The world isn't saved. The Kessari are still out there. The sky is still gray. The plants are still dying. The crisis isn't resolved and may never be resolved and humanity's long-term survival is a question mark that no one in this room or any room can answer yet.

The question isn't whether I saved the world. The question is whether the man who was given a second chance did something with it that justified the chance. Whether the specific life of Nathan Callahan, lived twice — once badly, once imperfectly but with intention — adds up to something that matters.

I look at the room. Two hundred and sixty-three people, alive, together, sheltered. A network of eighteen thousand across the country. A father with a blanket over his arm. A sister on the radio. A partner leaning against my shoulder. A friend checking the water pressure. A physicist on the satellite link. A dog at my feet.

The question answers itself. Not with triumph. Not with certainty. Just with this — a room full of people who are alive because someone cared enough to prepare. A quiet Tuesday evening in a gymnasium at the end of the world, where the lights are on and the water runs and people are eating canned peaches and someone is playing guitar and a man is sitting with his arm around the woman he loves and his dog at his feet and his family in the room and the building holds.

The building holds.

---

It's late. The gymnasium is mostly dark — fluorescents dimmed to their lowest setting, the nighttime mode we established on day one. The gray light through the windows has gone fully dark now, or as dark as it gets. Not true dark. A charcoal murk that passes for night in the new world.

People are sleeping. The soft sound of breathing fills the space — two hundred and sixty-three people, breathing in their own rhythms, unconscious and trusting and alive. Children curled under blankets. Families pressed together on cots that were never designed for two. Singles and strangers side by side, sharing space the way humans share space when there's no alternative and no pretense and nothing left to protect except the bare fact of being here.

I'm awake. I'm sitting on a folding chair near the south wall, my back against the cinder block, a cup of cold coffee on the floor beside me. Elena is asleep on a cot ten feet away. Hazard is with her, pressed against her legs. Frank and Diane are asleep in the corner they've claimed — Frank on a cot, Diane in the folding chair beside him, her hand on his arm even in sleep. Sophie is asleep at her station, headphones around her neck again, the satellite link pulsing its quiet green.

I'm alone. The first time in three days I've been alone — not literally, I'm in a room with two hundred and sixty-three people, but alone in the specific sense of not being needed. Not being asked a question. Not making a decision. Not moving toward the next task. Just sitting. Just existing in the quiet.

The building hums. The filtration system. The generator. The distant gurgle of the water recycling. The sounds of a thing that works, running in the dark, doing what it was built to do.

I think about the first timeline. The morning of my thirty-fifth birthday. The apartment at Briarwood. The eviction notice. The empty bottles. The spider plant dying on the windowsill. The gray sky through the blinds. The man I was then — soft, drunk, hollowed out, alone in every way a person can be alone. The man who watched the world end from the window of a studio apartment with nothing in his life worth saving and no one to save him and no one he had saved.

That man died. Not metaphorically. Actually died. Was unmade by something vast and indifferent that didn't care whether he existed or not.

And then — by some mechanism I still don't understand, that Chen has set aside as irrelevant to our response, that I've stopped trying to explain — he woke up. At seventeen. In a body that hadn't been ruined yet. In a house where his father was still alive and his mother still spoke to him and his sister still loved him without qualification. In a life that still had all its doors open.

And he chose.

Not perfectly. Not cleanly. With stumbles and compromises and one blackmail and years of secrecy and a period of drinking that almost undid everything and a relationship destroyed and rebuilt and the slow, grinding, daily work of becoming someone different from who you were. But he chose. Every day for eighteen years. He chose to be the version of himself that earned the room he's sitting in now.

I don't know if that's enough. I don't know if anything is ever enough. The Kessari are still up there. The world is still broken. People are still dying, out there, in places that didn't have a shelter, that didn't have a Nate Callahan or a Judith Chen or a Marcus Wade. People are dying right now and I can't stop it and I couldn't stop it and that weight will live in me for the rest of my life, however long that is.

But this room is real. These people are alive. This building holds. And I'm here — not drunk, not dead, not unmade — sitting in a folding chair at the end of the world with a cup of cold coffee and a family sleeping ten feet away and a network of eighteen thousand people across the country who are also alive, also sheltered, also holding.

I think about something Frank said to me once. Years ago. I was twenty-two, fresh out of the hospital after the drinking got bad enough to put me there, sitting on his porch in Cedar Falls while he fixed a gutter that didn't need fixing. He didn't look at me. Just worked. And then he said, without preamble, without turning around: "You don't have to earn the right to be here, bud. You just have to show up."

I didn't understand it then. I'm not sure I understand it now. But I think what he meant is: the point isn't becoming someone worthy. The point isn't the destination. The point is the showing up. The daily, repetitive, unglamorous act of being present. Of doing the thing that's in front of you. Of being in the room.

I'm in the room.

The gray light presses against the windows. The building hums. Two hundred and sixty-three people breathe in the dark. The world outside is broken and vast and indifferent and continuing without us and because of us and despite us.

I sit in my folding chair. I drink my cold coffee. I watch the room.

Anyway. The point is—

I finish the sentence. For the first time. All the way to the end.
