## Chapter 23: The Ones Who Stay

Marcus comes over on a Saturday in June. He doesn't know why I've asked him. He thinks it's about NEORAN — maybe the new shelter site in Omaha, or the seed vault partnership that's been stalling. He brings beer. Sets it on the counter.

"So," he says. "What's the fire?"

"Sit down."

He looks at me. Something in my face must register because he doesn't argue. Just pulls out a chair at the kitchen table and sits.

Elena is upstairs. She offered to stay. I told her no — this is between me and Marcus. But I know she's up there, probably pretending to read, actually listening through the floor. Hazard is asleep under the table. His breathing is steady. Grounding.

"I need to tell you something," I say. "And I need you to hear all of it before you respond."

"Okay." Marcus leans back. Crosses his arms. The posture of a man who's heard a lot of pitches. "Go."

So I go.

I start at the beginning — the real beginning. Not college, not NEORAN, not the blackmail. The first life. The drinking. The dead-end jobs. Waterloo. The years of nothing. Frank's funeral. The displacement.

Marcus's face doesn't change for the first ten minutes. He's an engineer. He listens to complex systems all day. He collects data before reacting.

But at the fifteen-minute mark — around the part where I explain waking up at seventeen with thirty-five years of memories — his jaw tightens. And when I get to the part about knowing the future, about the stock predictions and the atmospheric data and the objects approaching Earth, he uncrosses his arms.

"Wait," he says.

"I asked you to hear all of it."

"The — you're saying time travel."

"I'm saying I lived another life. I came back. I don't have a mechanism."

"That's not—" He stops. Presses his lips together. "Keep going."

I keep going. NEORAN's real purpose. Chen's confirmation of the data. The trajectory projections. The deceleration. The objects that steer.

When I finish, Marcus is very still. His beer is untouched. His hands are flat on the table.

"How long," he says, "have you known me?"

"Since freshman year. In both timelines."

"And in the first one?"

I take a breath. "You gave me twelve hundred dollars. Senior year of college. I was behind on rent. You put the money in an envelope on the kitchen counter with a key."

Marcus's eyes flicker. Something.

"I never paid you back. I moved out that summer. I left the key on the counter with a note that said 'Sorry.' And we never spoke again."

The kitchen is very quiet. Hazard shifts under the table. Snores.

"The twelve hundred," Marcus says slowly. "You know about the twelve hundred."

"I was there."

"I never told anyone about that. Not — anyone."

"I know."

His hands flex on the table. Flat. Then fists. Then flat again. "And the blackmail. In — what was it, 2019?"

"I needed you to stay. I'd lost you once. I couldn't—" I stop. Because this is the ugly part. The part where I was wrong. "I blackmailed a city council member — Aldiss — to get infrastructure permits through. Used information about his affair as leverage. It worked. It was the worst thing I've done in this life."

Marcus nods. Slow. Not forgiving — acknowledging. He knew about the blackmail — he'd confronted me about it years ago. He stayed despite it. But now he knows the why behind the permits.

"You blackmailed a guy to get your permits."

"Yes."

"Because in the other version—"

"You left. And I deserved it. And this time I was terrified of it happening again and I did a terrible thing to prevent it."

"You could have just told me the truth."

"I was twenty-three. I was terrified. I wasn't ready."

Marcus picks up his beer. Puts it back down without drinking. Stands up. Walks to the window. Looks out at the backyard — the garden, the shed, the fence I built two summers ago.

"I need evidence," he says.

"I have it."

"Not your word. Data. The papers. Chen's number. Cross-references. The trajectory data."

"All of it. Whatever you need."

He turns. His face is — complicated. Not angry. Not disbelieving. Something harder to name. Something that looks like a man standing at the edge of a cliff, deciding whether the ground below is real.

"Give me everything. And I'm going to verify. Everything."

"Take as long as you need."

"I'll need days."

"I know."

He nods. Once. Walks to the table. Picks up his beer. Looks at it.

"Does this one still count?" he asks. "Or is future-beer different somehow?"

A laugh breaks out of me. Surprised and grateful. "It still counts."

He cracks it open. Drinks. Sets it down.

"I'm going to go. I'll take the documents. And I'm going to verify every single data point. And if it checks out—" He stops. "And if it doesn't—"

"Then I'm delusional and you can drive me to a psychiatrist."

"Deal."

He picks up the folder I've prepared — thick, organized, tabbed. Chen's papers. Trajectory data. Atmospheric readings. Prediction logs with dates. My notebooks.

At the door, he pauses.

"The key on the counter," he says. "The note that said 'sorry.' In the first version. What did I do? When I found it?"

"I don't know. I was already gone."

He stands in the doorway. His face does something I can't read.

"I would have been angry," he says.

"Yeah."

"I would have hated you."

"Probably."

"And you spent eighteen years making sure that didn't happen."

It's not a question. He doesn't wait for an answer. He leaves. I hear his car start. The crunch of gravel. Then nothing.

Elena comes downstairs four minutes later.

"How'd it go?"

"He took the documents. He's going to verify."

"And then?"

"I don't know."

She sits beside me. Hazard crawls out from under the table and puts his head on her foot.

"He'll come back," she says.

"How do you know?"

"Because he's Marcus. And you're his best friend. And the evidence will check out." She pauses. "Also because he left his other four beers in the fridge."

---

Five days. Marcus takes five days.

I don't call him. Don't text. Don't drive past his apartment. I give him the space he needs because I've learned — the hard way, in another life — that some people need to reach their own conclusions. You can't push someone into belief.

Day one, Chen calls me. "Your friend Marcus called. He's very thorough."

"What did you tell him?"

"The data. He asked good questions. I gave good answers."

"Did he believe you?"

"He believed the data. That's all that matters."

Day two, nothing.

Day three, Sophie texts: *Marcus called me. Asked about NEORAN operational details. I told him everything. He sounded calm.*

Day four, nothing.

Day five. Saturday evening. 7 PM. The doorbell rings.

I open it. Marcus is standing on the porch. He's wearing a wrinkled dress shirt and jeans. He looks like he hasn't slept well. There's a legal pad in his hand — the yellow kind, covered in notes.

"Can I come in?"

"Yeah."

He comes in. Sits at the kitchen table. Same chair as before. Puts the legal pad down. Looks at me.

"I called Chen," he says. "Twice. She walked me through the trajectory mathematics. The deceleration data is real. Objects in vacuum don't decelerate without thrust. That's— that's physics."

"Yeah."

"I cross-referenced your prediction logs against historical records. Seventeen out of twenty verifiable predictions accurate to within a week. Three were off by small margins — days, mostly. One was wrong entirely."

"The Senate vote in 2018."

"Yeah. You predicted it would fail. It passed."

"Things change. When you interfere with a timeline, things shift."

Marcus nods. Makes a note. "I talked to Sophie. She walked me through NEORAN's infrastructure. Forty-three sites. Twelve states. Operational protocols. She knows everything."

"She knows most of it. Not the time travel part."

He looks up. "She doesn't know?"

"She knows what's coming. Not how I know."

"But Frank does."

"Yeah."

"And Diane."

"Yeah."

Marcus sets his pen down. Leans back. Runs both hands through his hair — a gesture I've seen a thousand times, the one that means he's arrived at a conclusion and is about to say it.

"You had eighteen years of future knowledge," he says. "You knew what was going to happen. Markets. Elections. Technology. Everything."

"Yes."

"You could have made a billion dollars."

"Yes."

"You could have — I don't know — been president. Predicted 9/11. Prevented wars."

"I thought about all of it. Early on."

"And you spent them—" He gestures around the kitchen. At the house. At Hazard asleep on the floor. At the window where you can see the neighbor's fence. "You spent them fixing your relationship with your dad. And building a disaster nonprofit. In Iowa."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The question hangs. Simple. The simplest question.

"Because the first time around, I was rich in nothing that mattered."

Marcus is quiet. He picks up the pen. Puts it down. Picks it up again.

"The twelve hundred thing," he says. "The key on the counter."

"Yeah."

"In the first version. I left a key and you left a note and that was it."

"That was it."

"And you spent eighteen years making sure that didn't happen."

"Yeah."

His eyes go bright. Not tears — Marcus doesn't cry. But a brightness. A heat behind the eyes that says the truth has landed somewhere deep. Somewhere structural.

"I verified for five days," he says. "I called your physicist. I cross-referenced data. I went through seventeen predictions line by line."

"And?"

"And the evidence is overwhelming. The data is real. The deceleration is real. Something is coming."

"Yeah."

"And the time travel—"

"You don't have to believe that part. Chen doesn't."

"I don't need to believe it," Marcus says. "I just need to believe you. And I've known you for twelve years in this version and you've never lied to me. Except the blackmail. The Aldiss thing."

"Yeah."

"Which you've explained. And apologized for. Repeatedly."

"Yeah."

He picks up the legal pad. Flips to a clean page. Takes the cap off his pen.

"Okay," he says. "So. Eighteen months."

"Approximately."

"And NEORAN is at — what. Seventy percent readiness?"

"Something like that. Eighty on shelters. Sixty on communications. Fifty on food production."

"Then we have work to do." He starts writing. Lists. Columns. The engineer's brain clicking into gear. "Food production is the gap. Sixty percent won't sustain the population numbers you're projecting. We need—"

"Marcus."

He looks up.

"You're staying."

It's not a question. But he answers anyway.

"I'm staying." He clicks the pen. "You had eighteen years of future knowledge, and you spent them becoming a guy who shows up." He shakes his head. Slight. Amazed. "Of course I'm staying."

---

The summer accelerates. Marcus embeds in NEORAN full-time — takes unpaid leave from his engineering firm. Within two weeks, he's redesigned the hydroponic yield projections. Within a month, he's identified sixteen structural vulnerabilities in the shelter network that nobody else caught.

"Your ventilation specs are wrong," he tells Sophie on his first day in the office.

"Excuse me?"

"The airflow calculations assume standard atmospheric conditions. If there's particulate contamination — which is what Chen's data suggests — you need three times the filtration capacity."

Sophie looks at me. Back at Marcus. "Who is this person?"

"He's your new head of infrastructure."

"I didn't agree to—"

"Yes you did."

She glares. But by the end of the week, they're working side by side. Sophie on communications and logistics. Marcus on structural engineering and life support. Two competent people who speak the same language of spreadsheets and deadlines and contingency planning.

---

July. Full-network test.

All forty-three sites. Simultaneous activation. Communications, power systems, water recycling, air filtration. Sophie coordinates from the grain elevator office. Marcus monitors structural telemetry from a laptop balanced on a filing cabinet.

I watch. That's mostly what I do now — watch. The machine I built has outgrown me. It runs on the competence of people who are better at their specific jobs than I ever could be. Sophie. Marcus. The site coordinators. Chen, on satellite link, monitoring atmospheric data in real time.

"All sites reporting," Sophie announces. "Thirty-nine green. Four yellow — minor issues. Zero red."

"Which four?" Marcus asks.

"Duluth, Boise, Little Rock, and Austin. Duluth is a communication lag — probably the satellite realignment. Boise has a water pressure issue. Little Rock and Austin are backup generator delays."

"None of those are structural."

"No."

"Then we're good."

"We're good."

I stand in the corner of the office and watch them work and I think: this is what it looks like. Eighteen years of building, and this is what it looks like. Not me saving the world. Not one person with future knowledge making heroic choices. Just a network. Just people. Just competence and trust and the simple, radical act of showing up.

---

August. Chen's third paper drops. This one doesn't need a science section. It makes the front page.

*Seven Objects Confirmed on Earth-Approach Trajectory: International Team Publishes Deceleration Data.*

The world notices.

The President makes a statement. Measured. Calm. The language of managed disclosure: "anomalous objects," "continued monitoring," "no immediate threat." But the data is public now. Anyone with a physics degree can run the numbers. Anyone with a calculator can project the arrival window.

Fourteen months.

The news cycle does what it does. Panic. Then denial. Then memes. Then panel discussions. Then a strange settling — the way a river finds its level after a flood. People adjust. They go to work. They buy groceries. They pick up their kids. Because what else can you do? The objects are far away and abstract and the rent is still due on the first.

"They're handling it better than I expected," Elena says, watching the news.

"It's far away. It doesn't feel real yet."

"When will it feel real?"

"When they're visible. Without a telescope."

"How long?"

"Three months out. Maybe four."

She nods. Turns off the television. "Then we have time."

"Some."

"Come to bed."

---

September. NEORAN goes from forty-three sites to sixty-one. The announcement from the President triggered something — state governments reaching out, FEMA partnerships, NGOs with resources looking for people who've been planning for this. We were already here. We were ready.

Marcus handles the expansion. Sophie handles the comms. I handle — what? The vision. The why. Meetings where I sit at the head of the table and explain what NEORAN does and why and someone inevitably asks: "How did you know? How were you ready?"

And I say: "I've been planning for this for a long time."

Which is the truest thing I've ever said.

---

October. Elena and I are in the living room. Late. Hazard between us on the couch — too old and dignified to sleep on the floor anymore. Elena has her laptop open. She's coordinating with veterinary networks across the Midwest — animal triage, livestock protocols, pet evacuation plans. Things no one else thought about.

"There are sixteen million dogs in the affected radius," she says.

"I know."

"No one has a plan for them."

"You do."

She looks at me. Over the top of her laptop. That look — the one that says: *I see what you're doing and I love you for it but also we have a problem.*

"I have a draft," she says. "I need NEORAN resources."

"Name them."

"Transport. Storage. Supply chains for food and medication."

"Done."

"You didn't even look at the numbers."

"I trust your numbers."

She studies me. Then smiles. Small, warm, private. "You've gotten better at that."

"At what?"

"Trusting people."

She's right. In the first life, I trusted no one. Couldn't. Trust requires being present, and I was never present. Always half somewhere else — the bar, the next town, the next excuse.

Now I trust. Elena. Marcus. Sophie. Frank and Diane. Chen. The sixty-one site coordinators. The network. The people.

It's the best thing I've ever built. And I barely built it. I just showed up, and they built it around me.

---

November. Thanksgiving. Frank's house.

The whole family. Diane's pot roast — Thanksgiving edition, which means turkey AND pot roast because Frank refuses to choose. Sophie and her boyfriend, a quiet electrician named David who installs solar arrays for NEORAN and blushes when Sophie talks about him. Elena beside me. Marcus across the table — invited for the first time, because he has no family nearby and because he's earned his place.

Frank carves the turkey. He's good at it — precise, methodical, the same way he used to cut pipe. His hands are steady. His color is good. The stent holds.

"Marcus," Frank says, passing a plate. "Sophie says you fixed the ventilation."

"It was a calculation error. Not hard to fix."

"Takes a smart person to find a calculation error."

"Takes a smart person to build the system in the first place." Marcus glances at me.

Frank follows the glance. Nods. The smallest acknowledgment — a triangle of knowing. Frank, Marcus, me. The three people at this table who know everything. Diane knows too, of course. And Elena. But the men have their own geometry. Their own quiet understanding.

"Good turkey," Marcus says.

"Diane's recipe," Frank says.

"My mother's recipe," Diane corrects. "Forty years running."

"Forty-one," Frank says. "We've been married thirty-seven but you made it the year before."

"You remember that?"

"I remember everything you cook."

Diane shakes her head. But she's smiling.

I sit at this table and I eat turkey and pot roast and mashed potatoes and I listen to my family talk about nothing important and I think: in fourteen months, the world changes. In fourteen months, everything I've built gets tested. In fourteen months, these objects arrive and whatever they bring with them — destruction, contact, war, something I can't imagine — it comes.

But tonight, there's turkey. And my father is alive. And my mother is strong. And my sister is competent and fierce. And my best friend is here. And the woman I love is beside me, her hand on my knee under the table, warm and present.

This is what I built. Not the shelters. Not the seed vaults. This.

---

December through March. The longest winter.

The objects are visible now. Not to the naked eye — not yet. But amateur astronomers with decent telescopes can find them. Seven points of light, moving. Decelerating. Coming.

The government ramps up. FEMA activates. National Guard deployments to major metropolitan areas. Shelter programs. Evacuation planning. All the things that should have started years ago, starting now.

NEORAN integrates. Sophie is on calls with FEMA coordinators daily. Marcus reviews structural assessments for government shelter sites and finds — predictably — that they're inadequate.

"They built for earthquakes," he says. "Not for prolonged atmospheric contamination."

"Can they retrofit?"

"In fourteen months? Some of them. Maybe half."

"Then we focus on the half."

"Already on it."

---

Chen is on satellite link now. Full-time monitoring. The data streams in constantly — velocity, trajectory, thermal signatures. The objects are not just decelerating. They're maneuvering. Subtle course corrections. Formation maintenance.

"They're coordinating," Chen tells me, 2 AM on a Tuesday. Her face on the laptop screen is tired. Boltzmann is asleep behind her. "Seven objects maintaining formation geometry. That's not gravity. That's communication."

"What kind of formation?"

"Hexagonal. Six around one. The center object is larger."

"Can you determine size?"

"Working on it. Preliminary estimates — the outer objects are approximately three kilometers in diameter. The center object is eight."

Eight kilometers. A city, flying through space. Decelerating toward Earth.

"Chen. What do you think they want?"

She takes off her glasses. Rubs her eyes. "I'm a physicist, Nate. I can tell you what they're doing. I can't tell you why."

"In the first timeline—"

"In the first timeline, did they communicate?"

"I don't know. Infrastructure collapsed before arrival. Everything I know about the actual event is fragments. Fire. Power failures. Then nothing."

"Then we prepare for the worst and hope for less."

"That's been the plan for eighteen years."

"It's a good plan." She puts her glasses back on. "Go to sleep, Nate. I'll call if anything changes."

---

April. Spring. The one-year mark from the parking lot conversation with Frank.

He texts me on a Sunday morning. The same text he sends every week:

*Dinner Sunday? Your mom's making pot roast.*

I look at the phone. This text. This ordinary, routine, unremarkable text. Every Sunday for years. The same question. The same answer.

In the first life, I never got these texts. By twenty, I'd changed my number three times. Frank didn't have it. Couldn't reach me. Probably tried. Probably called the old numbers and got disconnected tones and eventually stopped trying, because even Frank — even patient, steady, show-up-anyway Frank — has a limit.

But not in this life. In this life, the text comes every Sunday. And every Sunday, I answer.

I type back: *I'll be there.*

Three words. The easiest three words in the world. The hardest three words in another life.

---

The phone buzzes again. Elena, from the kitchen: *Also tell your dad I'm bringing that eggplant dish he pretends to hate.*

I smile. Type back: *He'll complain.*

Elena: *And eat three servings.*

Elena: *That man is a fraud.*

Elena: *A lovable fraud.*

I put the phone down. Hazard is beside me on the bed — fully on the bed now, all pretense of floor-sleeping abandoned. His muzzle is gray. His eyes are cloudy on the left side. He's old. He won't be here forever.

But he's here now. Warm and heavy and breathing against my hip.

---

I get up. Shower. Dress. Normal Sunday things.

In the kitchen, Elena is making the eggplant dish. The kitchen smells like garlic and olive oil. She's humming something — off-key, like Frank, though she'd deny the comparison.

"Coffee's made," she says.

"Thanks."

I pour a cup. Stand at the counter. Watch her cook.

"Stop watching me cook."

"Can't help it."

"You're creepy."

"Noted."

She points the spatula at me. "Go do something useful. Walk the dog."

"The dog's asleep."

"Then sit and drink your coffee and stop making that face."

"What face?"

"The grateful face. The 'I can't believe this is my life' face. You make it every Sunday morning."

"Do I?"

"Every single one." She turns back to the stove. "It's not a bad face. It's just—" She pauses. Stirs. "I see you, Nate. Every time."

I drink my coffee. The morning light comes in through the window over the sink. The eggplant sizzles. Hazard wanders in, drawn by the smell of garlic, and parks himself at Elena's feet with surgical precision.

"Don't give him any," I say.

"I would never."

She absolutely will.

---

Afternoon. We drive to Frank and Diane's. The eggplant in a casserole dish on Elena's lap. Hazard in the back seat, nose pressed to the window.

The house looks the same as it always has. White siding. Blue shutters. The porch swing Frank installed twenty years ago. The garden in the side yard — Diane's, immaculate, even now. Especially now. She's been planting more this year. Root vegetables. Things that store.

She knows. She's preparing, in her way.

Frank is on the porch. Coffee cup. Flannel jacket even though it's April and warming up. He raises a hand as we pull in.

"Eggplant?" he says as we walk up.

"You'll love it," Elena says.

"I'll tolerate it."

"Same thing."

He stands. Holds the door. Hazard goes in first — knows the layout, goes straight to the kitchen where the treats are.

Inside: warmth. The smell of pot roast. Diane at the stove, apron on, radio playing low.

"Sophie's running late," Diane says. "David's working."

"Just us then," Frank says. Settles into his chair at the head of the table.

The four of us. Plus Hazard, who counts.

We eat. We talk. The pot roast is perfect — it's always perfect. Frank complains about the eggplant and eats two helpings. Diane talks about her garden. Elena talks about the veterinary network. Frank asks questions — real ones, engaged ones. He understands now. He understands all of it. And he's not afraid.

Or if he is, he doesn't show it. Because that's Frank. Because facing what's coming is no different from facing a burst pipe in a frozen basement at 3 AM — you assess, you plan, you fix what you can, and you don't waste time on what you can't.

After dinner, I help with dishes. Same as always. Frank washes. I dry.

"The network test," he says. Low. Private. "Sophie said it went well."

"Ninety-six percent."

"And the four percent?"

"Fixed."

He nods. Hands me a plate. "Good."

Silence. Water running. The clink of dishes.

"You scared?" he asks.

I consider lying. Don't.

"Yeah."

"Me too." He scrubs a pot. "But. We're ready as we can be."

"Yeah."

"And the people are good."

"They are."

"Then—" He rinses the pot. Sets it on the rack. "Then we do what we do. Show up. Handle it."

"Yeah."

He drains the sink. Dries his hands. Looks at me.

"You know what you did, right?"

"What do you mean?"

"All of this." He gestures — small, encompassing. The kitchen. The house. The family in the next room. The network beyond. "You could have done anything with what you knew. And you built a family. Built a team. Built a thing that might save people." He puts his hand on my shoulder. Solid. Heavy. "That's not nothing, bud."

"I know."

"I mean it." He squeezes once. Lets go. "Whatever happens next — whenever they get here — you did what you could. And that's enough."

I put the pot away. Close the cabinet.

"Thanks, Dad."

He nods. Walks to the living room. The La-Z-Boy. The television. The documentary — tonight it's about deep-sea creatures, glowing things in the dark.

I stand in the kitchen doorway and watch my family. Diane on the couch, knitting something. Elena cross-legged on the floor, scratching Hazard's belly. Frank in his chair, glasses on, watching luminescent fish drift through black water.

This is what I came back for. Not the objects in the sky. Not the shelters or the seed vaults or the satellite links. This room. These people. This moment.

In fourteen months, the sky changes. In fourteen months, everything I've built gets tested.

But tonight, there's pot roast in my stomach and my father is alive and my family is whole and the dog is getting his belly scratched and the objects are far away and abstract and the evening is warm and the spring peepers are singing outside and everything — for one more night, for this night — everything is okay.

Anyway. The point is—

Not yet. Soon. But not yet.

The point is: I'll be there.
