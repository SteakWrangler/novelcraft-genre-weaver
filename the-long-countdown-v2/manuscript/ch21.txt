## Chapter 21: The Things We Build

The summer comes in hard and fast, the way Iowa summers do — one week it's April rain, the next the corn is knee-high and the air sits on you like a second skin. I spend June in meetings. Conference calls with shelter coordinators in six states. Grant applications. Elena driving to Des Moines every Thursday for veterinary triage training — "If I'm going to be useful," she says, "I'm going to be useful for more than dogs." Sophie running communications drills from the NEORAN office, which is now three rooms in a repurposed grain elevator on the east side of town.

The world doesn't know yet. Not really. Chen's paper got attention in the right circles — atmospheric physicists, a handful of defense analysts, one persistent journalist at the Washington Post who keeps calling Chen's office and getting voicemail. But the general public is doing what the general public does. Living. Working. Arguing about things that won't matter in eighteen months.

I don't blame them. I spent a whole first life not paying attention.

Elena moves in fully in July. Not a grand gesture — she just stops going back to her apartment. Her toothbrush appears in the bathroom holder. Then her books on the shelf. Then a note on the fridge: *Hazard ate my left shoe. We're even now.*

Hazard, for his part, is unrepentant. He's seven now, gray around the muzzle, slower on the stairs. But he still puts his head on my knee every evening at exactly 6:15, which is when I used to feed him before Elena changed the schedule to 6:30. He's patient about it. Fifteen minutes of silent protest, every day.

"He's guilting you," Elena says.

"It's working."

She scratches behind his ears. "Good boy."

---

The NEORAN network is — I can say this now without it sounding like delusion — substantial. Forty-three shelter sites across twelve states. Hydroponic facilities in Iowa, Nebraska, Minnesota, Ohio. Seed vaults partnered with three universities. Communication redundancies: satellite uplinks, ham radio networks, hardened fiber lines. Solar arrays. Water recycling. Medical supply caches.

It's not enough. It will never be enough. But it's more than nothing, which is what existed in the first timeline.

Sophie handles logistics with a competence that still surprises me, though it shouldn't. She was always the organized one. The one who color-coded her homework folders in third grade. Now she color-codes emergency supply manifests.

"You need more medical personnel," she tells me one Tuesday, dropping a spreadsheet on my desk. "We have veterinarians, paramedics, and one retired surgeon. For forty-three sites."

"I know."

"So fix it."

"Working on it."

"Work faster."

She's right. She's usually right. I add it to the list — the list that never gets shorter, only rearranges its priorities.

---

August 14th. A Thursday. I'm in the NEORAN office reviewing shelter hardening specs when my phone buzzes.

Mom.

Not a text. A call. My mother doesn't call during the day unless something is wrong.

"Hey, Mom."

"Nathan." Her voice is controlled. Too controlled. The way she sounds when she's keeping the edges together. "Your father is at Mercy General. He's had — they think it's his heart. We're in the ER."

The room tilts. Not dramatically. Just enough.

"I'm coming."

"He's conscious. He's talking. They're running tests."

"I'm coming."

"Drive carefully."

"I will."

I don't drive carefully. I drive at eighty-seven miles per hour on Route 30, both hands white on the wheel, the cornfields blurring into a green wall on either side. The drive to Mercy General is forty-two minutes at the speed limit. I make it in twenty-nine.

The whole way, a drumbeat in my skull: *Not yet. Not yet. Not like this.*

In the first timeline, Frank died at sixty-three. Cardiac arrest. I was thirty-five, drunk, living in a studio apartment in Waterloo. I flew home for the funeral — showed up hungover, wearing the only suit I owned, which didn't fit. Sophie wouldn't look at me. Diane held my hand during the service and I could feel her fingers shaking and I thought: *I did this. Not the heart attack. But this distance. This absence. I built this.*

He was sixty-three. He's sixty-four now. Four years early. Unless I've changed enough — unless the stress is less, unless the diet is better, unless—

I park crooked in the hospital lot and walk through the automatic doors and my body knows exactly where to go because I have been in hospitals before, in another life, and the layout of grief is always the same. Reception desk. Elevator. Third floor. Cardiac unit.

Mom is in the waiting area. She stands when she sees me. Her face does something complicated — relief and fear braided together.

"He's stable," she says immediately. "They're saying it was mild. A blockage. They're talking about a stent."

I hug her. She's smaller than I remember. When did she get smaller?

"Where is he?"

"Room 314. They're prepping him. You can't go in yet."

So I sit. In a blue plastic chair in a hospital waiting room with CNN on mute and a vending machine humming in the corner. My mother beside me. Her hand finds mine and holds it — not tight, not desperate, just present. The way you hold something you're not willing to drop.

"He was mowing the lawn," she says. "Came inside and said his chest felt tight. I thought he was just overheated. But he sat down and he was gray, Nate. His lips."

"You called 911."

"Immediately." A pause. "He told me not to. Said it was indigestion."

"Of course he did."

A small sound. Almost a laugh. "He said, and I quote, 'Diane, put the phone down, I just need some water.'"

"And you called anyway."

"I've been married to that man for thirty-seven years. I know the difference between indigestion and—" She stops. Breathes. "Yes. I called anyway."

Good. Good. In the first timeline, nobody called. Because in the first timeline, Frank was alone when it happened. Diane was visiting her sister. No one was there to see the gray in his lips or ignore his protests. He sat in his recliner and he died and they found him the next morning.

But that's not this timeline. Diane was there. Diane called. Frank is alive. Frank is alive.

Sophie arrives twenty minutes later, out of breath, still wearing her NEORAN polo. She hugs Diane first, then me.

"What do we know?"

"Mild MI," I say. "They're placing a stent. Prognosis is good."

"How do you know prognosis is good? Are you a cardiologist now?"

"The nurse said—"

"Okay. Okay." She sits. Then stands. Sits again. "I hate this."

"Yeah."

"He's sixty-four."

"I know."

"He's not supposed to—" She stops. Presses her lips together. "He's supposed to be annoying and healthy for another twenty years."

"He will be."

"You don't know that."

"He will be."

She looks at me. Something in her face — the sister-knowledge that runs deeper than words. She nods. Once.

We wait.

---

The cardiologist comes out at 4:47 PM. Dr. Reeves — no relation to the NOAA researcher. She's young, steady-eyed, clipboard in hand.

"Family of Frank Callahan?"

We stand. All three of us.

"Stent placement went well. Single blockage in the LAD artery — we caught it early. He's awake, he's alert, and he's already asked me twice when he can go home."

Relief doesn't crash. It seeps. Through the floor and up through my shoes and into my bones.

"Can we see him?" Diane asks.

"Absolutely. Two at a time."

Diane looks at me. Looks at Sophie.

"Go," I say. "Both of you. I'll wait."

They go. I sit back down in the blue plastic chair. CNN is showing something about a trade dispute. The vending machine hums.

Hazard is at home, probably sitting by the front door, wondering where everyone went. Elena is in Des Moines. I should call her. I pull out my phone.

Me: *Dad had a mild heart attack. He's okay. Stent went well.*

Three dots appear immediately.

Elena: *I'm coming. Which hospital?*

Me: *Mercy General. He's fine. You don't have to—*

Elena: *I'm coming.*

---

Twenty minutes later, Sophie comes out. Red-eyed but smiling.

"He wants to see you," she says. "He's very annoyed about the hospital gown."

"He would be."

I walk down the hall. Room 314. The door is open. Inside: the hum of monitors, a narrow bed, the sharp smell of antiseptic that will always mean fear to me no matter how many times I smell it.

Frank is propped up on pillows. There's an IV in his left arm and a monitor clip on his finger and his hair is mussed and he looks — old. For the first time, he looks old to me. Not ancient. Not frail. Just old. The lines in his face deeper than I remembered, the skin at his neck looser, the hands on the blanket weathered in a way that says: *I have built things with these. I have held things. I am tired.*

But he's alive. His eyes are clear. And when he sees me, they crinkle.

"Bud."

I pull the chair close. Sit.

"Hey, Dad."

"Your mother overreacted."

"You had a heart attack."

"A mild one."

"There's no mild version of your heart stopping."

"It didn't stop. It just — paused."

"Dad."

He waves a hand. The one without the IV. "I'm fine. Look at me. I'm fine."

I look at him. He's fine. He's not fine. He's a sixty-four-year-old man in a hospital bed with a piece of metal in his artery and I can see the fear underneath the bluster — just a flicker, just a shadow in the back of his eyes — because Frank Callahan does not get scared, except when he does, except when his body reminds him that it has a vote.

"The doctor says you need to change your diet."

"The doctor's twelve years old."

"She's a board-certified cardiologist."

"With the face of a child."

"Dad."

"I'll eat the rabbit food." He shifts. Winces slightly. "Don't tell your mother I winced."

"I won't."

"Good." A pause. He looks at me — really looks, the way he does sometimes when he thinks I'm not paying attention. "You drove fast."

"Yeah."

"Don't do that."

"Too late."

"Next time, drive the speed limit. I'll still be here when you arrive."

The word *next time* sits between us. An acknowledgment: this might happen again. Bodies do this. Hearts do this. They pause. Sometimes they resume. Sometimes they don't.

"Okay," I say.

"Okay." He reaches out. Pats my hand twice. Gruff. Quick. "Good kid."

I'm thirty-three years old. I'm a good kid.

---

Elena arrives at 6:30. She's brought sandwiches from the deli near the highway — turkey for me, roast beef for Sophie, egg salad for Diane. Nothing for Frank because he's on a restricted diet and Elena reads every instruction.

"How is he?" she asks, handing me the bag.

"Annoyed and alive."

"Best combination."

She sits with us. Eats. Talks to Diane about garden tomatoes — a conversation so mundane and normal that it functions as medicine. Sophie leans against my shoulder and falls asleep for twenty minutes. The hospital dims around us.

At 8 PM, visiting hours end. We say goodnight. Frank is already half asleep, the television playing some game show he's not watching.

"Tomorrow," I say from the doorway.

"Bud." His eyes are closed. "Go home. Pet the dog."

---

I drive home at the speed limit. Elena follows in her car. At home, Hazard is frantic — circling, whining, pressing his nose against my legs.

"It's okay," I tell him. "He's okay."

Dogs don't understand words, but they understand tone. He settles. Follows me to the couch and puts his head on my knee and sighs the way only dogs can sigh — with their whole body.

Elena sits beside me. Close. Her hand on my thigh.

"You okay?" she asks.

"No."

"Okay."

We sit. The house is quiet. Outside, fireflies. The last of the season.

"In the first life," I say. And stop. Because I don't talk about the first life much. Not the details. Not the small, sharp ones.

"Tell me," she says.

"He died at sixty-three. Alone. In his recliner. And I was in Waterloo and I was drunk and I didn't — I couldn't—"

"But that's not this."

"No."

"This is the one where Diane called 911. This is the one where you drove eighty-seven miles an hour—"

"You don't know how fast I was driving."

"I know you." She presses closer. "He's alive, Nate. He's alive because of choices. His. Diane's. Yours. This is the version where people stay."

Hazard's tail thumps once against the carpet. Agreement.

---

Frank comes home three days later. He's on medication — blood thinners, a statin, something for blood pressure. Diane has reorganized the kitchen: olive oil instead of butter, whole grains instead of white bread, vegetables in the crisper that Frank regards with suspicion.

"What's this?" he says, holding up a purple vegetable.

"Eggplant," Diane says.

"Why?"

"Because Dr. Reeves said—"

"The child doctor."

"Frank."

He puts the eggplant down. Looks at it like it has personally wronged him. But he eats it that night — roasted with garlic and herbs, because Diane is a good cook and even eggplant can be tolerable in the right hands.

I come for dinner twice a week. Tuesday and Sunday. Elena comes Sunday. Sophie comes whenever she can. We fill the house with noise and bodies and Frank sits at the head of the table and complains about the food and asks for seconds and I think: *This. This is what I came back for.*

Not the shelters. Not the seed vaults. Not the atmospheric data or the satellite tracking or the forty-three sites across twelve states. Those matter. Those will save lives. But this — this table, these people, this man cutting eggplant with a suspicious fork — this is what I came back for.

---

Fall. The leaves turn. NEORAN activates Phase Three protocols — full communication network testing. Sophie runs it. Three days of rolling drills across all forty-three sites. Satellite uplinks confirmed. Ham radio networks confirmed. Hardened fiber confirmed.

"Ninety-six percent response rate within target window," Sophie reports. "Four sites had equipment issues. Already addressed."

"Good."

"Also, Dad called during the drill. He wants to know if we're coming for Thanksgiving."

"Obviously."

"He also wants to know what NEORAN actually does."

I pause. "What did you tell him?"

"Disaster preparedness nonprofit. Same as always."

"And he bought it?"

"He's never bought it, Nate. He just doesn't push."

She's right. Frank has never pushed. Not about the nonprofit that grew too fast. Not about the late nights. Not about the conversations that go quiet when he walks into the room. He watches. He waits. He trusts that his son will tell him when it's time.

And it's almost time.

---

Winter. December through February — the cold months, the planning months. Chen publishes her second paper. This one is bolder: *On the Anomalous Deceleration of Seven Outer Solar System Objects: Implications for Non-Natural Origin.* Co-authored with Nakamura and Reeves at NOAA.

The Post journalist finally gets through. The article runs on page six of the science section. The headline is cautious: *Physicists Observe Unusual Behavior in Deep Space Objects.* But the data is there. Anyone who reads carefully can see it.

No one reads carefully. The story gets twelve hundred shares on social media. A few comments about aliens. A few jokes. Then it's gone, buried under the news cycle.

"They'll pay attention eventually," Chen says.

"When?"

"When it's obvious."

"By then—"

"I know." She takes off her glasses. Rubs her eyes. "I know."

---

March. Frank's six-month follow-up. The stent is holding. Labs are good. Blood pressure down. Weight down eight pounds — the eggplant is working.

"Clean bill," Frank says, calling me from the hospital parking lot. "Your mother can stop hovering."

"She'll never stop hovering."

"No." A pause. Fond. "No, she won't."

He sounds good. Strong. The fear has faded — not gone, never fully gone after your heart reminds you it can stop — but faded. He's walking every morning. Two miles around the neighborhood. Hazard goes with him sometimes, when I bring the dog over, and they make a pair: the retired pipe fitter and the aging mutt, both of them slower than they used to be, both of them still moving.

I watch them from the kitchen window one morning. Frank talking to the dog, Hazard's tail wagging, the spring light coming in sideways through the budding trees.

Anyway. The point is—

No. Not yet.

The point is that my father is alive. And I'm going to tell him everything.

---

April. I call Sophie.

"I'm going to tell Dad."

Silence. Then: "Tell him what, exactly?"

"Everything. The first timeline. The displacement. Why NEORAN exists. What's coming."

"Nate." A breath. "He's sixty-four. He just had a heart attack."

"He's recovered. And he deserves the truth."

"I'm not arguing he doesn't deserve it. I'm asking if this is the right time."

"There's no right time. There's only the time you choose."

Sophie is quiet for a long moment. I can hear her thinking — the particular quality of her silence when she's running scenarios.

"Do you want me there?"

"No. Just me and him."

"Okay." Another pause. "Be gentle."

"I will."

"He's tougher than we think, you know."

"I know."

"But also. Be gentle."

"Yeah."

---

I pick the day of his follow-up appointment. The six-month check. He'll be at the hospital anyway. Good news from the doctor — stent holding, labs clean. He'll be in a good mood. Relaxed. And I'll be there, because I always drive him now. Since the heart attack, I drive him to every appointment. Frank complains about this. Frank also never says no.

The appointment is April 17th.

I spend the two weeks before drafting what I'll say. Writing it down, crossing it out, starting over. There's no script for this. No playbook for: *Dad, I lived another life before this one. I was a different person. A worse person. You died and I wasn't there and I came back and I stayed.*

But the words don't have to be perfect. They just have to be true.

April 17th. Morning. Clear sky. I drive to Frank's house. He's waiting on the porch, coffee in hand, wearing the flannel jacket Diane bought him last Christmas.

"You're early," he says.

"Traffic."

"There's no traffic between here and your house."

"Got me."

He gets in. We drive. The radio plays classic rock — Tom Petty, which Frank tolerates. Thirty minutes to Mercy General. We talk about nothing: the Hawkeyes' chances next season, the leak in the garage roof, whether Hazard is getting fat.

"He's not fat," I say. "He's sturdy."

"He's round, bud."

"He's a lab mix. They're supposed to be—"

"Round?"

"Sturdy."

Frank laughs. It's a good sound. A sound I want to bottle and keep.

---

The appointment goes well. Dr. Reeves — the child doctor, as Frank insists — reviews his labs, checks the stent imaging, pronounces him in excellent shape for a man of his age and history.

"Keep doing what you're doing," she says. "Walking. Diet. Medication."

"The eggplant?"

"Is it working?"

"Apparently."

"Then yes. The eggplant."

Frank shakes her hand. We walk out. Through the lobby, past the gift shop, through the automatic doors into the late afternoon.

The parking lot is half empty. Late in the day — most appointments done. The light is going golden. That particular spring evening light that makes everything look like a photograph.

Frank's truck is in the far row. We walk toward it. He's moving well — no shortness of breath, no hesitation. Just a man walking to his truck after a good doctor's visit.

He pulls out his keys. Unlocks the door. Doesn't get in. Leans against it instead, the way he does — arms crossed, looking out at nothing in particular.

"Good news," he says.

"Yeah."

"Your mother'll be relieved."

"She will."

He's still leaning. Still looking. And something in the way he's standing — patient, unhurried, present — tells me: now. This is the moment. He's been waiting.

"Dad."

"Yeah, bud."

"I need to tell you something."

He looks at me. Steady. Those pale blue eyes that don't miss anything, that have been watching me for eighteen years, that saw me change from a drinking teenager to whatever I am now and never asked why. Never pushed. Just watched.

"Okay," he says.

And I tell him everything.
