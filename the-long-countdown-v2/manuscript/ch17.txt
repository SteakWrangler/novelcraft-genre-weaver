The apartment is quieter than it should be.

Not silent. Hazard's nails click on the hardwood when he follows me from room to room, which he does now, constantly, like he's assigned himself a shift and the shift is me. Gerald sits on the back of the couch and watches the front door with the unblinking focus of a creature conducting an experiment. He watched it for three days straight after Elena left. On the fourth day, he moved to the kitchen counter and knocked her coffee mug off the edge. The blue one. The one with the chip in the handle that her mother gave her. It shattered on the tile and I swept it up and threw it away and then stood there with my hands on the counter for a long time because I understood, in some wordless way, that the cat had just decided she wasn't coming back.

That was two weeks ago. Or three. The days have a quality now. They blur at the edges, run together like watercolors left in the rain. I go to work. I come home. The space between is a smear of competence that requires no participation from whatever part of me used to be here.

I go to work. I come home. I feed the animals. I run the numbers on a supply chain contract for a municipality outside Cleveland. I answer Chen's emails with the minimum number of words that still qualify as communication. I eat something, usually standing over the sink, usually cold, usually whatever requires the least number of steps between the refrigerator and my mouth. I sleep on my side of the bed, which is a stupid thing to do when the whole bed is empty, but the body has its habits and the body is the last organ to receive bad news.

Her shampoo is still in the shower. I haven't moved it. I haven't opened it to smell it either. I'm not that far gone. But I know it's there the way you know a bruise is there — you don't have to press it to remember.

The closet is half-empty. She came Saturday while I was out, as she asked. Took her clothes, her books from the nightstand, the framed photo of her parents from the dresser. Left everything else. The apartment looks like a diagram of absence — the shapes of what's missing more visible than what remains.

---

It's a Tuesday when I go to the bar.

Not my bar. I don't have a bar anymore, haven't had one in years. The last bar I went to was in Iowa City in 2011, a night I don't remember clearly, which is its own indictment. This is just a place on Ninth Street with a green neon sign that says CASEY'S in a font that was probably stylish in 1974. I've walked past it a hundred times on the way to the hardware store. Tonight I walk in.

The reason is simple and stupid: the apartment is too quiet and the silence has a texture tonight, something thick and woolly that sits on my chest like a physical weight, and I need to be around noise that isn't mine. I need to be in a room where people are talking to each other and nobody is talking to me and I can exist as a shape on a stool without anyone asking if I'm okay. That's it. That's the whole reason.

I tell myself this as I push through the door. I tell myself this as I sit at the far end of the bar, away from the two guys watching a Cavs game on the mounted TV. I tell myself this as the bartender walks toward me with the easy unhurried gait of a woman who has walked toward ten thousand men on ten thousand Tuesday nights and can sort them into categories before she reaches the taps.

She's maybe fifty. Short gray hair, reading glasses pushed up on her forehead like she was doing the crossword in a back booth before I came in. She looks at me the way bartenders look at everyone — a quick assessment, a sorting. Type of guy. Type of night. What does this one need.

She sets a glass on a napkin and pours two fingers of whiskey without asking. I must look like the kind of man who drinks whiskey on a Tuesday. She's not wrong. I was that man for a long time. In another timeline, I still am.

"Thanks," I say, which is reflex, the way you thank someone for handing you a grenade. She nods and moves down the bar.

The glass sits in front of me.

It's bourbon. I can tell by the color — darker than Scotch, more red in the amber. The overhead light catches it and there's a whole sunset trapped in there, all the warm tones, all the gold. I know without tasting it that it's mid-shelf. Not the well stuff but nothing fancy. The bartender made a judgment call on me and landed in the middle and she's right about that too.

I put my hands on the bar. The wood is scarred and slightly sticky in the way of all bars that have been bars for a long time. Someone carved initials near my left thumb. J.R. + somebody, but the second set of initials has been sanded down, either by time or intention. One half of a declaration that outlived whatever it was declaring.

The glass sits in front of me.

It's warm in here. The heating system is working too hard, the way it does in old buildings with old radiators that nobody's serviced since the Clinton administration. One of the guys at the other end laughs at something on the screen. The Cavs are losing. I don't know the score. I don't care about the score. I'm looking at the glass.

I could pick it up. It would be the easiest thing I've done in weeks. Easier than sleeping. Easier than answering the phone when Sophie calls with her specific questions and her careful concern. Easier than walking past the half-empty closet every morning and not opening the door to check if something has changed, as if closets refill themselves overnight, as if absence is a temporary condition that corrects without intervention.

I pick up the glass.

The weight of it. Two fingers of bourbon and a rocks glass and it weighs almost nothing. It weighs everything. It's warm from the room, body temperature almost, like something alive, and the smell rises — corn and oak and something like vanilla, something sweet hiding beneath the burn. I know what the burn feels like. The way it lands in the back of the throat first, then spreads downward. The warmth in the chest that isn't warmth, that's a lie the nervous system tells itself, but it's a good lie. The best lie. Better than any lie I ever told another person. The way the second sip is always easier than the first and the third is easier than that and by the fourth you're not sipping anymore, you're just lowering a level, and by the fifth the thing in your chest — the wound, the knowledge, the weight — goes quiet, finally quiet, and you can breathe for the first time in however long it's been since you last let yourself do this.

I hold the glass at eye level. The light through the bourbon turns the world amber. Everything looks better through bourbon. Warmer. Softer at the edges. The bar, the carved initials, the two guys watching the game — all of it rendered in gold.

I think about the party in Iowa City. Tyler Brennan's place. The Keystone Light that Marcus handed me because we were twenty-two and it was a party and it was what you did. I remember the can in my hand. Cold and wet with condensation. The beer was cheap and bad and none of that mattered because the thing in my chest — the wound, the knowledge, the clock — went quiet for the first time in years. I remember the relief. Physical, total, like setting down something so heavy your shoulders didn't remember what straight felt like. I remember thinking: *oh. This is what other people feel like all the time. This is what normal is.*

I remember waking on Marcus's couch the next morning. The ceiling spinning. The sour taste. Marcus in the doorway with a glass of water, not saying anything, just holding it out. His face doing something I didn't have the vocabulary for then. Concern. Not the sharp kind. The patient kind. The kind that already knows this isn't the last time.

I remember the months after that. The progression. Beer to liquor. Weekends to weekdays. The slow slide from "I deserve this" to "I need this" to "this is just who I am now" to waking up in places I couldn't account for with bruises I couldn't explain and time missing from my life like pages ripped from a book. I remember the version of myself that lived at the bottom of a bottle for the better part of two years and I remember what it cost to climb out and I remember that I nearly didn't.

I remember Sophie's voice on the phone: "Nate, I'm scared of you." She was twenty. I was twenty-six and drunk at two in the afternoon and I'd called her for reasons I couldn't articulate and she said those words and I hung up and sat in my apartment and looked at the empty bottles on the counter and thought about how strange it was that a man could know the end of the world was coming and still find a way to make his life smaller.

The bourbon smells like every promise I ever broke.

I set the glass down.

Not a slam. Not a dramatic gesture. I just set it down, the way you set down anything that's too heavy to hold anymore, and I push it six inches forward with my fingertips. Away. Six inches of scarred bar wood between my hand and the glass. My hand is shaking. I notice this the way you notice weather — as a fact, external, unrelated to will. The hand shakes. The glass sits. The six inches holds.

The bartender sees. She comes back. Doesn't hurry. Just arrives, the way bartenders do, like they materialize from the atmosphere of the bar itself.

"Coke," I say. "Please."

She picks up the bourbon without comment. Doesn't pour it out — sets it behind the bar, out of sight, gone. She reaches for the soda gun. The Coke comes in the same kind of glass, dark and fizzing, and she sets it on a fresh napkin.

"Rough night?" she says.

"Rough life."

She nods once. She's heard worse. She's heard better. She's heard everything a man can say at the end of a bar on a Tuesday night, and none of it surprises her anymore, and that's its own kind of mercy — being unremarkable in your damage. She goes back to the other end of the bar.

I drink the Coke. It's too sweet and the carbonation burns in a way that's nothing like bourbon and everything like being alive in a body that doesn't get what it wants. I drink it all. I order another one. I sit there for an hour, watching a basketball game I don't care about, surrounded by noise that isn't mine, and I don't drink the bourbon.

This is not a triumph. I want to be clear about that. A triumph is a thing you do from a position of strength. This is survival at the margin. This is a man sitting on a ledge who chose not to jump tonight, this once, and who will have to make the same choice again tomorrow and the day after that and every day for the rest of however long this goes on, because the choosing doesn't stop. The choosing is the thing. It doesn't get easier. It just becomes familiar.

I pay for both drinks. The bourbon and the two Cokes. I tip too much because the bartender didn't make it a thing and because not making it a thing is a kindness I can't articulate and don't want to try. I walk home in the cold. The streets are empty. Cedar Falls at eleven on a Tuesday night in December — nothing moving except the wind and one man walking home from a bar he didn't drink at.

Hazard meets me at the door. His whole body wags, tail to ears, the full-system response of a dog whose person has returned from the void. I sit on the kitchen floor with him and he puts his head on my knee and his weight settles against my leg and we stay there for a while. The tile is cold. My back is against the cabinet. The apartment is quiet but it's the right quiet now — not the thick wool quiet from before, but the thinner kind, the kind you can breathe through.

"Good boy," I say. It means something else. It means: I came home. I didn't disappear. I'm still here.

January passes. February. I work.

NEORAN is growing in the way that things grow when you feed them constantly and without joy — efficiently, relentlessly, with the brutal focus of a man who has nothing else to do with his hours. We land a contract with a regional hospital network to optimize their supply chain logistics. We bring on two new staff members. The books are healthy. The mission is sound. Everything is functioning as designed.

I am not functioning as designed. But I am functioning, and that's enough. That has to be enough.

The news starts to change in late February. There's a virus. China first, then Italy, then the numbers start climbing in a way that stops being ignorable. I've been waiting for this the way you wait for a bus you know is coming but can't remember the exact time — you know it's this stop, you know it's soon, you keep looking down the street.

My foreknowledge of COVID is vague. A pandemic. Respiratory. 2019 or 2020. In the first timeline, I was thirty and mostly drunk and the pandemic was a blur of closed bars and isolation that felt exactly like the isolation I was already practicing — just with government permission. I remember masks. I remember the death count on the news. I remember not much else because the bourbon ate those years the way it ate everything.

But NEORAN — NEORAN I built for exactly this. Not this disaster specifically. All of them. Supply chain resilience, communication redundancy, distribution logistics. The infrastructure is there because I put it there years ago, not because I knew the details of what was coming but because I knew something was always coming. The timeline guarantees catastrophe. The specifics are just variables.

The calls start in March. A county health department in Ohio that can't get N95 masks through normal channels. A city in Michigan whose testing site logistics are a catastrophe. A nonprofit in Pennsylvania trying to coordinate food distribution for quarantined families. They call because someone knows someone who knows someone who's heard of this organization in Cedar Falls that's good at making things work when things stop working.

I take every call. I work eighteen-hour days. I coordinate PPE shipments using the supplier relationships we built for natural disasters. I set up testing site logistics using the same frameworks we developed for hurricane evacuation routing. It's not what these tools were designed for and it works anyway, because the underlying problem is always the same: too many people need something and the systems that are supposed to deliver it have broken down and somebody has to build new ones fast.

The work fills the hours. That's what the work is for. I won't pretend otherwise. The work is a machine that converts grief into competence, and the conversion rate is excellent, and the people on the other end of the phone don't care about my motivation as long as the masks arrive and the logistics hold and the supply chain doesn't collapse.

Sophie calls twice a week. She's in Columbus, finishing her master's in public health, working remote like everyone else, and she's worried about me in the way that little sisters worry — aggressively, with specific questions designed to extract information whether you're willing to give it or not.

"Are you eating?"

"Yes."

"Real food?"

"Define real."

"Nate."

"I ate a sandwich. Today. With bread and everything."

"Are you sleeping?"

"Enough."

"Have you talked to anyone who isn't a client or a vendor?"

I pause too long on that one. She hears the pause.

"Nate. You can't just work."

"Watch me."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Silence on her end. I can hear her thinking. Sophie is twenty-four and finishing a graduate degree in the middle of a pandemic and somehow she has the bandwidth to worry about her older brother's emotional state, which says something about her character that I don't deserve.

"I love you," she says. "You know that."

"I know."

"Call me if you need anything. I mean it."

"I will."

I won't. We both know that. But the offer is the thing, and the thing matters, and she hangs up and I go back to the spreadsheet and the spreadsheet doesn't ask me if I'm eating.

---

Frank shows up on a Saturday in May.

He doesn't call first. He never calls first. Frank's communication style is physical — he communicates by arriving, by occupying space, by doing things to pipes and hinges and engines while the important stuff happens around the edges of the doing. He drives his truck down from the house in the suburbs with a toolbox in the bed and knocks on my door at nine in the morning.

When I open it, he's standing in the hallway in his work jacket and boots, holding a pipe wrench. He's sixty-two and looks it — the gray in his hair has won the war, and his knees pop when he bends, and his hands are scarred from four decades of pipe fitting. But his eyes are the same. Clear and quiet and seeing more than they let on.

"Kitchen faucet," he says.

I stare at him. "What?"

"Sophie said your kitchen faucet's leaking."

I don't remember telling Sophie about the faucet. I also don't remember the last time I talked to Sophie about anything domestic, which means it was probably during one of those calls where she's extracting information like a dental surgeon and I'm on autopilot, giving it up without noticing. She is her mother's daughter in this way — patient, methodical, relentless.

"Frank, it's a drip. It's fine."

He's already past me, into the apartment, boots on the hardwood. Hazard rushes him with the full-body joy reserved for people who always have dog treats in their jacket pockets, which Frank does. Frank drops a hand to scratch behind his ears, produces a biscuit from somewhere, and continues toward the kitchen without breaking stride.

He goes to the sink. Turns the handle. Watches. There's a drip. It's been dripping for a month. I've been putting a towel under it and wringing the towel out every few days and calling that a solution.

"Washer," he says, and opens his toolbox on the counter.

I lean against the doorframe and watch my father take apart my faucet. He works the way he's always worked — carefully, without hurry, his hands knowing what to do before his conscious mind tells them. Forty years of muscle memory. Forty years of understanding that things break and that broken things can be fixed if you know what to do and you have the right washer and you don't force it.

He doesn't look around the apartment. He doesn't comment on the dishes in the sink or the mail piled on the counter or the half-empty closet visible through the bedroom door. He noticed all of it the moment he walked in. Frank notices everything. But Frank is also a man who understands that noticing something and commenting on it are two different acts, and that sometimes the first one is enough.

He turns off the water under the sink. He disassembles the handle. He pulls out the old washer — cracked, degraded, done — and inspects it with the casual authority of a man assessing a familiar failure. He grunts. Rummages in his toolbox. He has the right size because Frank always has the right size. He carries a tackle box full of washers and gaskets and O-rings sorted by size in small plastic compartments, because Frank is a man who has solved this particular problem a thousand times and does not intend to be caught without the right part.

"Hand me that towel," he says, and I hand him the towel.

We're quiet for a while. He works. I watch. Gerald jumps on the counter to supervise and Frank moves him aside without comment, a gesture so gentle and unselfconscious that it says more about the man than any sentence could. Gerald resettles two feet away and stares at the proceedings with the critical intensity of a foreman who didn't authorize this repair.

Frank replaces the washer. Reassembles the handle. Turns the water back on. Runs the faucet. No drip. He nods once, satisfied. Starts putting his tools away. The toolbox closes with the same sound it's always made — a solid click, the sound of completion. Job done. Problem solved. Move to the next one.

He straightens up. Stretches his back with a series of pops I can hear from across the kitchen. Puts both hands on the edge of the sink and looks out the window for a moment — the parking lot, the alley behind the building, the brick wall of the next structure over. Not a view. Just a direction to look while you say something.

"She was good for you, bud," he says. Not looking at me.

My throat closes. A fist in my chest. I swallow.

"I know."

He turns around. Picks up the toolbox. Looks at me — one long look, the kind he's never been good at holding but holds anyway because he knows this moment requires it. In his eyes there's everything he's never been able to say. Everything he means but doesn't have the vocabulary for. Frank doesn't do feelings. Frank does faucets. But the faucet is the feeling. The showing up is the feeling. The driving across town with a pipe wrench on a Saturday morning because his son's sister told him something was dripping — that's the whole language, and I'm only now old enough to read it fluently.

"You need anything else?" he says.

"No. Thanks, Frank."

"Truck's making a noise. Might be the alternator. I'll come back next week, take a look at yours too."

My truck is fine. He knows it. I know he knows it. He'll come back next week anyway. He'll find something to fix.

"Sure," I say. "Yeah. Next week."

He leaves. The whole visit — forty minutes, maybe. The faucet doesn't drip. I stand at the sink and run the water and turn it off and it's tight, it's right, it holds. A thing that was broken, fixed. Not complicated. Not dramatic. Just a man with a wrench who showed up when it counted.

I run the water one more time. Turn it off. Tight.

Marcus calls on a Wednesday night. Late — eleven, maybe later. I'm on the couch with the laptop open to a spreadsheet I stopped working on an hour ago. The news is on with the volume low, which is all pandemic all the time now, the numbers scrolling across the bottom of the screen like a stock ticker measuring the daily cost of being alive on Earth in 2020.

"Hey," he says.

"Hey."

"I heard about Elena. I'm sorry."

I close the laptop. "Yeah."

Pause. I can hear him breathing. I can hear something in the background — music, maybe, or Gabe cooking. Marcus and Gabe got a place together in Portland last year. A bungalow with a yard. They sent me photos of the kitchen renovation. I was happy for them. I'm still happy for them. It just sits differently now — other people's happiness, when your own has packed a duffel bag and walked out the door.

"You want to talk about it?" Marcus says.

"Not yet."

"Okay." No pressure. No push. No follow-up question designed to pry me open. Just: okay. The single most respectful syllable in the English language, delivered by the only person who has never tried to fix me.

"I'm here when you do," he says.

"I know."

A beat. I can hear him thinking. Marcus and I have known each other for twelve years, and in that time we've developed the ability to communicate in silences — the length, the texture, the weight of what's not being said.

"Nate?"

"Yeah."

"You're not drinking."

It's not a question. It's not entirely a statement either. It lives in the space between — the space where Marcus has always lived with me, where he holds the line without pulling it tight. He's asking and not asking. He's checking and trusting. Both at once.

"No," I say. "I'm not drinking."

"Good." Simple. No elaboration. No congratulations, no concern, just: good. The word sits between us like a handshake. "Call me whenever. I mean it. Three a.m., I don't care."

"Okay."

"Okay. Goodnight, man."

"Goodnight."

I sit on the couch after he hangs up. The news murmurs. The death toll scrolls. Outside, Cedar Falls is quiet in the way all cities are quiet during lockdown — not peaceful, just paused. Held breath. Two hundred and thirty thousand people in this metro area, all sitting in their separate rooms, waiting for something to end.

I think about what Marcus said. *You're not drinking.* Two years since Elena left — no. Less than that. Months. Four months? Five? The blur again. But in all those months, the only time I came close was the Tuesday at Casey's, the bourbon on the bar, the six inches between my hand and the glass.

I'm not drinking.

Anyway. The point is—

It's June when I finally move the shampoo.

I don't throw it away. I put it under the sink, behind the cleaning supplies, where I don't have to see it every morning. Then I stand in the bathroom and look at the shelf where it was. The shelf is just a shelf now. Empty, a little dusty, a rectangle of clean tile where the bottle sat for five years. That's fine. That's what it is.

I clean the apartment. Not because I want to but because I've been living in the shell of something and shells aren't meant to be inhabited. I do the dishes — all of them, the ones that have been accumulating for days. I sort the mail. I take out three bags of recycling. I open the windows and the spring air comes through — warm, finally, after a winter that lasted decades — and the apartment smells like outside instead of like the interior life of a man who forgot that rooms need air.

Hazard follows me from room to room with his tail wagging, confused and pleased by the activity. Gerald watches from the bookshelf with the expression of a creature who has long suspected this apartment was below his standards and is gratified to see improvements.

I don't throw away the box. The prediction box, under the bed, Elena's neat rows still inside it. I don't open it either. The predictions in those sealed envelopes are either right or they're wrong and either way they're not what matters. They were never what mattered. The box is just information. Information without trust is just a more organized form of loneliness.

Night. Late.

I'm in bed. The streetlight makes its usual stripe across the ceiling. Hazard is on the floor beside me, breathing deep and steady, the rhythm of a dog who has finally stopped watching the door. Gerald is somewhere in the apartment — a drawer he's pried open, a shelf I've told him a hundred times is off-limits. The apartment is quiet but it's a different quiet than it was in December. Smaller. Manageable. The size of one man's life instead of the size of a missing woman.

I think about the first timeline.

Not the details. I try not to get lost in the details anymore because the details are a labyrinth I can wander forever and never come out. But the shape of it. The arc. In the first timeline, I'm twenty-eight, twenty-nine. My drinking is accelerating. My relationships are ash. I've burned through two jobs and I'm about to burn through a third. Sophie has stopped calling. Frank has stopped trying. Marcus is holding on by his fingernails, coming to my apartment with glasses of water and the patience of a man who hasn't yet decided to give up.

This is where I go down. This is the stretch — in the first timeline — where the spiral tightens. Where the drinking stops being recreational and becomes structural. Where I disappear into a version of myself that takes six more years to die and does it badly, alone, in an apartment full of empty bottles with an eviction notice on the counter and a sky that's turning gray.

That's the exit ramp. The long slow curve off the highway into whatever ditch is waiting.

In this timeline, I'm lying in bed in a clean apartment with a dog on the floor and a cat in a drawer and a company that's helping people survive a pandemic and a father who fixes faucets and a sister who calls twice a week and a best friend who said "I'm here when you do" and meant it.

In this timeline, I went to a bar and sat with a bourbon for an hour and put it down.

In this timeline, I'm alone. And the woman I love is gone because I couldn't tell her the truth. And the clock is still ticking — 2026, June, the Kessari, everything — and I still can't tell anyone who isn't Chen and I'm still carrying this weight that never gets lighter, it just becomes more familiar, the way a chronic injury becomes familiar, the way you stop noticing the limp until someone asks why you're walking like that.

But I'm here.

In the first timeline, this is where I disappeared for good. This is the exit ramp I always took.

This time I'm staying on the highway. I don't know where it goes. I used to think the foreknowledge was a map. That if I followed it carefully enough I'd arrive somewhere that justified everything — the secrecy, the isolation, the inability to be fully honest with the people who loved me. But it's not a map. It was never a map. It's a weather forecast for a storm I can see coming and can't stop and can barely describe to anyone who might help me prepare for it.

I'm staying on the highway. Not because I know where it goes. Not because I'm brave or hopeful or healed. Not because the whiskey at Casey's proved something or because Frank's wrench fixed something or because Marcus's phone call meant I'm okay. I'm staying because the alternative is the ditch, and I've lived in the ditch, and the ditch is worse than not knowing. The ditch is worse than being alone. The ditch is worse than everything except the thing that put me there, and the thing that put me there is me, and I'm choosing, tonight, to not be that version.

Tomorrow I'll have to choose again. And the day after that. And every day until the choosing becomes something other than white-knuckling, if it ever does, and maybe it doesn't, and maybe that's fine, and maybe surviving at the margin is still surviving.

I get up.

It's five-thirty. The sky through the bedroom window is gray — not dawn yet, but close, the thin gray that precedes it. Hazard lifts his head, ears forward, questioning. I pull on running shoes and a sweatshirt. He understands running shoes. He's at the door before I am, tail going, ready, and his readiness is simple and immediate and doesn't require anything from me except forward motion.

We go out. The streets are empty — lockdown empty, pandemic empty. The air is cool and smells like rain. Hazard pulls at the leash and I let him, let him set the pace, and we run. Not fast. Not far. Just forward.

I go to work.
