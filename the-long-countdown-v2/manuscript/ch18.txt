The permits should have been simple.

Three buildings on the east side of Cedar Falls — a decommissioned textile mill, a shuttered elementary school, and a warehouse that hadn't stored anything since 2014. NEORAN needed them retrofitted: reinforced basements, underground storage for water and medical supplies, communication arrays that could survive an EMP burst or, more specifically, the orbital electromagnetic interference that precedes a Kessari atmospheric breach, but I couldn't write that in the grant application, so we called it disaster preparedness and let the federal agencies fill in their own assumptions.

I'd been working the process for five months. Public comment sessions. Private meetings with council members. Impact studies, economic projections, job creation estimates. Chen had assembled a presentation so thorough it could've been submitted as a dissertation — seismic reinforcement specs, FEMA compliance matrices, cost-benefit analyses that showed the shelters paying for themselves in tax revenue within eighteen months. We had letters of support from the fire chief, the county emergency management director, and Colonel Jim Petersen, retired Army Corps of Engineers, who'd moved to Cedar Falls to fish and ended up volunteering with us three days a week because the man couldn't sit still and the trout weren't biting.

It didn't matter.

The council voted 4-3 against. Zoning concerns. Budgetary implications. Neighborhood character. Historic preservation considerations for the mill. The usual vocabulary of institutional inertia, which sounds reasonable in meeting minutes and means absolutely nothing when you're standing in the parking lot afterward watching the council members file out to their SUVs and knowing — with the particular nausea of someone who has already watched the ending — that the buildings they just protected from renovation will be rubble in five years.

"We'll appeal," Chen said, standing next to me in the parking lot. She had her tablet tucked under one arm, the presentation she'd spent three weeks building reduced to a prop. The December air was cold enough that our breath made small clouds between us. "There's a process. We file with the county planning commission, request an override based on federal emergency preparedness guidelines—"

"That takes fourteen months."

"Twelve, if we expedite."

"We don't have twelve months to waste on permits, Chen."

She looked at me. The parking lot lights caught her face, and I could see her calculating — not the timeline, but me. Three years of working together had taught her to read the difference between my frustration and my resolve, and right now she was seeing something in the latter category that she didn't like.

"Then we find another approach," she said carefully. "Other buildings. Other locations."

"There are no other buildings. We surveyed every viable structure in a thirty-mile radius. Those three are it. You know that."

She didn't argue. Just watched me for a moment longer — a long moment, long enough that I felt the weight of it — then nodded and walked to her car. The tablet was still under her arm. I stood in the parking lot until it was empty, and then I stood there a little longer, because the alternative was going home to the apartment where the silence had textures I was still learning the names of.

---

The fundraiser was Chen's idea. A gala, she called it, though in Cedar Falls a gala means a hotel ballroom with rented tablecloths and centerpieces donated by a florist who owed us a favor and a DJ who'd also done the high school prom three weeks earlier. But NEORAN had grown. The pandemic had done that — given us credibility that money couldn't buy. When COVID hit and our supply chains held, when our communication networks kept rural hospitals connected to county health departments that were drowning in their own bureaucracy, when our volunteer corps delivered medication to isolated elderly residents while everyone else was still arguing about masks, people noticed.

Donations quadrupled. Membership tripled. We had government contracts now, partnerships with three universities, a full-time staff of eleven. The scrappy little disaster-prep organization from Cedar Falls had become something legitimate. Something with weight.

The gala was meant to cement that. Show the donors where their money went. Make the case for the next phase of expansion. And, not incidentally, put social pressure on the council members we'd invited — let them see the community support in person, in formal wear, holding glasses of wine and writing checks and talking about what NEORAN had done for their neighborhoods during lockdown.

Four of the seven council members showed up. Including Richard Aldiss, who'd cast the deciding vote against our permits and who, at approximately 9:47 PM on the second Saturday of January 2021, stepped into the hallway outside the ballroom to take a phone call he should have taken in his car.

I was coming back from the bathroom. The hallway was that generic hotel corridor — patterned carpet in beige and burgundy, brass sconces that needed new bulbs, the faint smell of chlorine drifting up from the pool two floors below. Aldiss was standing near the emergency exit, phone pressed to his ear, back to me. He hadn't heard me come around the corner. The carpet was thick. My shoes made no sound.

"—can't keep calling me here. I told you, not when—" A pause. His voice dropped, but the acoustics in that hallway were unkind to secrets. The walls were close and the ceiling was low and sound carried with a clarity that the architect had not intended. "Linda doesn't know. Linda *can't* know, I've told you that. If this gets out before the election—"

I stopped walking.

"—no, listen to me. Listen. After November. I'll figure it out after November, I promise. But right now you have to—"

He shifted his weight. His free hand rubbed the back of his neck — a nervous gesture, a guilty gesture. I knew it. I'd made it myself, in another life, standing in bar parking lots in Waterloo talking to women whose names I'd forget by the weekend. The body language of a man negotiating the terms of his own betrayal.

"—I love you too. I know. I know it's hard. Just — give me until November. That's all I'm asking."

He hung up. Stood there for a moment with his hand still on the back of his neck, head slightly bowed. A man in a hotel hallway, alone with a secret he thought was private. I knew what that felt like. I knew exactly what that felt like.

Then he turned around and I was already walking toward him, casual, unhurried, a man heading back to the party with nothing on his mind except the open bar and the DJ's questionable song selection.

"Aldiss. Good to see you came out tonight."

"Callahan." He pocketed his phone with the practiced ease of a man who'd been pocketing that phone all year. His smile snapped into place — the campaign smile, the council-meeting smile, the one that showed teeth but not intention. "Hell of an event. Your people do good work."

"We do our best."

We walked back to the ballroom together. He talked about the centerpieces. I said something about the turnout. We shook hands at the door — firm, professional, two men who understood each other less than they thought — and he went to find Linda, and I went to find a corner where I could stand with a glass of club soda and think about what I'd just heard, and what it meant, and what the old machinery in my chest was already doing with it.

---

I should have let it go.

I know that. I knew it then, in the rational part of my mind, the part that had spent ten years learning to be someone better than the man who came back from the first timeline. I should have walked back into the ballroom and danced badly and shaken hands and gone home and let the permits work their way through the appeal process and let Richard Aldiss's marriage be Richard Aldiss's business.

But.

The thing about the old machinery is that it never actually breaks down. You can leave it idle for years. You can pile good intentions on top of it, bury it under therapy insights and hard-won sobriety and the memory of Elena's face when she said *you lied like I was someone you had to manage*. You can build a whole new life on top of it, a good life, a life that helps people. It's still there. Under everything. Oiled and patient and ready. And all it needs is a reason.

Three buildings. One mill, one school, one warehouse. Four hundred people, two-sixty, and a regional command center. The combined capacity to shelter nearly a thousand people, to coordinate communication across the county, to store enough medical supplies and water to keep a population alive through whatever comes after the sky changes color and the plants start dying. One thousand people who will survive because of those buildings. One thousand people who won't, if the buildings stay empty and unmodified and the permits sit in an appeal queue for fourteen months while the clock grinds toward 2026.

That was the reason.

I sat in my office the next morning — the real office now, not Chen's garage, not the folding table. Second floor of the old First Federal Bank building, converted to commercial space three years ago. My desk had a computer on it and a framed photo of the NEORAN team from last Christmas and a coffee mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST DISASTER PREPAREDNESS COORDINATOR, which Sophie had given me as a joke that landed so accurately it stopped being funny.

I sat there and I thought about Richard Aldiss and his phone call and his hand on the back of his neck and the specific tone of voice a man uses when he's telling someone he loves them in a hallway where he shouldn't be saying it.

The math was simple. Aldiss was the swing vote. He'd voted against us as a favor to a developer named Garrett Lynch who wanted the warehouse site for luxury condos — not because Aldiss cared about zoning or historic preservation or neighborhood character. He cared about Lynch's campaign contributions. The principle was hollow. Which meant the resistance was shallow. Which meant it wouldn't take much pressure to reverse it.

Aldiss had a secret. The secret, revealed, would end his marriage and his reelection campaign. Therefore, Aldiss would do quite a lot to keep the secret. Including, for instance, changing a vote that he'd only cast for cynical reasons in the first place.

The math was simple. The math is always simple. That's the seduction of it — the reduction of human suffering into variables, the frictionless algebra of leverage. I knew this math. I'd spent my first life doing this math, at Meridian, at bars, in every room I'd ever entered where the goal was to find the angle and work it until the other person gave you what you wanted. I'd been good at it. I'd been very, very good at it.

I'd also been a person no one trusted, no one loved, and no one mourned when I disappeared into a bottle for two years. There was a connection between these facts.

I thought about Elena. Not on purpose — but she surfaced anyway, the way she always did when I was standing at the edge of something. Her voice in my head, not saying anything specific. Just the quality of her silence. The way she watched me when I was making a decision, as if she could see the gears turning and was waiting to find out which version of me would walk out of the calculation.

She would have stopped me. Not with a lecture. Elena never lectured. She would have just been there, being the person I'd have to look at afterward, and that would have been enough. The witness. The accountability. The love that cost me the option of being who I used to be.

But she wasn't there. She'd been gone for over a year. The apartment was quiet. The permits were denied. The clock was ticking. And the old machinery was running, smooth and quiet, the way it always ran when the justification was good enough.

I picked up my phone.

---

I called Aldiss on a Tuesday. Asked him to meet for coffee. He suggested the diner on Fifth Street — public, casual, safe — and I said I'd prefer somewhere private. There was a silence on his end that lasted four seconds. Four seconds is a long time on a phone call. In those four seconds, I heard him understand something he couldn't yet articulate. The shape of what was coming.

"My office," he said. "After six."

We met at his district office at six-thirty. The building was a converted storefront on Main Street — glass front, venetian blinds half-drawn, the word ALDISS on the door in gold letters that were starting to peel. He let me in through the back. The office was the kind that small-city elected officials always have: too many flags in the corner, framed photos with constituents on every surface, a desk that was slightly too large for the room it was in. He sat behind it and I sat across from it and for a moment we were just two men in a room with bad fluorescent lighting, and then I told him what I'd heard.

I didn't embellish. Didn't threaten. Didn't frame it as leverage, exactly. I just laid out the facts with the same precision I used in grant applications and compliance filings — what I'd overheard, what it implied, what the consequences would be if it became public knowledge before November. I was calm. Professional, even. The voice I used was the voice of a man presenting options, and the options were real, and the weight between them was deliberately asymmetric.

And then I laid out the alternative: a changed vote, three permits approved, a community that would be safer for it, and a conversation that would never leave this room.

Aldiss sat very still while I talked. He was fifty-three, going soft around the middle, with the kind of face that looked trustworthy on campaign signs — broad, open, the face of a man who coaches Little League and goes to First Baptist every Sunday with Linda and their two kids. A face built for trust. The kind of face that makes betrayal feel like a personal insult to the universe.

"You're blackmailing me," he said when I finished.

"I'm presenting options."

"You're blackmailing me, Callahan. Call it what it is."

I didn't argue with him. There was no point. We both knew what this was. The vocabulary didn't change the mechanics. I could have said *I'm giving you an opportunity to do the right thing* and it would have been technically true and we both would have known it was a lie, and I'd already lied to enough people this decade.

"The permits," I said. "That's all I want. The permits get approved, this conversation didn't happen. Your personal life stays personal. The shelters get built. People are safer. Everyone wins."

"Everyone wins." He said it flat. Like the words tasted wrong.

He stared at me for a long time. I could see him running his own calculations — the same kind I'd run, but from the other side. The weight of exposure versus the weight of compliance. The cost of fighting versus the cost of folding. His marriage. His kids. His career. All of it balanced against three building permits he'd voted against for money, not principle.

He wasn't a bad man. I want to note that. He was a man with a weakness and I was a man with a lever, and the distance between those two things is smaller than anyone who hasn't held the lever wants to believe.

"The permits," he said finally. His voice was scraped clean. No affect. "That's it."

"That's it."

"And this stays between us. Forever."

"Between us."

He nodded once. I stood up. We didn't shake hands. I walked out of his office and down the narrow staircase and through the back door into the alley where my truck was parked under a light that buzzed and flickered and cast orange shadows on the asphalt. I sat in the driver's seat for ten minutes before I started the engine.

I felt the old machinery click off. Not the slow wind-down of something powering itself into standby. The abrupt stop of something that had completed its task. Job done. Target acquired. Leverage applied. Outcome achieved. The efficiency of amorality. The clean, cold satisfaction of a system operating exactly as designed.

I felt nothing.

And then, driving home, I felt everything.

The permits were approved the following Tuesday. 5-2. Aldiss changed his vote and brought Morales with him — some cover story about new information from a federal compliance review that made the project more attractive. Chen called me from the council chamber. I could hear people clapping in the background.

"We got it, Nate. We got all three. The mill, the school, the warehouse. All of it."

"That's great," I said. I was standing in my kitchen. The coffee mug — WORLD'S OKAYEST — was in my hand. The coffee in it was cold.

"What did you do?"

The question hung in the air. Direct. Unambiguous. Chen didn't do ambiguity.

"What do you mean?"

"Nate. Last week Aldiss was publicly committed to blocking us. He called us a waste of taxpayer resources in the Gazette. Now he's championing the project and brought Morales along. What did you do?"

"I had a conversation with him. Presented the case again. Sometimes people just need time to process."

Silence on her end. Long silence. Chen was not a stupid woman. Chen was the furthest thing from a stupid woman I'd ever encountered, and she'd spent three years watching me operate, and she knew what my conversations sounded like when they produced results this clean and this immediate.

"Okay," she said finally. One word. In it, I could hear the calculation — the weighing of method against outcome. The decision to not look too closely at a gift that gave her what she needed. The same calculation I'd made in my office, from a different angle.

We were both choosing not to ask the question. We were both choosing the shelters over the principle.

"I'll get the contractors scheduled," she said. "We can break ground on the mill by March."

"Good. That's good."

"Nate."

"Yeah."

A pause. "Thank you." She said it simply and she hung up, and I stood in my kitchen with cold coffee and looked at nothing and felt the specific gravity of having won something at a cost I couldn't calculate yet.

We broke ground on the mill two weeks later.

---

Marcus figured it out in September.

I should have predicted it. Marcus is an engineer. His professional existence is organized around identifying load-bearing failures, tracing systems to their root causes, finding the single point where stress exceeds capacity. He designs bridges that don't collapse. He spots the weld that's going to fail before it fails. He reads systems the way other people read sentences — structurally, causally, with an eye for what's holding the weight and what's about to give.

He does this with infrastructure. He does it with people too, when he pays attention.

What happened was this: Dave Reinhardt, who worked in the county planning office and played in Marcus's Tuesday night softball league, mentioned over post-game beers that something strange had happened with the NEORAN permits. The timeline was irregular. The reversal was sudden. No new documentation had been submitted, no additional environmental review completed, just a clean 180 from Aldiss with a thin procedural justification that nobody in the planning office believed.

Dave wasn't suspicious — he was impressed. "Your buddy Callahan must be one hell of a salesman," he said to Marcus, and laughed, and ordered another beer.

Marcus didn't laugh. Marcus called around. He asked questions in the careful, methodical way he asked questions about everything — the structural engineer's approach, where you test each assumption sequentially until you find the one that doesn't hold. He called people who knew Aldiss. He called people in the planning office. He asked when I'd met with Aldiss and what the meeting was about and he cross-referenced the timeline and he arrived at the answer the way you'd solve a load calculation — by process of elimination.

He came to my apartment on a Saturday afternoon in September. The leaves were turning. It was still warm enough to have the windows open and a breeze was coming through the kitchen that smelled like fall — woodsmoke and dry leaves and something cooling in the air.

I opened the door and he was standing there in jeans and a flannel shirt, hands in his pockets, and the look on his face was one I'd never seen directed at me before. Not anger. Anger I could have handled. Anger has energy in it — heat, motion, something you can engage with. This was something else. This was the face of a man who was tired of something he couldn't name, and had just found a name for it.

"Can I come in?"

"Yeah. Of course."

He came in. Looked around the apartment the way he always did — the architectural assessment, the structural scan. Everything was clean. The dishes were done. The counters were wiped. The whiskey bottle that Frank had given me two Christmases ago was still unopened on the shelf, gathering dust like a small amber memorial to choices I was making one day at a time.

He sat on the couch. I sat in the chair across from him. The arrangement felt deliberate. Like we'd set the furniture for a conversation that needed facing, not adjacency.

"Dave Reinhardt told me something about Aldiss," Marcus said. "About the permits."

"What did he say?"

"Enough." Marcus rubbed his face with both hands — a gesture so weighted with fatigue that it aged him while I watched. "I made some calls. I put it together. You want to tell me what you did, or do you want me to tell you?"

I could have lied. I thought about it. The machinery was right there, offering templates — a plausible narrative, delivered with the right cadence, enough detail to satisfy without inviting further questions. I'd done it a hundred times. A thousand times, in my first life.

But this was Marcus. Marcus who handed me a Keystone Light at a college party and then handed me glasses of water for two years while I tried to drown myself. Marcus who said *I'm here when you do* and meant it in a way that didn't have conditions or expiration dates. Marcus who'd been the first person in this timeline to see me clearly and decide I was worth seeing.

"I leveraged him," I said. "He had something he didn't want public. I used it."

Marcus nodded. Not surprised. Confirming. The structural engineer, verifying his calculation.

"What did he have?"

"That's between me and him."

"Jesus, Nate."

"The permits were going to save lives. The permits *are* going to save lives. Those shelters—"

"I know what the shelters do. I helped design the reinforcement specs."

"Then you understand why—"

"I understand that you blackmailed a city council member." He said it quietly. That was the worst part — the quietness. The lack of heat. The last time Marcus raised his voice at me was eight years ago, when I showed up drunk to his birthday dinner and made a scene. The yelling was easier. Yelling is engaged. Yelling is a form of caring. This quiet was something else. This quiet was a man reassessing the foundations.

"I understand that the guy who sat in my living room three years ago — the guy who told me he was done operating like that, done with the angles, done being the smartest guy in any room who also happened to be the most corrosive — that guy found the angle and worked the leverage. Same as always."

"This was different."

"That's who you were." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, looking at me with an intensity that had nothing to do with anger. "I thought you changed."

The words landed like a door closing. Not a slam. A click. A latch engaging. Something that had been open, now wasn't.

"Marcus—"

"I'm not going to lecture you." He stood up. The fatigue was in every line of his body — shoulders, hands, the way he moved toward the door like the air in the room had thickened. "You're thirty. You've been through things I don't pretend to understand. And maybe you're right. Maybe the shelters justify it. I'm an engineer, not a philosopher. I don't know where that line is."

He stopped at the door. Hand on the knob. Looking at the floor, then at me.

"But I'm tired of being the friend who finds out afterward. I'm tired of watching you decide that the right outcome justifies the wrong method. I thought — after Elena, after everything — I thought you'd hit a wall and the wall had changed something." He turned the knob. "The friendship isn't over. That's not what this is. But something cracked. I can feel it. Can you?"

"Yeah," I said. "I can feel it."

He left. The door closed softly behind him. Not a slam. Nothing dramatic. We were thirty years old and we'd learned by now that relationships don't end in explosions — they erode. A millimeter at a time. Imperceptible until you step back and measure and realize the gap is wider than you thought.

I sat in the chair for a long time after he left. The autumn air through the open window. The smell of woodsmoke. The whiskey on the shelf. Elena gone. Marcus cracked. And the old machinery, cooling now, satisfied, having delivered exactly what it promised to deliver — the permits, the shelters, the outcome.

The outcome was correct. The shelters would save lives. The math was sound. The cost was... what? A friendship slightly damaged? A council member doing the right thing for the wrong reason? A set of tools I'd promised to leave in the drawer, taken out and used one more time because the need was real?

I'd always told myself the drinking was the danger. The thing that could pull me under. But sitting there in the silence, listening to the sound of Marcus's car pulling away, I understood something I'd been circling for years without landing on: the drinking was a symptom. A numbing agent. The real pathology — the reading of people, the identification of pressure points, the frictionless conversion of human weakness into personal advantage — that was the engine. The drinking was just what I did when the engine ran too hot and I needed to cool the shame.

And I'd just run it again.

The shelters would save lives.

That's what I told myself. That's what I still tell myself. I don't know if it's enough. I don't know if the math actually works that way — if you can offset the cost of becoming someone by counting the people you helped while you were being them.

Anyway. The point is—

Winter. The mill renovation was on schedule. Chen hired a contractor who'd done federal bunker work, and the basement was being reinforced with poured concrete and steel rebar to specifications that would survive anything short of a direct strike. Construction crews worked through November and December, pouring foundations in the cold, framing interior walls while the snow fell outside.

I went to the site twice a week. Walked the floors. Watched it take shape. The mill was beautiful in a way I hadn't expected — the old brick and timber frame absorbing the new infrastructure like a body accepting a transplant. It would hold four hundred people. It would keep them alive.

Marcus and I still talked. Still had dinner once a month. He came to the mill site in October and walked through the construction with his professional eye, pointing out details, suggesting modifications to the ventilation system. The expertise came through — he couldn't help it; he was an engineer, and engineering was a reflex that overrode personal grievance. But there was a distance that hadn't been there before. A half-beat of hesitation before he laughed at my jokes. A careful neutrality when NEORAN came up in conversation. The trust account — Elena's phrase, which I couldn't stop thinking in — was depleted. Not empty. Depleted. Running on reserves.

December. Sophie came for Christmas. Twenty-five now, almost done with her master's, applying for jobs at health departments and community organizations. She stayed for four days in the guest room that used to be Elena's office and we cooked badly together and watched movies and she asked me once, carefully, how I was doing, and I said fine, and she looked at me with our mother's eyes and let it go.

I didn't tell her about Aldiss.

January 2021.

The hum started on a Thursday.

I heard about it from Frank first. He called in the evening, which was unusual — our rhythm was Saturday mornings, brief and functional, the communication pattern of men who've been through something together and don't have the words for it but have the habit, and the habit is enough.

"You hear anything strange at night?" he asked.

"Strange how?"

"Like a hum. Low. Under everything." A pause. The sound of Frank thinking, which was the sound of silence because Frank thought without narrating. "Dogs on my street have been going nuts. Howling at three, four in the morning. Every night this week. Sally Peterman's German shepherd nearly broke through her fence last night."

"Could be a gas line. Or electrical interference from the new substation."

"Could be," he said, in a tone that meant he'd already ruled those out and was calling me for a different reason. A reason he couldn't articulate but that lived in the space between us — the space where Frank knew, without knowing how he knew, that his son would have an answer.

I didn't have an answer I could give him.

"Probably geological," I said. "Micro-seismic. The county's been doing that survey work near the ridge."

"Mm." Frank's thinking sound. "Let me know if you hear it."

"I will."

I hung up and went to the kitchen window and looked out at Cedar Falls in the January dark — the downtown lights, the ridge beyond, the clear cold sky. I listened.

Nothing. Not yet.

But over the next two weeks, the reports multiplied. The Cedar Falls Gazette ran a piece — front page of the local section, below the fold — about residents in three neighborhoods complaining of sleeplessness. A persistent low-frequency sound, they said. Too low to identify, too constant to ignore. The kind of sound you felt more than heard. The kind that lived in your chest and your jaw and the backs of your eyes. The city sent inspectors to the water treatment plant, the electrical grid, the gas mains. Everything was normal. The utility companies issued statements. The county environmental officer filed a report. Normal, normal, normal.

A physics professor at the community college — Dr. Sarah Wynn, environmental acoustics — measured it with equipment she borrowed from the university's geology department. 14.2 hertz. Below the threshold of conscious hearing for most people, but within the range the human body registers as unease. Infrasound. The frequency at which the human chest cavity resonates, producing a sense of dread that has no identifiable source. In her professional opinion, published as an op-ed in the Gazette, it was likely geological. Subsurface activity. Perhaps related to the fracking operations forty miles south. Nothing dangerous. Nothing to worry about.

It wasn't geological.

Hazard knew. He started sleeping by the front door instead of beside my bed — alert, ears swiveling, making small sounds in his throat that weren't quite whines. Gerald became nocturnal in a way he hadn't been before, pacing the apartment at 3 AM, yowling at nothing visible. Animals know. They don't have the cognitive architecture to lie to themselves, to rationalize a sound into something manageable. They just hear it and they respond with the only vocabulary they have — howling, pacing, the pressed-flat ears of a creature that senses a predator too large to fight or flee.

February. The hum spread. Reports from neighboring towns — Worthington, Mapleton, communities thirty miles out. A trucker on I-90 called a local radio station and said he'd felt his steering wheel vibrating at 3 AM on an empty highway. No wind, no construction, no traffic — just a low steady pulse coming from everywhere and nowhere, like the road itself was thrumming. The radio host made a joke about aliens. The studio audience laughed. The trucker didn't.

I didn't either. I lay in bed at night and I listened. And sometime in late February — a Thursday, I think, or maybe a Friday, the kind of night where the air is cold enough that sound carries differently and the whole town is still — I heard it.

Not with my ears. With my chest. A vibration in the cavity behind my sternum, deep and continuous, like someone had struck a tuning fork the size of a building and pressed it against the bedrock beneath the city. It wasn't loud. It wasn't even a sound, exactly. It was a presence. A frequency. Something in the air that hadn't been there before and was now, and would be from now on, and would only get louder.

I knew what it was.

Not the technical details — I didn't have those, had never had those. In the first timeline, I was drunk and alone and the hum had started and I'd assumed it was my hangover, and then everyone started hearing it, and by then there were other things to worry about. The mechanism was beyond me. But I knew what it meant.

The atmosphere was being tested. Probed. Low-frequency pulses transmitted through the ionosphere, bouncing off the planetary surface, mapping density and composition. Acoustic topography. The way you'd tap a wall before you drilled into it. The way a doctor taps your chest and listens to the resonance.

Reconnaissance.

The Kessari were still years away. The main body, the arrival, the event that ends everything I know — that was 2026. Five years. But their instruments were long and their patience was absolute and the hum was the sound of something very far away doing its preliminary work. Testing the medium. Calibrating. Taking measurements.

I lay in bed and listened. The vibration in my chest. The distant howling of a dog — not Hazard; three blocks away, maybe four — answering something it couldn't see. Another dog joined it, farther off. Then a third. A chain of howling stretching across Cedar Falls, across the ridge, into the dark beyond, dogs calling to dogs calling to dogs, a network of alarm that no one was listening to because it was just dogs being dogs. Except it wasn't. It was never just that.

The sound was familiar. Not from Cedar Falls. Not from this life. From the other one — the first timeline, the one I'd lived and lost and been sent back from. In those final weeks before the end, the hum had been constant. A presence in the air like humidity. People stopped mentioning it the way they stopped mentioning gravity — it was simply there, part of the conditions of being alive in those last days, until it wasn't a hum anymore but a roar, and then it wasn't a roar but a silence, and then it wasn't anything because we weren't there to hear it.

I'd heard this before. I was hearing it again. Earlier than last time — years earlier. Which meant either the timeline had shifted, or I'd been too drunk in the first life to notice when it started. Probably the second. Probably the hum had always started this early and I'd just been too far gone to register it.

It didn't matter. It was here now. The advance signal. The first audible evidence that the clock I'd been carrying in my head for thirteen years was not metaphor, not abstraction, not the delusion of a man who'd convinced himself the world was ending. It was real. It was measurable. Dr. Sarah Wynn had measured it with borrowed equipment and published a number — 14.2 hertz — and that number was the sound of something coming.

I closed my eyes. I didn't sleep. The hum pressed against my ribs and I lay there and thought about permits and shelters and the old machinery inside me and Marcus's face in my apartment and the word *cracked* and whether, when the time came, the things I'd built would matter more than the things I'd broken to build them.

Outside, a dog howled. Then another. Then silence.

Then the hum.

The clock is no longer ticking. It is audible.
