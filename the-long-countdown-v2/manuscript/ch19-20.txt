## Chapter 19: The Parking Lot at Midnight

The phone rings at 12:07 AM and I know before I answer that something has cracked open in the night.

Not the world. Not yet. Something smaller and more important.

"Nate?" Sophie's voice is thin. Compressed. The sound of someone holding their jaw very still so the trembling doesn't reach their words. "Nate, I need—can you—"

"Where are you?"

"I'm fine. I'm fine, I just—"

"Soph. Where are you?"

She gives me the name of a gas station off Route 9, two hours north. I'm already pulling on jeans, phone wedged between shoulder and ear, searching for my keys in the dark.

"Stay there. I'm coming."

"You don't have to—"

"I'm coming. Stay there. Get something to drink. Water. Or coffee."

"Okay." A breath. "Okay."

I drive. The interstate is empty at this hour. My car smells like the granola bar I forgot in the center console. The radio is off. I drive with both hands on the wheel and I do not think about what could have happened. I think about the road. The next mile marker.

She didn't call Frank. Didn't call Diane. Called me.

That fact sits in my chest like a coal.

I make it in an hour forty-three. The gas station is one of those half-dead places — two pumps, a flickering sign, a clerk watching something on their phone.

Sophie's car is parked at the far edge of the lot. She's sitting on the hood. Arms wrapped around her knees. When my headlights sweep across her I can see she's been crying, but she's stopped now.

I get out. Walk over. Lean against her car beside her.

"You want coffee?" I ask.

She laughs. Wet and ragged. "Yeah. Yeah, okay."

I go inside. Pour two coffees from a pot that's been sitting since God knows when. Add three sugars to hers. Grab peanut butter crackers because she forgets to eat when she's stressed.

Back outside. I hand her the coffee and lean against the doorframe.

"Thank you," she says. Sips. Makes a face. Sips again.

I wait. Four minutes.

"I got pulled over," she says finally. "Coming back from Melanie's thing. The fundraiser."

"Okay."

"I had three glasses of wine. Over four hours. I ate. I felt fine."

"Okay."

"I blew 0.07. He said that's under. He let me go." Her hands tighten around the cup. "I can't stop shaking."

I sit down on the asphalt next to her open door. "Yeah," I say. "That makes sense."

"It does?"

"You got scared. That's okay."

"I kept thinking—if it had been one more glass. If I'd said yes instead of switching to water—"

"But you didn't."

"But I could have."

"But you didn't."

Sophie looks at me. "How are you so calm about this?"

"Because you're okay," I say. "You made good choices. You stopped drinking. You ate. You called someone when you got scared. Those are all the right things."

"I called you."

"Yeah."

"At midnight."

"Yeah."

"And you drove two hours."

"Hour forty-three, actually."

She laughs. Better this time. "Of course you timed it."

We sit. The coffee is terrible. A truck pulls in, fills up, leaves. We are just two people in a parking lot at one in the morning.

"I didn't call Dad," Sophie says, around 1:30. "He'd worry. There'd be this whole production."

"Yeah."

"I just wanted someone to sit with."

"Yeah."

Around 2:45, Sophie yawns. "I should get home."

"You good to drive?"

"Yeah. Follow me? Just be behind me until I get there?"

"Yeah. Of course."

I follow her taillights for twenty minutes through dark county roads until she pulls into her apartment complex. She waves from her door. I flash my lights twice. Then I drive two hours home in the dark, arriving at 5 AM.

I sleep for three hours. Then I get up and do the thing I've been putting off for six weeks.

---

Elena's apartment is on the third floor of a building that smells like cumin and laundry detergent in the stairwell. I knock.

Footsteps. The chain. The door opens.

Elena is wearing paint-stained jeans and an old flannel shirt I recognize as mine. Her hair is shorter. There's blue acrylic on her forearm.

"Nate." An acknowledgment that I exist and am standing in her doorway.

"Hi."

"Hi."

"Can I come in?"

She looks at me a long time. Then steps aside.

The apartment is different. Rearranged. Plants everywhere. An easel by the window with something half-finished — blues and grays.

She sits on the couch. Nods toward the chair.

"Okay," she says. "You have something to say. Say it."

The rehearsed speech scatters. What comes out is raw.

"I have knowledge of something catastrophic coming. I've spent my adult life preparing for it. Everything I did that looked obsessive or secretive — it was because of this. I couldn't tell you because you'd think I was crazy. I chose the mission over you. And that was wrong."

Elena doesn't move.

"I'm not asking you to believe me about what's coming. I know how it sounds. But even if I'm wrong — the thing that's true either way is that I chose wrong. I lied to you. I'm sorry."

Silence. Plants sway in the draft.

"What kind of catastrophe?" she asks.

"Existential. Species-level."

"When?"

"Years. Several."

"And you've known since—"

"Since before we met."

She closes her eyes. Opens them.

"Nate. I don't believe the world is ending."

"I know."

"But I believe you believe it. And I believe you're sorry."

Something in my chest releases.

"What do you want?" she asks. "From me. Right now."

"I just want to be in the room with you. Whatever you'll allow."

She studies me. Minutes pass.

"Okay," she says. "You can be in the room."

---

She doesn't move back right away. But she comes for dinner three days later. Then two days after. The third time she stays — falls asleep on the couch. I put a blanket over her. In the morning: a note. *Coffee was cold. Upgrade your machine. -E*

The fourth time, she brings an overnight bag. Sets it by the door like a question.

"Stay," I say.

She stays.

Two weeks of sleeping in the same bed with measurable distance between us. Then one Tuesday evening she's unpacking clothes and finds the file box in the closet.

She looks at me. I look at her.

"Show me," she says. "All of it."

I pull the box down. Set it on the kitchen table.

I open it. For the first time, I do not close it.

## Chapter 20: Full Disclosure

Elena reads for six hours.

I make coffee. Refill her cup twice. She doesn't look up. Pages shuffle. At 2 AM she puts down the last page.

"You're either the most elaborate paranoid I've ever met," she says, "or you're right."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

She picks up Chen's atmospheric data. "This woman. The physicist."

"Dr. Chen. Judith."

"She believes you?"

"She believes the data."

Elena stacks the papers neatly. Returns them to the box in order.

"If you're right," she says slowly, "then everything you've done makes sense."

"And if I'm wrong—"

"If you're wrong, I've wasted my life."

"That's my line," I say.

Something in her face softens past caution. "It's mine now too."

---

Three days later, Chen's office. Past eight. Building empty except for us and Boltzmann, asleep on lab reports.

"Judith," I say. "I need to tell you the full version."

She takes off her glasses. Folds them. Sets them down.

"Go on," she says.

I tell her everything. The first timeline. The displacement. Waking up at seventeen. The years of planning. NEORAN. Marcus. Elena. Sophie in the parking lot. All of it.

When I finish, Chen is quiet for a long time. Boltzmann has relocated to her lap.

"Time displacement," she says finally. Neutral. Scientific.

"Yes."

"You're telling me your consciousness was translated backward through time."

"That's what I'm telling you."

"I don't believe in time travel," she says. "The physics don't support it."

"I know."

"But." The but finger. "I believe in your data. Fourteen predictions verified. Atmospheric data matching my measurements to four decimal places. Pathfinder trajectory data accurate to 0.003 percent." She goes to the whiteboard. "I don't need to understand the mechanism to trust the data. The evidence precedes the explanation."

"So you believe me?"

"I believe you have accurate knowledge of future events. The time travel — I set aside. It's irrelevant to our response." She picks up the whiskey. Pours two measures into mismatched mugs. "Now. Tell me about the timeline."

We plan.

---

Six weeks later, the paper publishes. *Anomalous Atmospheric Particulate Trends: Evidence for Non-Natural Origin.* Dr. Judith Chen. Forty-three pages.

Three researchers respond. Dr. Patricia Reeves at NOAA. Dr. Kenji Nakamura in Tokyo. A climate scientist at the British Antarctic Survey. All with independent data matching Chen's findings. All sitting on it because they couldn't explain it.

"Four independent data sets," Chen tells me. "Different hemispheres. Different altitudes. Same result."

NEORAN pivots. Seed vault partnerships. Hydroponic facility grants. Community shelter hardening. Air filtration. Water recycling.

Elena adjusts. She reads everything. Attends meetings. Asks questions that expose gaps.

"Your hydroponic facilities," she says one night. "Six facilities. Three thousand people. There are three hundred and thirty million in this country."

"NEORAN isn't going to save everyone," I say. "The point is to save enough to rebuild."

"That's a terrible answer."

"I know."

"Then scale it. Build sixty. Build six hundred."

"The timeline—"

"Then we'd better hurry."

Her hand finds mine. "What do we need to do?"

"Everything we're doing. Faster. Bigger. More."

"Okay."

---

Two months later. Chen calls me to her office. Thursday. Late September.

"Close the door," she says.

She pulls up Pathfinder tracking data. Swivels the monitor.

I'm looking at trajectory plots. Familiar — I've studied these for years. But something is different.

The trajectory lines have changed. They're curving. Not dramatically. But measurably. Course corrections that don't happen without propulsion.

And the velocity data. They're slower than last month. Slower than the month before. Decelerating.

Objects in space don't decelerate without thrust.

"In your first timeline," Chen says. "Did they decelerate?"

"I don't know. The infrastructure collapsed before they arrived. We never saw the approach."

"So this is new information."

"Yes."

"Objects don't decelerate in vacuum without thrust."

"No."

"So these aren't comets."

"No."

We look at each other. The office is quiet.

"I need to make calls," I say.

"Go. Come back tomorrow. Bring the whiskey."

I leave. Behind me, the door closes.

What happens next — Chen tells me later, over that whiskey:

She sits alone. Pulls up the data one more time. Seven objects. Seven decelerating objects. All converging on the same point. If you extend the trajectories forward, accounting for deceleration—

A point that isn't far anymore.

"Oh," she says to Boltzmann. He purrs.

She enlarges the data. Watches the velocity numbers decrease. Only things with engines do this. Only things choosing where to go.

"Oh no," she says.

Boltzmann purrs.

Outside, the campus is quiet. Students gone home. The world doing what it always does — continuing, unaware of the numbers on a physicist's monitor.

Something is coming. Something that can steer. Something that is slowing down.

Something that is arriving.
