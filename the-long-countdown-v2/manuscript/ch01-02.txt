# The Long Countdown

## Chapter 1: The End of Everything

The thing about dying on your birthday is that it's not even ironic. It's just lazy writing.

I wake up on June 14, 2026 — thirty-five years old for about six hours now, technically, since I was born at 2:47 AM — and the first thing I register is that my mouth tastes like an ashtray somebody poured Miller High Life into. Which is exactly what happened. The second thing I register is that my phone is dead, its charger disconnected from the wall and coiled on the floor like it gave up on me too. The third thing is the light coming through my blinds, which is wrong.

But I don't process the light yet. Not right away.

I sit up. The eviction notice is still on the counter where I left it three days ago, pinned under an empty bottle of Evan Williams like a paperweight. Final Notice — Failure to Pay. Thirty days. The thirty days are almost up. I haven't done anything about it because I haven't done anything about anything in approximately five years, give or take, and stopping now would break my streak.

My apartment — and I use the word generously, the way realtors use "cozy" for a closet — is a studio at Briarwood Apartments. One room, kitchenette, bathroom with a shower stall so narrow I have to turn sideways. Three empty beer bottles on the nightstand. Two Camel Blue butts in the coffee mug I use as an ashtray. One pair of work boots by the door, steel-toed, caked with dust from the Menards warehouse where I spend my nights scanning pallets of lumber and PVC pipe for $18.50 an hour.

Happy birthday to me.

I swing my legs over the bed. My gut hangs over my boxers — not fat exactly, just soft, the body of a man who eats gas station burritos at 3 AM and calls it dinner. I find a cigarette in the pack on the nightstand, light it with a Bic, and that's when I finally look at the window.

The light is gray. Not overcast gray — I've lived in Iowa my entire life, I know what clouds look like — but *gray*. A flat, even, yellowish gray that has no depth to it, like someone hung a painter's drop cloth over the sky.

Storm, I think. I think that for almost a full minute while I smoke and stare. Derecho, maybe. We had one in 2020 that ripped siding off half the houses in Waterloo. I should check the weather.

My phone is dead. I plug it in, wait for the screen to light up, and while I'm waiting I notice the spider plant on the windowsill. Elena — I never learned her last name, the woman from 2B who moved out eight months ago and left it behind — her spider plant is curling. The leaves are going brown at the tips, pulling inward, like a fist closing. As I watch, one leaf drops.

Huh.

I cross to the window and look out at the parking lot. My 2014 Chevy Cruze sits in its spot, check-engine light presumably still glowing its eternal orange vigil inside. The strip of grass between the lot and the sidewalk is — wrong. It's browning. Not like drought-brown, not like August. Like right now. Like I can see it happening, one blade at a time, the green draining out.

My phone finally boots. Three percent battery. No signal. I open the notification shade: nothing. No missed calls, no texts. Nobody texts me anyway except my shift supervisor when someone calls off.

I flip on the TV — the 32-inch Roku I bought refurbished that loses its wifi connection every third day — and get static on four channels before I hit one that's still broadcasting. A woman at a desk, no makeup, reading from a paper in her hand.

"—atmospheric anomaly currently being assessed by NOAA and the Department of—"

Then static.

I stand there holding the remote, cigarette burning between my fingers. The gray outside the window hasn't moved. It doesn't look like weather anymore. Weather moves. This just sits there, pressing down.

I pick up my phone. Two percent now. I open contacts and scroll. The list is short. Shift supervisor. Pizza place. Marcus — no. Marcus's number is still in here from four years ago but Marcus isn't. His apartment key is in my junk drawer. I kept it after he — after the thing I don't think about.

I scroll past Marcus. Past the pizza place. I'm looking for a number I deleted three years ago. Mom. I deleted it after the last fight, the one where she said *you're killing yourself slowly and I can't watch* and I said *then don't watch* and that was that. That was the last thing I said to my mother.

I don't have the number memorized. I don't have it written down. I open the phone app and try to remember it anyway, punching in the 319 area code and then — what? What came after? I try three combinations. The first rings once and goes to a fax machine. The second doesn't connect. The third gives me a recording: "The number you have reached is not in service."

One percent battery.

I call my dad's old number, the landline at 2847 Tremont, and I get the same recording. Of course I do. Dad's dead. Two years now. Heart attack at sixty-three, fast at least, probably didn't feel much. I went to the funeral. I was drunk. I wore a shirt that wasn't ironed. Sophie wouldn't look at me and I didn't blame her. Mom hugged me and I stood there with my arms at my sides like a coat rack.

The phone dies in my hand.

I go back to the window. The spider plant has lost two more leaves. The grass outside is completely brown now. And the trees in the lot — three scraggly maples the property management planted to make the place look less like a Soviet housing block — their leaves are curling inward, drying, going the color of tobacco.

Something is killing everything that grows. Right now. While I watch.

I pull on jeans, a t-shirt, my boots. I don't know what I'm doing — going outside, I guess. Some animal part of my brain says *move*. I grab my keys, my cigarettes, my dead phone.

I open the door and step into the hallway. It's empty. I can hear a TV through someone's door — static. I take the stairs down, push through the fire exit into the parking lot, and stop.

The sky. I couldn't see it properly from my window. Now I can see it edge to edge. It's gray, yes, but there's something in it. A texture. Like looking at static on a screen from very far away — tiny points of movement so fine they almost look still. The air smells wrong. Not like chemicals, not like fire. Like nothing. Like the smell has been subtracted.

The trees are dead. Not dying anymore — dead. The brown leaves are falling, slow spirals in air that doesn't move. The grass is the color of cardboard. In Mrs. Petersen's flower bed by the entrance — she's 4A, waters those things every morning, talks to them — the marigolds are lying flat, gray.

I look toward the road and I see it.

I don't know what it is. I don't have a word for it. It's crossing the Menards parking lot across the highway — the lot where I park every night for my shift, where I smoke on my breaks and look at the stars and wonder if this is really all there is. Something is crossing that lot. It's tall — not human-tall, tall like a streetlight is tall — and it moves in a way that doesn't track with anything I've seen move before. Smooth. Unhurried. Behind it, the asphalt is — I don't know. Different. A trail of gray. The same gray as the sky.

I don't run. I should run. I just stand there and watch it cross the lot, smooth and unhurried, and I think: so this is what was always coming. Some part of me always knew something was coming. I just thought it would be a heart attack at fifty, like Dad. Or cirrhosis. Or just a long slow fade into nothing anyone noticed.

Not this.

I go back inside. I walk up the stairs. I sit on my bed. I light a cigarette with hands that don't shake because I think maybe I'm in shock, or maybe this is what it feels like to be too hollowed-out to be afraid.

At 9:17 AM — I know the time because the microwave clock is the only thing in this apartment that works reliably — the light comes.

It's white. Not like sunlight, not like a lamp. White like the concept of white, the platonic ideal of it, white that has no source and no shadow. It comes through the walls. Through me. I feel pressure — not pain exactly but pressure, in my skull, behind my eyes, in the marrow of my bones — like I'm being read. Like something is moving through me the way you move through a sentence, left to right, absorbing meaning.

I have time to think one thing. One clear thought, the clearest I've had in years: *I wasted it. All of it. Every day.*

Then the sensation of being unmade. Not death — I don't think this is death, though I have no frame of reference — but *unmade*. Disassembled. Taken apart at a level below atoms, below the small pieces that make up atoms, at whatever level is underneath that.

I'm gone.

---

I wake up and I am dying.

My heart is slamming against my ribs like it wants out. My lungs won't fill. There's a sound — high-pitched, keening — and it takes me four seconds to realize it's coming from me. I'm making a sound like a wounded animal and I can't stop.

I'm in a bed. A twin bed. The sheets are — plaid. Blue and green plaid, flannel, soft in the way that means they've been washed two hundred times. There's a ceiling fan above me, off, dust on the blades. A poster on the wall: *The Dark Knight*, that shot of Batman standing on the building ledge.

I am dying. I'm having a stroke or a heart attack or an aneurysm and this is the hallucination, the last firework the brain shoots off before it goes dark. Because this is my bedroom. My bedroom at 2847 Tremont Street, Cedar Falls, Iowa. My bedroom from when I was —

I hold up my hands.

They're thin. Long fingers, no scar on the right knuckle from the night I punched the wall after Marcus — no scar. Smooth. Pale. Young.

I scramble out of the bed, trip on something — a backpack, Jansport, covered in Sharpie tags — and hit the floor. Hardwood. My shoulder takes the impact and it *hurts*, real physical pain, and I grab onto that pain because it means I'm here, I'm somewhere, I'm in a body.

I get up. I'm wearing boxers and a t-shirt — Cedar Falls Tigers, faded black and orange. I'm standing and my center of gravity is wrong, higher, lighter. I look down at a body that is lean and narrow, hipbones visible, no gut.

The mirror. There's a mirror on the back of the door.

I open the door — hallway, bathroom straight ahead, Sophie's door with the Keep Out sign — and close it again so I can see the mirror.

Seventeen.

I am looking at Nathan Callahan at seventeen years old. Sandy brown hair sticking up in six directions. Blue eyes, bloodshot now, wide enough to see the whites all around. Jawline — Jesus, I forgot about the acne. Three red bumps along the jaw, the deep painful kind that won't come to a head for days.

I am seventeen. I am lean and tall — well, 5'11", which felt tall at seventeen — and my lungs are clear and my hands are smooth and I don't smell like cigarettes because I haven't started smoking yet. Won't start until freshman year of college. Won't become a real habit until twenty-three.

I grip the edge of the door. My reflection grips back. The face looking at me is terrified in a way that seventeen-year-old Nate Callahan never was, because seventeen-year-old Nate Callahan hadn't yet learned how many things there are to be afraid of.

I don't know how long I stand there. Long enough for my heartbeat to come down from sprint to jog. Long enough for the keening sound to stop. Long enough to notice the details: the stack of AP textbooks on my desk. The silver Samsung flip phone on the nightstand, charging. The calendar on the wall — Cedar Falls Tigers football schedule — September 2008.

September 2008.

I am seventeen years old and it is 2008 and I'm standing in my childhood bedroom and I don't understand what's happening but I understand one thing with absolute clarity:

Whatever was done to me — the white light, the pressure, the being unmade — I'm here now. In this body. In this time. And downstairs, through the floor, I can hear —

"Breakfast is getting cold, bud."

My father's voice. From the kitchen. Calling up the stairs in the same flat tone he used every morning for eighteen years. *Breakfast is getting cold, bud.* Like it's nothing. Like it's Tuesday. Like he isn't two years dead and cremated and scattered in the Cedar River because he asked for that in his will.

I sit down on the floor. I put my head in my hands. And I breathe.

## Chapter 2: The Man in the Boy

Here's the thing they don't tell you about miracles: they don't wait for you to be ready.

I'm sitting on my bedroom floor, hands shaking, heart doing something unnatural in my chest, and my dead father is downstairs eating Cheerios. I can hear the spoon clinking against the bowl. I can hear the rustle of the Cedar Falls Courier. I can hear Diane — Mom — running water at the sink. These are sounds I haven't heard in years. In some cases, sounds I'll never hear again. In other cases, sounds I'm hearing right now, so maybe everything I knew is wrong.

I stand up. My legs feel like someone else's legs. I'm too light, too fast, too clear-headed despite the panic. This is what it feels like to be seventeen and not hung over — I'd forgotten. The absence of fog.

I get dressed. My closet is a museum exhibit. Abercrombie t-shirt. Bootcut jeans — Jesus, bootcut jeans. A hoodie that says CEDAR FALLS TIGERS. I put them on and they fit because this is my body and these are my clothes and I am going to go downstairs and see my dead father and if I am insane then at least I'm insane in a comfortable place.

The hallway. Bathroom door open — Sophie's toothbrush in the holder, pink, the bristles splayed out because she presses too hard. Family photos on the wall going down the stairs. I pass one of all four of us at the state fair, 2005 or 2006, and I have to look away because my face in the photo is so young and so unguarded it feels like staring at something private.

Kitchen.

Dad is at the table. The Cedar Falls Courier is spread in front of him, folded to the sports section. He's wearing a flannel shirt, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms thick and ropy from thirty years of pipe fitting. His hair is gray at the temples but still mostly brown. His hands — I stare at his hands. Calloused, scarred, a wedding band buried in the flesh of his ring finger. Those hands held me upright when I was learning to walk. Those hands built the deck out back. Those hands were folded on his chest in a casket I could barely look at.

He glances up. Registers me. Back to the paper.

"You look rough, bud."

I grab the counter. Both hands, white-knuckled, because the floor is tilting. He's *here*. He's sitting at the kitchen table in his flannel shirt reading about the UNI Panthers and he said *bud* and he's alive, he's so alive, and I am going to throw up or pass out or scream.

I don't do any of those things.

"Didn't sleep great," I say. My voice. Seventeen. Higher, thinner, no gravel. It sounds like someone else talking.

"Mmh." He turns a page.

That's it. That's the whole conversation. Frank Callahan, man of approximately eleven words per day, allocated two to me and returned to box scores. And it is — I can't — it is the most beautiful thing I have ever experienced in my life. The sheer ordinary nothing of it. The way he doesn't look up again. The way he trusts that I'm fine, that the world is fine, that breakfast is Cheerios and the paper and then work and then home and then tomorrow, on and on, ordinary and ordinary and ordinary.

I realize I'm still gripping the counter. I let go. I sit down. There's a bowl in front of me — Diane must have set it out — and the Cheerios box and a carton of milk. I pour cereal. I pour milk. I eat. It tastes like Cheerios. I don't know what I expected. Ambrosia. Ashes. It tastes like Cheerios and that's enough to make my eyes sting.

Mom comes in from the laundry room, basket on her hip. She's forty-four or forty-five here, I'm doing math I shouldn't have to do. Hair in a ponytail, reading glasses pushed up on her head, that specific mom-energy of someone managing four lives simultaneously while making it look easy.

She looks at me and her face does the thing. The tilt. The narrowing. Twenty years of reading her children's moods.

"Honey? You okay?" She sets the basket down, comes over, presses the back of her hand to my forehead. Her palm is cool and smells like fabric softener. "You're not warm."

"I'm fine." The second lie of the day. Or the first truth, depending on how you define *fine*. I'm here. They're here. That might be fine. That might be the finest thing that has ever happened.

"You look pale." She's studying me. Moms are detectives. They don't need evidence — they read micro-expressions the CIA would envy.

"Stayed up too late." Third lie. I force a smile. Seventeen-year-old-Nate's smile, the one that got me out of conversations. I don't know if I'm doing it right. I haven't smiled for real in long enough that the mechanics feel foreign.

She watches me for another second, then squeezes my shoulder. "Eat. You'll miss the bus."

The bus. Right. I take the bus to school. I am seventeen and I take the school bus, or — no. September 2008, I have my license. I got it in March of '08. But I don't have a car yet. That comes junior year when Dad finds the used Civic. So yes. The bus. Unless I walk. I used to walk sometimes.

"I'll walk," I say.

"It's going to rain."

"I'll walk fast."

She gives me the look — the *you're being weird but I'll allow it* look — and goes back to sorting laundry. Frank stands, folds the paper, puts his bowl in the sink. As he passes me he puts one hand on top of my head, brief, barely a touch, and then he's grabbing his lunch pail and heading for the door.

"Have a good one," he says to the room at large.

"You too, babe," Mom says.

The screen door bangs shut. I hear his truck start — the F-150, forest green, rust around the wheel wells. He'll drive to the job site in Independence or wherever they're working this month, he'll cut pipe and sweat joints for nine hours, he'll come home and drink two beers on the porch and fall asleep during Letterman. He has seventeen more years of this. Seventeen more years of being alive and not knowing that's remarkable.

I finish my cereal. I rinse the bowl. I go upstairs, brush my teeth, grab the Jansport backpack. I check the Samsung flip phone — no messages. The date on the screen: September 9, 2008.

Two months before Obama wins. Seven months before the economy collapses completely. Eighteen years before the sky goes gray and everything dies.

I walk to school.

---

Cedar Falls in September is all yellowing elms and lawns still green from summer rain and flags on porches for football season. I walk down Tremont to Main, past the Hendersons' house where old Mr. Henderson is raking leaves he'll rake again next week, past the Kowalski place where their golden retriever barks once at me and wags its tail. I know these houses. I know these people. I know which ones will move, which will divorce, which will die.

The Hendersons will sell in 2014 when he gets dementia. The Kowalskis' dog has maybe two years left. The elm on the corner will come down in the 2020 derecho.

I walk and I breathe and my lungs — God, my lungs. Clean. Huge. I can breathe all the way to the bottom and there's no rattle, no tightness, no morning cough. Seventeen years of breathing without a single cigarette. I want to run. I want to sprint down Main Street and feel what this body can do when it's not poisoned.

I don't. I walk. I pass kids I half-recognize heading the same direction. A girl in a North Face jacket who might be Abby Schaefer. Two guys in letter jackets who are definitely Ryan Park and somebody else. They don't talk to me. I'm not popular and I'm not unpopular — I exist in the vast middle space of high school social geography, known enough to be nodded at, not known enough to be called over.

That's fine. Better than fine. The idea of performing social interaction right now makes my skin crawl. I'm thirty-five years old in a seventeen-year-old's body, walking to a high school I graduated from in 2009, and I have to sit in classes and pretend that any of this matters when I know — I *know* — that in eighteen years something is coming that will end everything.

Anyway. The point is—

The school. Cedar Falls High. Home of the Tigers. It's smaller than I remember, which is what everyone says about the places they grew up, but I mean it literally — the building looks compressed, somehow. Low ceilings. Narrow halls. Lockers in that specific institutional beige that exists only in schools and DMVs. The smell: floor wax, body spray, pencil shavings, cafeteria grease. The sound: five hundred teenagers doing their best impression of chaos.

My locker combination lives in muscle memory I didn't know I still had. 24-8-31. The lock pops open and inside is a textbook for AP US History, a Pre-Calculus book with a cracked spine, a hoodie I don't remember owning, and a photo of Marcus and me taped to the inside of the door.

Marcus.

He's gap-toothed and grinning, arm around my shoulder, both of us in those stupid plastic sunglasses they give out at football games. We're probably sixteen in this photo. He's alive. He's here. He's somewhere in this building right now, gap-toothed and grinning and seventeen and he doesn't know that in ten years he'll—

I close the locker. Hard.

First period: AP US History. Mr. Driscoll. I remember him — short, enthusiastic, the kind of teacher who genuinely believes the past has lessons for the present. I take my seat. Third row, by the window. The desk is too small — or my body is too young for it — both, neither. I sit.

Driscoll is talking about the 2008 election. McCain versus Obama. The primaries are over, the conventions just happened. He's asking us what we think will determine the outcome.

A girl — Rachel something, front row — says the economy. Driscoll nods, writes ECONOMY on the board. A guy in the back says Palin. Driscoll writes PALIN (VP PICK). He's building a list. He looks around the room, that teacher-scanning thing, looking for a hand that's not raised.

He finds me.

"Nate? Thoughts?"

And I — thirty-five years old, having lived through this election, having watched the post-mortems and the documentaries and the retrospectives — I open my mouth and say: "The ground game. Obama's field operation is operating in states nobody's paying attention to. Virginia, North Carolina, Indiana. The map is going to look different than people expect."

The room is quiet. Driscoll's eyebrows go up. He was expecting *the war* or *healthcare* or some other talking-point answer from a seventeen-year-old. Not campaign logistics.

"That's... a very sophisticated observation, Nate. You've been reading Nate Silver?"

"Something like that." Nate Silver. Right. FiveThirtyEight exists in 2008 but barely anyone knows about it yet. Shut up. *Shut up. Shut up. Shut up.* I am going to blow this in week one because I can't keep my idiot mouth closed.

"Well, hold that thought. We'll revisit after November."

I nod. I look at my desk. I feel heat in my face — the acne-prone skin of seventeen flushing with a thirty-five-year-old's panic. I have to be more careful. I have to remember what I knew and when I knew it. I have to be dumber than I am, which is ironic because I wasn't exactly an intellectual powerhouse in the first timeline either. I just happened to live through the answers.

The rest of the morning passes in a haze. I sit through classes I don't remember having — Pre-Calc, English, Chemistry — and I take notes in handwriting that's neater than my adult scrawl because these muscles haven't been ruined by keyboards and warehouse clipboards. I answer questions when called on, briefly, nothing that stands out. I am trying very hard to be unremarkable, which is a skill I had at seventeen and lost somewhere in my twenties when I started being remarkable in all the wrong ways.

Lunch.

Marcus finds me in the cafeteria. He drops his tray across from mine and grins — that gap in his front teeth, the one he'll close with a retainer junior year. His eyes are bright. He's seventeen and the whole world is a thing he hasn't been broken by yet.

"Callahan. What's up."

"Not much." Everything. Nothing I can say.

He's talking about something — a project, a girl, a game. I nod in the right places. I'm watching him and seeing double: this Marcus, seventeen, and the Marcus from six years ago, the last time I saw him, gaunt and shaking and telling me he needed help and me saying *I can't do this right now, man*. His apartment key is in my junk drawer. Was in my junk drawer. In a timeline that might not exist anymore.

"—so you coming Friday or what?"

"What?"

"Friday. Tyler's. His parents are in Des Moines."

"Yeah. Sure. Friday."

He looks at me sideways. "You good? You seem weird today."

"Tired."

"Stay up jerking off to Palin?" He grins.

"Jesus, Marcus."

"What? She's a MILF."

I watch him eat his turkey sandwich and laugh at his own joke and I think: I'm going to keep you alive this time. Whatever it takes. Whatever I have to do or say or be. You're sitting across from me making MILF jokes and you don't know what's coming and I do and I'm going to fix it.

I don't know yet if I can fix any of it. The big thing — the gray sky, the white light, the thing that ended everything — I don't even know what that was. I don't know if anything I do in the next eighteen years can prevent it. I don't know if this is a second chance or a longer sentence.

But Marcus. Dad. Mom. Sophie. These are small enough that maybe I can hold them. These are just people, just choices, just the ordinary daily work of being present instead of gone. I was gone the first time. Checked out. Drunk. High. Working thirds and sleeping through the days and calling it a life.

Anyway. The point is—

The afternoon. I have a study hall last period, which means I'm home by 3:15, dropping my backpack by the door, standing in the living room of a house that smells like Pledge and laundry and the vague hint of whatever Mom's planning for dinner.

Sophie comes in at 3:30 — middle school lets out later, the bus drops her at the corner. Thirteen years old. Braces catching the light when she talks. Hair in a ponytail she'll fight Mom about straightening by Christmas. She's sharp the way small animals are sharp: watchful, quick, ready to bite.

She throws herself into a chair at the dining table, pulls out a textbook. Geometry. I can see her scowling at it from the kitchen doorway where I'm pouring a glass of water.

I walk over. I look at the page. Angle bisectors. I remember this — not from high school, but from the half-semester of community college I did at twenty-two before dropping out. The tutoring center. A woman with a whiteboard explaining the angle bisector theorem like it was the most obvious thing in the world, and me finally getting it.

"You need the perpendicular from the bisector to each side," I say, pointing at problem four. "They're equidistant. That's the whole trick."

Sophie looks up at me. Her expression is — I don't know how to describe it. Like I've sprouted a second head. Like the family dog just spoke French.

"You *listened*?" she says. Meaning: in class. Meaning: you, Nate, who historically zones out during anything involving numbers. Meaning: who are you and what have you done with my brother.

"I pay attention sometimes."

"Since when?"

"Since now, I guess."

She stares at me for another three seconds, then looks back at her textbook. She doesn't say thank you. She doesn't have to. I can see her pencil moving, working the problem, and the scowl is gone.

I go upstairs.

I make it to the bathroom. Close the door. Lock it. Third stall energy without the stall — just me and the sink and the mirror and the face that isn't mine.

The panic comes like weather. One second I'm fine and the next I'm on the floor with my back against the bathtub and my hands over my mouth and my lungs refusing to fill and the room telescoping around me. I know what this is — I've had panic attacks before, in my thirties, twice in my car in the Menards parking lot — but knowing doesn't help. Knowing never helps. You can't logic your way out of the body's conviction that it's dying.

I am thirty-five years old and I am seventeen years old and I am in my parents' bathroom and I know that the economy will crash in six months and that a pandemic will kill a million Americans in twelve years and that something will come from the sky in eighteen years and end everything. I know all of this. I know it like I know my own heartbeat, constant and inescapable.

Foreknowledge is not a superpower. It is a sentence. It is solitary confinement inside your own head, trapped with information you cannot share, cannot use, cannot unlearn. Nobody will believe me. Nobody should believe me. I wouldn't believe me.

I press my forehead to the cold tile floor. I breathe. In through the nose — four seconds. Hold — seven. Out through the mouth — eight. The counting gives me something to do that isn't spiral. Slowly, like a radio station tuning in, the room comes back. The floor. The bathtub rim against my spine. The hum of the exhaust fan.

I sit there for ten minutes. Fifteen. Until my hands stop shaking. Until I can stand up without the room swinging. I wash my face. Cold water. I look at myself in the mirror — seventeen, still, somehow — and I say, very quietly: "Okay. Okay."

It is not a plan. It is barely even a word. But it's what I've got.

---

I'm sitting on the front porch when Dad gets home at 5:40. I don't know why I'm out here. The air, maybe. The specific September quality of Iowa evenings — warm enough to sit, cool enough that you can feel fall coming. The maples on Tremont are just starting to turn. They look healthy. Green. Alive.

Dad's truck pulls into the driveway. He gets out, lunch pail in one hand, keys in the other. Flannel shirt darker at the collar where he sweated. He sees me on the porch and gives me a look — not concerned exactly, just registering. Frank Callahan catalogs his children's locations the way he catalogs his tools: noted, filed and useful later if needed.

He sits in the other chair. The plastic Adirondack, white, with a hairline crack along one arm that he'll never fix because it doesn't affect function. He doesn't say anything. He just sits. Leans back. Looks at the street.

I don't say anything either.

We sit there for ten minutes. Cars pass. The Hendersons' sprinkler goes tss-tss-tss. A kid bikes past on the sidewalk, standing on the pedals. Somewhere a dog barks twice and stops.

After ten minutes, Frank stands. He puts his hand on my shoulder — two seconds, firm, then gone — and goes inside. Screen door bangs. I hear him say something to Mom in the kitchen. The clatter of a pan.

That's it. No conversation. No questions. No *what's wrong* or *talk to me* or any of the things that well-meaning people say when they can tell you're drowning. Just presence. Just ten minutes of shared silence and a hand on my shoulder.

It is the most comfort I have received in years.

I sit on the porch until Mom calls dinner. I go inside. I eat meatloaf and green beans and mashed potatoes at a table with four people — three of whom I lost in various ways, two through my own negligence, one to a thing I don't understand — and I eat every bite and I say "it's good, Mom" and I mean it with every cell of this secondhand body.

After dinner. Dishes done — Sophie washes, I dry, same chore rotation since we were old enough to hold a sponge. She's quiet while we work, which isn't unusual. But when we're done, and I'm hanging the dish towel on the oven handle, and she's sitting back down at the dining table with her textbook, she looks up.

"Why are you being weird?"

I stop. She's looking at me with those sharp thirteen-year-old eyes. Not accusing. Just observing. Noting the data points: geometry help, voluntary dish-drying without complaint, the general aura of whatever it is I'm radiating. She doesn't know what it means. She just knows it's different.

I don't have an answer. Or rather, I have an answer so enormous and impossible that it might as well be no answer at all.

"I don't know," I say. Honest.

She looks at me for another second. Then she looks back down at her textbook. She didn't expect an answer. She doesn't need one. She's thirteen, and her brother is acting strange, and she's filed that away for future reference.

I go upstairs. I close my door. I lie on the twin bed with the plaid flannel sheets and stare at the ceiling fan and I think: tomorrow. I have to do this again tomorrow. And the day after. And the day after that. For eighteen years. Knowing what's coming. Not knowing if I can stop it. Not knowing if any of this is real.

But my father is alive downstairs. My mother is watching TV in the living room. My sister is doing geometry at the dining table. And tomorrow Marcus will find me at lunch and tell me some joke I won't remember and flash that gap-toothed grin.

Tomorrow I will try again.
