Chapter Three: The Friend He Didn't Deserve

The court at Byrnes Park has three cracks running through it like fault lines and a rim on the south end that's bent two degrees to the left from when Cody Voss tried to dunk in sophomore year and hung on the thing like a cat on a curtain rod. The nets are gone. They've been gone since July. Someone took them — stole them, actually stole basketball nets, which is either a crime of pure pettiness or a statement about the declining social contract in mid-size Iowa municipalities.

Anyway. The point is—

Marcus pulls up from fifteen feet and the ball catches the back of the rim, rattles twice, drops through. He pumps his fist once, small, like even his celebrations are polite.

"That's seven. I'm up by three."

He's up by three because I cannot make my body do what my brain knows it should do. I have seventeen years of basketball experience that says drive baseline when the defender gives you the angle, and I have two arms that apparently received different memos about what driving baseline looks like. My feet are too fast for my hips. My hands remember a wingspan I no longer have. It's like playing an instrument you sold ten years ago — your fingers know the shapes, but the shapes come a half-beat late, ghost muscle memory reaching for strings that aren't there.

But my brain reads the game better than it ever did at seventeen. I can see Marcus's tendencies — left hand is decorative, he always goes right off the dribble, his pull-up jumper follows a three-dribble setup like a password you can watch him type. In the original timeline, I never noticed any of this. In the original timeline, I played basketball the way I did everything: instinctively, lazily, well enough to not get embarrassed and poorly enough to never get better.

I steal the ball on the next possession. Read his eyes, jumped the passing lane to an imaginary teammate, came up with it clean. My brain says layup. My body says okay but let me trip over my own feet first. I miss the layup, hit the bottom of the backboard, and the ball caroms into the grass.

"That was the worst fast break in the history of organized basketball," Marcus says. "And this isn't even organized."

"The steal was clean."

"The steal was beautiful. Everything after the steal was a war crime."

It's mid-October now. The trees along University Avenue have started turning — that first wave, the maples going yellow while the oaks are still pretending it's summer. The air tastes like woodsmoke from somewhere. Two kids, maybe twelve, are waiting on the bench by the water fountain, watching us, probably hoping we'll leave soon so they can have the court.

Marcus checks the ball at the top and we play. He backs me down — he's got two inches on me and about fifteen pounds of frame that's already turning into the man he'll become — and I let him because fighting it will just result in me fouling him in a game with no fouls. He spins, goes right because he always goes right, and puts up a soft hook that's actually pretty.

"Game," he says. "Eleven to six."

"Eleven to seven."

"It's definitely six."

"It's absolutely seven. I hit that three."

"That was a two. You were inside the line."

"There is no line. The line is a concept. The line is whatever we decide it is."

Marcus laughs — full, loud, the gap between his front teeth showing. That laugh. God. In 2019, I was at a bar in Waterloo and I heard someone laugh like that from across the room and I turned around so fast I knocked my drink off the counter. It wasn't him. It was never going to be him, because by then Marcus Wade and I hadn't spoken in three years, because you can only fail someone so many times before they do the healthy thing and leave.

We sit on the bench. Marcus drinks from a Nalgene bottle that has a sticker of the Iowa State Cyclones on it — prospective, hopeful, already leaning toward the future. I drink from a half-empty water bottle I found in my backpack that might be two days old.

"So," Marcus says. He wipes his forehead with the collar of his shirt. "What are you thinking? For after."

"After basketball?"

"After high school, man. After this." He gestures vaguely at everything. Cedar Falls. Iowa. The court with its broken nets and its crooked rim. "My mom keeps asking if I've looked at applications."

"Have you?"

"Iowa State. Maybe Minnesota." He shrugs. He's doing the thing he does when he's about to say something real — looking at the court instead of at me, bouncing his knee, working up to the sincerity. "Engineering, I think."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah." He's quiet for a second. "Bridges, specifically. If I could pick anything. Civil engineering, focus on bridges."

"Why bridges?"

He looks at me then. Direct, open, that Marcus thing where he just says what he means without any protective irony layered over it. "They connect things. Places that are separate — a bridge makes them one place. I don't know. That's stupid."

"It's not stupid."

"It's a little stupid. It's like a motivational poster."

"It's not stupid, Marcus."

He looks at me. Something in my voice, probably. I'm trying to keep it level but there's a quality to my insistence that isn't seventeen, and I can see him filing it away. Marcus has always noticed things. That's part of what made him so easy to hurt — he paid attention, so when you let him down, he saw it coming and let you do it anyway, because he was loyal in a way that cost him.

In 2014, I borrowed twelve hundred dollars from Marcus to cover rent. That's the number I carry. Twelve hundred dollars. I told him I'd pay it back in two weeks. I didn't. I told him I'd pay it back in a month. I didn't. I avoided his calls. I ducked him at the one bar in Waterloo where our circles still overlapped. I let a debt turn into a distance and a distance turn into a silence and a silence turn into the kind of permanent wound that you can't reopen because the person you'd apologize to has already done the work of healing without you.

The last time I saw Marcus Wade in the original timeline was 2016. He came by my apartment to get a box of stuff he'd left there — books, a jacket, some video games. He let himself in with the key I'd given him. I came home and the box was gone and the key was on the counter and I stood in my kitchen looking at that key and understanding, finally, that some things don't come back.

"Engineering," I say. "Iowa State. Do it."

"Yeah, we'll see." He caps his water bottle. "What about you? Any thoughts?"

In eighteen years, the sky will change color and humanity will face something it isn't prepared for, and I'm the only person on the planet who knows it's coming, and I have no plan, no expertise, no credentials, and a GPA that currently hovers around a 3.1 because I can't bring myself to do precalculus homework even with the benefit of future knowledge because precalculus homework is tedious in every timeline.

"Still figuring it out," I say.

"Fair enough." He stands, stretches. "Hey, you want to come over Tuesday? Election night. My mom's doing a whole thing. Food, the coverage on."

"Yeah. I'd like that."

---

The overcorrection starts subtly. I don't notice it happening, which is the thing about overcorrection — it feels like goodness. It feels like making things right. It feels like the healthy response to guilt.

I lend Marcus the truck. Frank's F-150, the one I technically have permission to drive on weekdays after school. Marcus needs to get to a study group at the Waterloo library and his mom's working. I hand him the keys without him asking.

"You sure?" He holds the keys like they might be evidence.

"Totally. I'll get a ride home with someone."

I buy him lunch the next day. Arby's — his order, roast beef classic and a Jamocha shake. And the day after that, pizza from Godfather's because we're standing in line anyway and I have a twenty in my pocket. And the day after that, I bring him a Mountain Dew from the vending machine between fourth and fifth period.

On the third day, Marcus puts down the Mountain Dew and looks at me.

"Did you join a cult?"

"What?"

"A cult. A church thing. An MLM scheme. Something where they tell you to be generous and then later you have to recruit me?"

"I'm just buying my friend a soda."

"You're buying your friend a soda three days in a row after lending him a truck and paying for lunch twice." He's not accusing. He's genuinely trying to solve a puzzle. "This is weird behavior, Nate."

"I'm being nice."

"You're being something."

He's right. He's exactly right, and the fact that he's calling it out makes me realize what I'm doing. I'm not being generous. I'm making payments. Twelve hundred dollars in 2014, so twelve hundred dollars' worth of generosity in 2008, doled out in Arby's combos and loaner trucks and vending-machine Mountain Dews. As if kindness is a ledger. As if you can pre-apologize for sins you haven't committed yet. As if the transactional approach to friendship is somehow less ugly when you're the one spending.

"Sorry," I say. "I'm just — I've been in a weird headspace."

"Yeah, I noticed." He opens the Mountain Dew, though. He drinks it. That's Marcus. He'll name the weird thing, and then he'll stay in the room with you anyway.

---

The group project happens in AP Government, which I don't remember taking but apparently do — it's on my schedule, second period, Mr. Kowalski's room with the poster of the Bill of Rights that has water damage on the Ninth Amendment like even the building doesn't think anyone cares about the Ninth Amendment.

The assignment: design a political campaign strategy for a fictional candidate. Four-person groups. I'm with Marcus, a girl named Ali Weston who mostly does theater, and Derek Holt.

Derek Holt. I haven't thought about Derek Holt in years, and now he's sitting across from me in a circle of desks, spinning a mechanical pencil between his fingers, looking at the assignment sheet like he's already finished it in his head and is waiting for the rest of us to catch up.

Derek is lean and sharp. Cheekbones like geometry. Dark hair, worn longer than regulation but just short enough that no teacher has said anything yet. He has the look of someone who's been doing math in his head since before the conversation started — not school math, but the other kind. The social kind. Who's useful. Who's expendable. What angle pays out. He smiles easy but the smile doesn't always reach all the way up, and I recognize that, because I spent ten years perfecting the same trick.

"Okay," Derek says. He's already drawn a diagram on his notebook — boxes, arrows, a flow chart. "So the obvious play is to focus resources on swing districts, ignore the safe zones, and build our messaging around the opponent's weaknesses rather than our guy's strengths."

"Negative campaigning," Ali says. She doesn't sound disapproving, exactly. She sounds like she's testing the word.

"Strategic positioning," Derek corrects. "You don't have to lie. You just have to make the other side's truth look worse than yours." He taps the diagram. "You control the conversation. If we let the opponent define the terrain, we lose. If we define it, we dictate the outcome."

Marcus shifts in his seat. "What about actual policy? Like what our candidate believes?"

"That's secondary." Derek says this the way you'd say *the sky is blue.* Not aggressive. Just obvious. "Voters don't vote on policy. They vote on feeling. Feeling is manipulable. Policy is boring."

He looks at me. Direct. Like he knows I'm the one in the group who will understand what he's actually saying.

And I do. I understand it perfectly. Because what Derek Holt just articulated in thirty seconds is the operating system I ran on for fifteen years of adult life. It's how I treated jobs — identify the leverage, apply pressure, extract value. It's how I treated relationships — figure out what someone needs to hear, say it, collect the benefit. It's how I treated Marcus, eventually. What does he need from me? How much does that cost? What's the minimum viable friendship?

Looking at Derek is like looking at a mirror from 2018. Smart, strategic, completely hollow at the center. Using intelligence as a tool for extraction rather than connection. It's efficient and it's amoral and it works beautifully right up until the moment you realize you've optimized yourself into a life where nobody would notice if you disappeared.

"What if the candidate actually stands for something?" I say.

Derek looks at me. Patient, the way a teacher looks at a kid who's asked a question they'll grow out of. "Then we build messaging around what they stand for. But the strategy is the same. You don't win elections by being right. You win them by being unavoidable."

I want to argue. I want to say something about integrity, about how the long game of decency always outperforms the short game of strategy, about how the person you build with these tools isn't someone you'd want to be. But I can't say any of that without sounding insane, and also — he's not wrong. Not about elections. Not about this specific assignment.

"Okay," I say. "Fine. Let's build it your way."

Marcus glances at me. I can't read his expression. That bothers me.

We build the campaign. Derek takes point on strategy. Marcus handles the policy platform because he actually cares about it. Ali does the visual presentation because she's good at making things look professional. I write the speech — the candidate's announcement — and Derek reads it over my shoulder and says, "That's good. You've got a feel for what lands."

I know. I've had a feel for what lands my entire life. That's not a compliment. That's a diagnosis.

Election night. November 4th, 2008. The Wades' living room.

Marcus's house is on Tremont Street, a Cape Cod with blue siding that his dad painted himself two summers ago and will repaint in three years when it starts peeling. The living room is dominated by a sectional sofa that's seen better days and a television that's huge by 2008 standards and will look like an antique by 2015. There are framed photos on every surface — Marcus at different ages, his older sister Keisha at graduation, family vacations to places I'll never ask about.

Mrs. Wade — Gloria — is in the kitchen, and the kitchen is producing smells that make my seventeen-year-old metabolism sit up and bark. Collard greens. Cornbread. Something with brown sugar.

"Nate! Glad you could come." She hugs me. She's a hugger. Marcus's dad, Vernon, is in a recliner already, wearing a Howard University sweatshirt, leaning forward like the TV is going to tell him something it hasn't told anyone else yet.

The coverage starts at six. The early returns are East Coast — Vermont called immediately for Obama, Kentucky for McCain. The usual overture. The living room fills gradually — Keisha and her boyfriend, a couple of Marcus's cousins, a neighbor whose name I catch and immediately forget.

I know everything that's going to happen tonight. I know that by nine o'clock Central time, the networks will call Ohio, and that's when the room will shift. I know that by ten, it'll be over. I know that at 10:00 PM Central, CNN will project Obama as the 44th president, and I know what this room will sound like when that happens.

But I also know what this room sounds like before that. The anxiety. The superstition. Gloria saying "I can't look" every time a new state pops up on the map. Vernon leaning further and further forward in his recliner like he can will the numbers to move. Marcus sitting next to me on the sectional, his knee bouncing, his Casio watch three minutes ahead of the actual time.

I stay quiet. I eat cornbread. I let the night happen to me instead of happening to it.

Indiana goes blue early. The room gets louder.

"Indiana," Vernon says. He shakes his head. "Indiana. Lord."

Pennsylvania holds. Ohio starts trending. I can feel the trajectory of it in the room — the tension releasing in stages, the superstition giving way to hope giving way to something that looks like joy but is actually relief. Relief that the thing you believed might be possible actually is.

Gloria is crying before the call. Not sobbing — just tears on her cheeks, standing in the kitchen doorway with a dish towel in her hands, watching the television with an expression I don't have the right to fully understand.

At 10:04 PM Central time, the projection comes. The room erupts. It's cheering and hugging and Gloria actually drops the dish towel and Vernon stands up from his recliner for the first time in three hours and Marcus is on his feet, fist in the air, gap-toothed grin, and I'm sitting on the couch watching all of it and I feel—

What do I feel?

I feel like a tourist. I feel like someone watching a movie I've already seen, and I know the ending, and I know what comes after the ending — the disappointment and the complications and the long slow compromise of governance — and I can't access the purity of this moment. I've already spent it. I used it up eighteen years ago without knowing what it was worth, and now I'm sitting in this room full of joy and I can't reach it.

Marcus punches my shoulder. "We did it, man! Can you believe it?"

"I can," I say, and I smile, and it's real in the ways that matter.

Later. The room has settled. People are watching the acceptance speech in Grant Park, the crowd, the flags. Marcus and I are on the porch. It's forty degrees and neither of us has a jacket and neither of us cares.

"You called it," he says.

"What?"

"The election. In Kowalski's class. Virginia going blue. You called it dead on."

I shrug. "Lucky guess."

"You also told my mom that North Carolina would go blue by less than one point." He looks at me. "You said 'I think it'll be under fifteen thousand votes.' The margin is fourteen thousand."

I did say that. I said it without thinking, because Gloria asked and I was eating cornbread and my guard was down. Fourteen thousand votes. I remember the exact number because I once wrote a paper about it in a community college political science class that I took for three weeks before dropping out.

"I follow the polls closely," I say.

"What polls tell you margins to within a fraction of a percentage point?"

"Good ones."

Marcus looks at me. Not suspicious, exactly. Curious. The same way he looked at me when I insisted he pursue engineering. Like he's collecting data points about a thing he doesn't have a name for yet.

"Okay," he says. "Sure."

He doesn't push. That's Marcus. He notices everything and pushes nothing, and he holds the things he notices in a quiet place and waits until they assemble into something coherent.

I drive home. The streets of Cedar Falls are quiet, a few scattered honks, a car with a flag hanging out the window. I drive and I think about the look on Marcus's face. Not suspicious. Not even worried. Just — attentive. Just watching. Just being Marcus, who sees people as they are and stays anyway.

I think about the truck I lent him, the lunches I bought, the Mountain Dew. I think about how each of those gestures was a deposit in a guilt account that doesn't exist yet. I think about Derek's campaign strategy — control the conversation, define the terrain, dictate the outcome. And I think about how overcorrecting is just manipulation wearing a nicer shirt. Different intention, same mechanics. You're still treating a person as a problem to be managed rather than a human to be honest with.

The stoplight on University and Main is red. I sit there in the truck with the pine air freshener that hasn't worked in two years and I make myself admit it: I cannot fix what I did to Marcus by being generous. I cannot pre-empt a betrayal by being kind. The only thing I can actually do is not betray him. And that's not a strategy. That's just choosing, every day, to be a person who doesn't take the easy road out.

The light turns green. I drive home. Frank's porch light is on.

---

Two weeks later. Marcus and I are walking back from the library, November dusk, our breath just starting to show in the air. The streetlights on College Street are coming on one at a time, that warm-up flicker before they commit.

He hasn't mentioned the election thing again. He hasn't mentioned the truck or the lunches or the Mountain Dews. But he's walking next to me, and that's Marcus's way of saying everything he needs to say.

"You called that election thing dead on," he says. Casual. Like he's observing the weather.

I shrug. "I read a lot."

"Yeah." He nods. His breath makes a small cloud. "You said that before."

He doesn't push. He doesn't need to. He just walks next to me, hands in his jacket pockets, and I can feel him remembering.

Chapter Four: The Gravity of Old Habits

The party is at Jake Petersen's house because it's always at Jake Petersen's house. His parents go to their lake place in Okoboji every other weekend from October through April like clockwork, and Jake has the basement with the pool table and the mini-fridge his older brother stocked before leaving for Iowa City. This is the ecosystem of a small-town high school house party in 2008: someone's parents leave, word travels through a network of flip-phone texts and AIM away messages, and by nine o'clock there are thirty-five kids in a basement that was designed for twelve.

I'm not going to drink. I've made this decision firmly, clearly, in the rational daylight of Friday afternoon. I'm not going to drink because I'm thirty-five years old inside a seventeen-year-old body and I know what alcohol did to me — not the dramatic movie version, not the intervention and the rehab montage, but the real version. The slow version. The one where drinking becomes the thing you do so you don't have to feel the thing you feel, and then it becomes the thing you do so you don't have to think the thing you think, and then it becomes the thing you do, period, full stop, no preposition required.

In the original timeline, this is approximately when it started. Not this exact party — I don't think — but this season. The winter of senior year. The period when I went from drinking at parties to drinking at parties twice a week to buying a six-pack with Jake's brother's old ID and keeping it in the trunk of the truck where Frank wouldn't find it. It was gradual. It was easy. It was the most natural thing I ever did.

I'm not going to drink tonight.

I walk into Jake's basement at nine-fifteen. Marcus is already there, talking to Ali Weston and a kid named Travis whose last name I can't remember. The music is too loud — someone's iPod in a dock, playing something that in 2008 passes for hip-hop but in retrospect sounds like a historical artifact. The basement has wood paneling and a ceiling that's too low and a smell that combines stale beer and the particular musk of a space that has absorbed fifteen years of teenage gatherings into its drywall.

Cody Voss hands me a beer.

He doesn't ask. He doesn't offer. He just appears at my elbow with a red Solo cup in each hand and extends one in my direction, the same way you'd hand someone a napkin. Automatic. Social. Not even a transaction — just the baseline behavior of the environment.

I take it.

I don't decide to take it. That's the thing. There's no moment where I weigh the options, consider the consequences, make a conscious choice. My hand reaches out and accepts the cup the same way my hand would catch a ball someone tossed at me. Reflex. Muscle memory. The body doing what the body has always done.

The first sip is—

Okay. The first sip is warm Miller Lite from a keg that's been tapped for an hour, and it tastes like aluminum and regret and nothing and everything. It tastes like every version of myself I've tried to leave behind. It tastes like homecoming.

I drink it. The warmth starts in my stomach and spreads outward, that old familiar bloom, and my shoulders drop two inches, and the noise of the party goes from abrasive to acceptable to almost pleasant. The kid I don't know laughs at something and I laugh too, because the beer makes everything adjacent to funny. The ceiling isn't too low. The music isn't too loud. The world has soft edges.

The second beer is also not a decision. Someone fills my cup. The keg is in the corner by the pool table and the flow of bodies creates a current that carries you toward it. Physics. Inevitability. The path of least resistance, which is the path I built my entire first life on.

Halfway through the third cup, the thirty-five-year-old in my skull goes quiet. That's the thing about alcohol that nobody who hasn't experienced it understands — it doesn't make you someone else. It makes you less of yourself. It subtracts the parts of you that object, that resist, that remember. And right now, subtraction feels like mercy. Right now, not carrying the weight of eighteen years of future knowledge feels like setting down a suitcase you forgot you were holding.

I play pool. I'm bad at it in a fun way. I talk to people — easy, loose, charming in the way that I've always been charming when I'm three drinks in and the stakes are low. I make Rachel Kovacs laugh. I make a shot I shouldn't have made and raise my arms in triumph. I am seventeen years old and nothing is wrong and the world will not end and Frank will not die and I will not betray Marcus and the sky will never change color.

The fourth beer. Or is it the fifth? The count stops mattering at a certain point. That's a feature, not a bug.

Marcus appears at my elbow somewhere around eleven. "Hey. You good?"

"I'm great." I am. I'm magnificent. I'm a seventeen-year-old at a party and I feel like a seventeen-year-old at a party for the first time since I got here, and that feeling is worth every ounce of rationality I'm trading for it.

"You sure? You seem pretty—"

"I'm fine, Marcus. I'm having fun. Remember fun? This is what it looks like."

He watches my face for a second. Then he nods. "Okay. Just checking."

He doesn't push. He never pushes. That's the thing about Marcus that I exploited for fifteen years — his loyalty is patient, and patient loyalty is the easiest thing in the world to abuse, because it doesn't fight back. It just quietly endures until one day it picks up its key from the counter and walks out.

I don't remember the rest of the night. That's a lie. I remember parts of it — standing on the porch at some point, cold air on my face, talking to someone about something that felt important. I remember the spins when I closed my eyes. I remember Marcus's hand on my arm, steering me somewhere.

---

I wake up on Marcus's couch.

The couch is brown, corduroy, and it smells like fabric softener and the particular warmth of a house where someone cooked dinner last night. There's an afghan over me — hand-crocheted, zigzag pattern, blue and gold — that I recognize as the one Gloria keeps on the back of the loveseat. There's a glass of water on the coffee table next to me, full, with a coaster underneath it because the Wades use coasters.

My head is a construction site. My mouth tastes like something crawled in and died. My stomach is a negotiation between nausea and hunger, and hunger is losing.

I sit up. The room tilts, stabilizes. Morning light through the curtains. The house is quiet in that way houses are quiet when people are being quiet on purpose — the muted footsteps, the whispered voices from the kitchen, the deliberate care of people trying not to wake someone.

The water. Someone left me water and a blanket. Someone brought me here instead of leaving me on Jake's basement floor. Someone removed my shoes — I look down, and yes, my shoes are placed neatly by the end of the couch, laces still tied, the way you'd set out shoes for a child.

Marcus appears from the kitchen. He's in sweatpants and a t-shirt and he's carrying a plate of toast, plain, and a bottle of Advil. He sets them on the coffee table without ceremony.

"Morning," he says.

"Hey."

He sits in the chair across from me. He doesn't say anything for a minute. Just watches me eat toast and swallow Advil with the deliberate slowness of a person whose throat isn't cooperating.

"You were pretty gone last night," he says finally.

"Yeah."

"Like, pretty far gone. More than I've seen you."

"Yeah."

"You said some stuff."

My stomach drops. "What stuff?"

"Nothing bad. Just — stuff. About your dad. About wanting to be better." He pauses. "You said 'I'm running out of time' like three times."

I close my eyes. The couch is soft. The afghan smells like fabric softener. Marcus brought me water and Advil and toast and he's sitting across from me now with no judgment in his face, just that steady watchfulness, that patience, that impossible kindness that I do not deserve and have never deserved and will spend the rest of my life trying to be worthy of.

"Thanks," I say. "For — this. All of it."

"Yeah, man." He shrugs. "That's what friends do."

The kindness is worse than judgment. Judgment I could work with. Judgment gives you something to push against, something to be angry at, something that transforms your shame into a reaction instead of a feeling. Kindness just sits there. Kindness just hands you toast and waits.

---

December. The days get short. The sun sets at 4:45 and the campus of UNI goes gray and the streets freeze and Cedar Falls becomes the kind of cold that lives in your bones, the kind that makes every building you enter feel like a rescue.

I take inventory.

In the original timeline, the drinking escalated through senior year. Not dramatically — not in a way anyone noticed or worried about. Just steadily. Two parties a week became three. The six-pack in the trunk became a twelve-pack. The hangovers became a texture of daily life, a background hum, something I managed rather than something that managed me. It didn't feel like a problem because it didn't look like a problem, and in a town where half the dads have a cooler in the garage and half the moms have wine o'clock, the boundary between normal and dangerous is drawn in invisible ink.

This is the fork. Right here, right now, December 2008. This is where the road splits, and one path leads to the version of me that shows up to Frank's funeral drunk and the other path leads to — what? I don't know. Something different. Something that hurts differently.

I choose the other path. Not through willpower. Not through some epiphany or moment of clarity or Hollywood resolution where I pour the beer down the drain in slow motion while meaningful music plays. I choose it through terror. Pure, animal terror. I know where the other road goes. I've walked it for eighteen years. I've seen the end of it — not the dramatic end, not rock bottom, just the long slow erosion of every relationship and every possibility and every version of yourself that might have been worth something.

I stop going to parties. This is easier than it should be, because seventeen-year-old social dynamics are surprisingly forgiving of withdrawal — people assume you're grounded, or busy, or dating someone from another school. Marcus asks once if I'm okay. I say yes. He accepts this.

I start running. In December. In Iowa. It's idiotic and painful and the air burns my lungs and my nose runs and my ears ache even through a hat, and it is exactly what I need — a thing that is hard and physical and produces discomfort that I chose, rather than discomfort that chose me. I run three miles, then four, then five. The route goes down University, past the Hy-Vee, along the bike path by Dry Run Creek. My body is seventeen and it adapts fast, faster than it has any right to, and within two weeks the runs are less punishment and more — something else. A reset. A clearing of the static.

Late January. Eleven PM. Derek Holt's kitchen table.

We're studying for the AP Government midterm, which is in two days, and which I could pass in my sleep but need to appear to study for because inexplicable academic excellence is almost as suspicious as inexplicable election predictions. Derek's house is smaller than I expected — a two-bedroom ranch on the west side of town, closer to Waterloo than to the university. The kitchen is clean but sparse. No magnets on the fridge. No photos on the walls. The overhead light has a fixture with one dead bulb that nobody has replaced.

Derek's mom is working — she works two jobs, I've gathered, though Derek has never said this directly. His dad isn't in the picture, or isn't in this house, or isn't in a way that's easily explained. These are things I'm assembling from context clues: the two-bedroom with one empty room, the sparse fridge, the way Derek moves through his own house like a guest.

"The Commerce Clause," Derek says. He's quizzing me from flashcards. "Give me the main expansion cases."

"Gibbons v. Ogden. Wickard v. Filburn. Heart of Atlanta Motel. Gonzales v. Raich."

"Good." He flips a card. Then stops. Sets the stack down. Leans back in his chair.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Why are you slumming it in this class?"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean you know this stuff cold. You've known it cold since day one. You never take notes, you never look confused, and on the one group project we did you wrote a campaign speech in twenty minutes that Mr. Kowalski read to his other sections as an example." He looks at me with that sharp expression, the calculating one. "So what's the play? Why pretend to be average?"

I should have an answer for this. I should have prepared an answer for this. But it's eleven PM and I'm tired and Derek is looking at me with an intelligence that doesn't miss things, and I'm suddenly aware that I've been underestimating him.

"I just pay attention," I say. "It sticks."

"Bullshit." He says it without heat. Observational. "But okay. Keep your secrets."

He picks up the flashcards again. We study for another twenty minutes. Then, around 11:30, he gets up to make instant coffee — the cheap kind, Folgers crystals — and while he's standing at the counter waiting for the microwave to heat the water, he says:

"My dad lost the house."

He says it to the microwave. Not to me.

"Last year. The adjustable rate thing. Mom told him not to do the adjustable rate. He did the adjustable rate." He takes the mug out. Stirs. "Now he's renting an apartment on Ridgeway and my mom works at the Hy-Vee deli during the day and cleans offices at night and I'm going to get a scholarship or I'm going to get a job or I'm going to figure something out, because nobody is coming to save us."

He turns around. His face is perfectly composed. This isn't vulnerability — it's a statement of fact. A briefing.

"Money is the only thing that's real," he says. "Everything else is what people tell themselves to feel better about not having it."

I hear my own voice. Not literally — but the architecture of the sentence, the defensive certainty of it, the way it forecloses on hope as a strategy for avoiding disappointment. This is what I sounded like at twenty-five, at thirty, at thirty-five. This is the philosophy I built a life on, and the life I built was empty, and the emptiness was load-bearing — remove it and the whole structure comes down.

"That's not true," I say.

"Yeah?" He sits back down. "Name something that's real that isn't money."

I think about Frank on the porch. I think about Marcus on the bench at Byrnes Park, saying *bridges — they connect things.* I think about Gloria Wade crying with a dish towel in her hands on election night. I think about Sophie's geometry homework and Diane's pomegranate juice and the way the light looked in September, gold to amber to gone.

"People showing up," I say. "That's real."

Derek looks at me for a long time. Then he smiles — and it's not the sharp smile, not the strategic one. It's tired and young and unconvinced but not dismissive.

"Must be nice to believe that," he says.

He goes back to the flashcards. I drink my bad instant coffee and I think about how Derek Holt is going to be fine — he's too smart and too driven not to be — but he's going to be fine in the way I was fine. Functional. Successful, maybe. And completely alone in the ways that count.

I don't know how to help him. I don't know if I can. You can't save someone from a worldview by arguing with it. You can only show them a different one, and even then, they have to choose it.

February. The library.

I'm at the Cedar Falls Public Library three afternoons a week now. This started as a way to avoid parties and has become something else — a project, or the beginning of one, or at least the research phase of the beginning of one.

I don't know how to stop an alien invasion. That sentence is absurd no matter how many times I think it, and I've thought it approximately four hundred times since September. I have no military expertise, no scientific background, no connections to anyone with power or knowledge. I'm a seventeen-year-old in Iowa with a GPA that's improving but not exceptional, and my only asset is knowledge of an event that won't happen for eighteen years, knowledge I can't share with anyone without being institutionalized.

But I know something else. I know what happened *after.* I know how the emergency systems failed. I know that the communication infrastructure collapsed within hours. I know that the evacuation routes jammed because nobody had planned for concurrent mass displacement in every direction. I know that the people who survived the first week survived because of organization, not strength — because someone had thought about water purification, or communication without infrastructure, or how to move fifty people efficiently when every road is a parking lot.

I can't stop the Kessari from coming. I'm not an astrophysicist. I'm not a general. But I can learn how to keep people alive after they get here.

Emergency management. Disaster science. Organizational resilience.

The Cedar Falls library has a surprisingly decent section on emergency preparedness, buried between the reference section and the large-print mysteries. FEMA publications. Red Cross manuals. A textbook called *Fundamentals of Emergency Management* that smells like 1997 and hasn't been checked out in four years. I read it in three days.

I read about incident command systems and emergency operation centers. I read about continuity of operations planning. I read about crisis communication and resource allocation and the difference between a disaster and a catastrophe, which is scope — a disaster overwhelms local resources; a catastrophe overwhelms regional ones. What's coming in 2026 doesn't have a category. There isn't a word for something that overwhelms planetary resources simultaneously.

But the principles scale. That's what I keep coming back to. The principles of keeping people alive — clean water, shelter, communication, medical triage, organized evacuation — don't change whether you're managing a tornado in Waterloo or something from outside the solar system. The logistics get harder. The scale gets insane. But the bones are the same.

I check out six books. I fill a notebook — a plain composition book from the drugstore, black and white marble cover, nothing that would attract attention if someone found it. I write in a shorthand only I can read. Timelines. Resource chains. Communication protocols. The bones of a plan that doesn't exist yet, that won't exist for years, that might never work.

But it's something. It's better than knowing the end is coming and doing nothing. It's better than sitting in the dark and drinking and letting the weight of it crush me one day at a time.

February 14th. Sophie's birthday.

She turns fourteen. Diane makes German chocolate cake because it's Sophie's favorite, and Frank puts up a banner in the dining room — the same banner they've used since Sophie was six, the one that says HAPPY BIRTHDAY in uneven glitter letters that Sophie made herself and has become too old for but won't let them throw away.

I agonize over the gift for a week. In the original timeline, I got her a gift card. A Best Buy gift card, twenty-five dollars, because I didn't know what she wanted and didn't bother to find out. She said thank you and her voice was perfectly neutral and I didn't hear the neutrality for what it was, which was disappointment so practiced it had become invisible.

This time I listen. I've been listening since September, cataloging the small things, the offhand comments, the background data of my sister's life that I was too self-absorbed to register the first time through.

Three weeks ago, Sophie was in the living room reading a library copy of a book called *The Book Thief* by Markus Zusak. She was on the couch, legs tucked under her, completely gone into it — that reading posture where the world disappears and the book becomes the only real thing. She looked up when I walked through and said, without context, "Death narrates this. Like, actual Death. Capital D. And it's beautiful?" Like she was surprised that a thing could work that way. Like she'd discovered a new color.

I got her a copy. Hardcover, from the bookstore downtown — the independent one on Main Street that smells like paper and survives on stubborn principle. I also got her *I Am the Messenger* by the same author, because if she loved *The Book Thief* she'll love that too, and because buying someone the next thing they'd like is a different act than buying someone a thing you'd like them to have.

She opens it at the dining room table. Diane and Frank are watching. The cake is half-eaten. Sophie pulls back the wrapping paper and looks at the two books and her face does something I haven't seen it do in my direction in — either lifetime, actually. A specific kind of surprise. The surprise of being known.

"You listened?" she says.

"I'm trying to."

She looks at me. Fourteen years old, braces, ponytail, hoodie. That expression — the too-old one, the one that misses nothing. Something in it shifts. Not all the way to trust. Not yet. But the wariness recedes by half a degree, the way a tide pulls back and leaves a little more sand visible.

"Thanks, Nate," she says. She holds the books against her chest.

"Happy birthday, Soph."

Diane is smiling at me from across the table with an expression I can't hold eye contact with, so I look at my cake instead.

Three days later. Wednesday evening. I come downstairs for water and there's a UNI pamphlet on the kitchen table.

University of Northern Iowa. The college that's literally in this town, walking distance, so close that we grew up hearing the marching band practice on fall Saturdays. The pamphlet is glossy, tri-fold, the kind they stack in guidance counselor offices and hand out at college fairs. On the cover, students walk across a green campus that I've seen ten thousand times from the outside and never once imagined myself inside.

It's positioned at my spot. Not the center of the table — my spot. The place I sit for breakfast, between Frank's end and the wall. Placed there with the front facing up, readable, unmistakable.

I pick it up. The corners are slightly bent — dented, actually, in that way paper gets when someone has been holding it too tightly. Gripped. Frank has big hands with thick fingers and calluses from thirty years of pipe fitting, and when he holds something small — a pamphlet, a photograph, a letter — his grip tends to crumple the edges. He doesn't know he does this. Diane has mentioned it gently approximately fifty times.

He gripped this pamphlet. He picked it up somewhere — the guidance office, the library, the table at the Hy-Vee where they leave community materials — and he brought it home and he gripped it while deciding where to put it and his fingers dented the corners and he set it at my spot and he walked away without saying anything.

Because Frank Callahan does not say things. Frank Callahan leaves pamphlets on kitchen tables. Frank Callahan stays on porches and drives eight-minute rides and shows up to the room and hopes that his presence communicates what his mouth cannot.

I stand in the kitchen, holding the pamphlet. The house is quiet. Frank's in the living room — I can hear the television, low, something with a laugh track. Diane's upstairs. Sophie's in her room, probably reading one of the books I gave her.

I put the pamphlet in my backpack. I zip the backpack closed. I fill my water glass and I go back upstairs.

I don't mention it.

Neither does Frank.

But the next morning at breakfast, I catch him glancing at the table — at the spot where the pamphlet was — and when he sees it's gone, he doesn't smile. He just nods, once, to himself, and goes back to his Cheerios. And I sit down across from him and pour pomegranate juice that's full of antioxidants and I don't say anything either, because sometimes the truest conversations are the ones that never happen out loud.
