Paper outlasted flesh; ink outlasted breath. That, too, was a kind of immortality, cheaper than the one they offered, but honest.

The figures receded, folding into darkness the way waves fold into fog. The fire had died; only the beacon’s indirect glow remained, sweeping across curtain edges, counting time they no longer paid for. Alden closed the logbook, held it against his chest. His heartbeat slowed, accepting the new mortgage. Outside, gulls began their pre-dawn mutter. He listened, counting their cries the way other men count beads, until the room paled and the night’s visitation felt as distant as the freighter’s death knell.

When the sun finally edged above the horizon, painting the lantern room gold, Alden rose. He moved like a man underwater, joints stiff with borrowed years. At the window he raised the shade. The Atlantic stretched immaculate, reef empty, life-ring gone. Only the grave’s white stones winked back, faithful audience. He touched the glass, felt cold transfer from world to skin, and spoke the first sentence of the new day: “One less dawn to pay for.” The words fogged the pane, then faded, leaving him alone with the light that still owed him life, and the life that still owed the light.

The Assembly recoiled, salt prints smearing as if washed by upward rain. For an instant their faces clarified—not monsters, not drowned sailors, but mirrors: his own grief given outline, Maura’s absence shaped into silhouette, Silas’s cowardice cloaked in transparent skin. Then the wind hit—a solid wall of moving air that slapped the tower sideways. The gallery door burst open, salt spray hosing across the floor. Marisol lost her footing; the crank spun reverse, gears shrieking. Alden lunged, catching the handle before momentum ripped it free. The backlash snapped through his wrists, elbows, shoulders, but he held on, tendon and bone and stubborn will. Light first, last, always—yet the creed no longer felt like shackle. It felt like spine.

“Help me!” he shouted—not to Marisol, not to Silas’s ghost, but to the Assembly themselves. “Share the weight or fade!”

He felt them answer—not in words but in lessening. The next rotation came easier, as if invisible hands had joined theirs, palms laid over knuckles, splitting the tithe. Six weeks became three, then two, pain diffused across multiple heartbeats. The beam steadied, slicing through storm and grief alike, a blade that cut both keeper and kept.

The last thing he heard was Maura’s voice—not the counterfeit echo from shells, but the real timbre, the way she used to call his name across the cove when supper was ready: Alden, come home. And he understood, finally, that home was not the past. It was the next breath, the next rotation, the next hand offered in shared darkness.

Storm peak hit then—anvil cloud dropping seawater in sheets, wind howling like every ghost given throat. The tower shuddered but held, a stone fist raised against the sky. Alden collapsed against the housing, chest heaving. Marisol slid down beside him, shoulder to shoulder, both too spent for victory grins. They simply breathed, two hearts counting eight-second intervals in counterpoint to the light.

Outside, the Assembly melted back into surf, taking their handprints, their debts, their promises of eternal preservation. The beam swept on, clean and unburdened, guiding nothing visible yet everything that mattered: two people, one island, and the long, patient work of continuing.

When Alden could speak, his voice cracked but remained his own. “Next shift,” he said, “we trade off. One winds, one rests. Shared vigilance.”

Marisol’s answering nod was small, almost imperceptible, yet it felt like covenant. She tilted her head against the brass housing, eyes closing. “You’re still an idiot,” she murmured. “But you’re my idiot now. Ours.”

The word settled between them, warm as lamp oil. Ours. Not keeper and intruder, not duty and desperation, but something negotiated under storm light, paid for in honest ache. Alden let his own eyes close. In the dark behind lids he saw no ghosts, no idealized Maura, only the slow wheel of beam across water, guiding and letting go, guiding and letting go, the way a heart learned to beat again after grief had stopped it.

The storm raged on, but the tower stood, and inside it two hearts kept time with the light, sharing the cost of being seen.
