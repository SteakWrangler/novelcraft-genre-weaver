CHAPTER 1: Rocks and Hard Places
(3,519 words)
------------------------------------------------------------

The rock left Teo’s broomstick with a flat crack, nothing like the aluminum ping he craved, but the sound still felt official inside his head—like a scoreboard firing to life. It sailed over the listing chain-link fence, clipped the top, and vanished into the neighbor’s yard where the Rottweiler named Tía growled at everything that moved. Teo counted one-thousand-one, one-thousand-two, same as the YouTube coach with the Dodgers cap. The rock clattered against stucco right on beat. Home-run distance, no doubt.

He wiped sweat from the dust smudge on his right cheek and reset: feet wider than shoulders, knob pointed at the pitcher, hips firing first. Another rock—this one shaped like New Mexico if you squinted—waited on the cracked patio slab he used for home plate. Behind him, the Vega house hummed like a beehive somebody had poked with a broom handle. Twin Number One wailed, Twin Number Two answered an octave higher, Sofia shouted something about milk, and Diego’s cleats—two sizes too big—thudded down the hall in pursuit of a Pokémon he swore had slipped under the bathroom door. Normal Tuesday. Normal chaos.

Teo tuned it the way other kids noise-cancelled with headphones; he pictured a volume slider inside his chest and dragged it left. The stick felt heavier than last week, probably because he’d outgrown another growth spurt and the muscles along his forearms ached in a good way. He tapped the rock, once, twice, then exploded. The broomstick whooshed, connected, and pain zapped his fingers—sting he catalogued the way Willow Chen later would with her purple notebook. Foul tip off the handle. Rock skittered into the dead tumbleweed he’d dragged over for a backstop.

He shook out his hands, refusing to grimace. If Coach Ramirez had been watching, he’d have said, “That’s the sound of learning, hijo.” But Coach wasn’t here. Nobody was, which made the backyard the only place Teo could rehearse perfection without anybody asking for a ride, a diaper, or a translation of the water bill. He exhaled, squared again, and this time pictured Liam Hughes’s smug face on the mound at Rancho Caballo—hair the color of Mountain Dew Code Red, composite bat polished like showroom chrome. Liam always looked like the baseball card version of himself. Teo hated that almost as much as he respected it.

Crack. Sweet spot. The rock screamed over Tía’s yard, cleared the dog, and pinged off the crooked TV antenna the previous owners never bothered removing. Antenna rocked like a metronome. Teo allowed himself one fist pump—small, quick, nothing that would look stupid if anyone had been filming. He noted the distance in the mental spreadsheet he kept beside the batting-order he’d never yet batted leadoff in. Forty-eight antenna lengths equaled roughly 280 feet. Not his longest, but respectable for a rock.

From the kitchen window came a crash, followed by his mom’s voice, stretched thin: “¡Mateo! If that perro comes over the fence, you’re explaining to Mrs. Ortega why her roses look like Swiss cheese.”

“Wasn’t even close!” he shouted back, though the lie felt harmless. He glanced at the sky—sun still pinned high, which meant maybe an hour before the twins’ nap, which meant maybe Sofia would drive him to Veterans Park if he finished chores and if the van started and if the world stopped spinning long enough for luck to stick. Too many ifs. He bent, selected the next rock—smooth, skipping-stone flat—and set it on the slab.

He was dragging his foot forward when the screen door squealed. Diego appeared, Teo’s old batting gloves dangling from his belt loop like twin raccoon tails. The gloves were sized Youth Large; Diego was eight going on matchstick. “Sofia says you gotta watch the babies while she walks to Dollar General for diapers.”

Teo’s shoulders tightened. “She promised she’d take me to practice.”

“She promised she’d take me to Dollar General,” Diego corrected, proud of the logic. Behind him, Twin Number One launched a fresh siren. Diego winced. “Also, Sofía said if you argue, she’s posting that video of you singing ‘Let It Go’ while changing Lucía.”

Teo felt heat crawl up his neck. Blackmail was Sofia’s love language. He leaned the broomstick against the house, next to the bucket of rocks he’d collected from the arroyo after last week’s flash flood. Flood had rearranged the neighborhood like Lego pieces, but it also delivered premium ammunition—river stones worn egg-smooth, perfect for simulating curveball resistance.

Inside, the living room smelled like formula, roasted chile, and the lemony wipe his mom used on everything. Twin Number One—Lucía—kicked in a bouncer, face the color of a ripe tomato. Twin Number Two—Mateo Jr., called M.J. before he could object—wiggled on a blanket, fascinated by ceiling fan shadows. Sofia stood at the door juggling her phone, a diaper pack, and the house key dangling from a lanyard of plastic baseballs. She wore the sarcastic smile that came with deputy-parent duty. “You’re on point, slugger. Forty-five minutes, tops. Don’t lose anybody.”

“Hard to lose something that can’t crawl,” Teo muttered, but he sank to his knees, scooped Lucía, and felt her sobs drop from siren to hiccup against his shoulder. She smelled like warm milk and the lavender lotion their mom bought when the budget allowed a splurge. He rocked without thinking, the same sway the YouTube coach said to use in the box—controlled glide, weight back, release at the right moment.

Sofia paused, studying him. “You know the marshal’s driving around handing out curfew warnings. If he spots you outside without an adult, Mom pays the fine out of your cleat fund.”

“I’ll be inside,” Teo said, though his heart crumpled at the thought of missing another practice. Coach Ramirez kept a clipboard of attendance; miss three and you batted last, no exceptions. Teo was at two.

The door clicked shut. Outside, a freight train moaned through the sagging depot three blocks over, the sound long and lonely, like the town itself exhaling. Teo counted boxcars the way other kids counted sheep. Lucía’s breathing synced with the rhythm, and for a minute the house felt almost peaceful. Then M.J. discovered his foot and squealed loud enough to wake the neighbor’s chickens.

Teo lowered Lucía into the bouncer, wedged the pacifier, and scooped M.J. Sky-blue eyes locked on him as if he were the most fascinating thing in the universe, better than ceiling fans or shiny wrappers. The look punched a hole in Teo’s chest. He spun once, slow, and M.J. giggled, tiny fingers grabbing air. “You’re easy,” Teo whispered. “Wait till you figure out the world’s got curveballs.”

He set the baby down, retrieved the broomstick, and practiced shadow swings in the living-room mirror cracked along one corner. The reflection showed a tall-for-eleven kid with hair that refused to lie flat, a clay smudge on his cheek, and a left shoelace frayed into white spaghetti no matter how many knots he tied. He imagined pinstripes instead of the faded T-shirt that said ¡Viva Chile! in cracked letters. He imagined ESPN instead of Sofia’s Spotify playlist leaking from the kitchen.

Lucía started up again. Teo sighed, placed the bat against the couch, and remembered what Coach said about patience: “You can’t muscle a changeup, same as you can’t rush a teething baby.” Coach didn’t have five siblings, but the analogy felt sturdy enough.

By the time Sofia returned—sweaty, diaper pack victorious, twenty-seven minutes instead of forty-five—Teo had both twins asleep on a blanket fort he’d engineered from couch cushions and an old Rattlers banner he’d found in the garage. He was crouched beside them tapping a rock against his palm, quietly practicing grip changes the way Willow had demonstrated at the last game: two-seam, four-seam, splitter you weren’t supposed to throw until high school.

Sofia stopped, arms full, and for once her expression held zero sarcasm. “Not bad, Vega.”

He shrugged. “They like the train noises.”

She dropped her voice. “Van started on the second try. If you hustle, we can make the last forty minutes of practice.”

He was already moving, snatching the glove from the floor, stuffing rocks into his backpack because real baseballs cost money and the bucket of range balls at Veterans Park required a parent signature. He paused at the door. “You’re coming in with me, right?”

“Somebody’s gotta haul your cheering section,” Sofia said, which was as close to yes as she ever gave.

They tiptoed out, leaving the twins under the banner’s painted rattlesnake coiled around a bat. The screen door thumped. Heat slapped Teo’s face, but it felt like applause. He jogged to the van, shoelace flapping, and for the first time that afternoon the volume slider inside his chest cranked right, loud enough to drown out doubt.

Sofia cranked the engine, radio coughing up mariachi. She reversed onto Calle Del Sol, past houses the color of sand and sunset, past yard sprinklers hissing static, past the water tower painted like a baseball whose seams were sun-peeled into abstract art. At the stop sign she glanced at him. “You’re riding the bench if you’re late, you know.”

“I’ll hit bombs no matter where I bat,” Teo answered, because confidence was cheaper than batting gloves.

She snorted but smiled, and the van rolled toward Veterans Park, freight train still moaning somewhere ahead and behind, like the town itself keeping score of who stayed and who left and who found a way to belong to something bigger than their own highlight reel.

The van’s air-conditioning was broken—had been since the twins came early and the repair money turned into formula—so Sofia rolled both windows down. Hot wind whipped through, carrying the smell of roasted green chile from the food-truck lot outside the Walmart, mixing with exhaust and the faint iron scent of the rail line. Teo leaned his elbow out, fingers splayed so the wind could play them like piano keys. He watched the town slide by: the abandoned depot with its boarded windows tagged in looping neon spray, the Popeye’s where Jayden’s mom worked the drive-thru, the irrigation ditch where kids were strictly forbidden to swim but did anyway because the river was the only thing deeper than summer boredom.

Sofia tapped the brakes. “Look alive, superstar.”

Up ahead, the marshal’s cruiser idled beside Veterans Park’s chain-link, deputy half-out the window chatting with a cluster of parents. Teo’s stomach dipped. He checked his watch: 5:42. Practice ended at six. Still time to log official presence if Coach saw him step onto the dirt before the horn.

Sofia slowed, fingers drumming the wheel. “If he asks, you were with me buying diapers, capisce?”

“Capisco,” Teo answered, though the Italian felt weird in his mouth. He rehearsed an excuse anyway—something about traffic on the blacktop, about the Walmart light that stayed red for generations—then decided silence played stronger. Coach appreciated brevity the same way he appreciated hustle.

They rolled past the cruiser. Deputy Ortega—Mrs. Ortega’s nephew, same bulldog cheeks—lifted two fingers off the wheel in greeting. Sofia smiled sweet as agua fresca and eased into the gravel lot. Dust plumed, powdering the side of the van white. Teo was already unbuckling before she parked.

“Bat bag,” she reminded.

He doubled back, snatched it from the floorboard where twin diapers had spilled like foam. The zipper caught on something—maybe the pacifier clip, maybe fate—but he yanked free and sprinted, shoelace whipping. Behind him, Sofia shouted, “I’ll be in the bleachers doing homework—don’t embarrass me.”

The park opened like a flower: four diamonds carved into caliche so hard it could skin a knee through sliding pants, outfield fences patched with billboard vinyl—Mama’s Tamales, Silva & Sons Roofing—scoreboards flickering 0-0-0 because the town’s electricity couldn’t decide on steady voltage. Mesquite shadows striped the dirt. Overhead, the LED scoreboard at the major field glowed RATTLESS in half-lit letters; the missing R had been dead three seasons.

Teo’s cleats crunched the warning track of Field Three, where the Rattlers gathered. Coach Ramirez stood at the cage feeding balls, each crack from the machine echoing off the water tower like a second gunshot. Players dotted the grass: Jayden stretching while scrolling his phone, Mountain Dew hair bright as caution tape; Marisol long-tossing with a velocity that made her ponytail snap; Willow crouched behind a tripod, phone angled to chart exit velocity. She wore the purple notebook tucked under her knee like a security blanket.

Teo hesitated at the gate, chest heaving. Forty yards of shame separated him from the team—forty yards of being late, again, of proving unreliable. He almost turned around. Then Jayden spotted him, grin wide enough to catch rain. “Look what the stork dragged in! You’re just in time for the firing squad.”

Coach glanced up, face unreadable beneath the faded cap. He tapped the machine, stopping the wheels. “Vega, you’re in the hole. Hustle.”

No lecture, no sigh—just the next assignment. Relief flooded Teo so fast his knees felt liquid. He jogged to the on-deck circle, dropped bag, tugged on helmet. The chinstrap smelled of pine tar and somebody’s bubble gum, communal odors that meant team. Jayden clapped his back. “Nice of you to grace us with your majestic lateness.”

“Traffic,” Teo lied.

“On a road made of dust? Sure.” Jayden’s grin stayed bright, no edge. “I saved you seeds.” He poured sunflower shells into Teo’s palm like communion. Salt and earth. Teo popped a handful, cheeks bulbing, and felt something loosen in his chest.

Up at the plate, Willow’s swing looked scientific: short to the ball, one-handed finish, body tilted like a laboratory scale. She lined a shot up the middle, machine spitting another ball before the first left the cage. Coach nodded, jotted. When Willow exited, she flicked eyes toward Teo—cool, appraising. “Exit velo ninety-two. Your turn to beat it.”

“Stats are for post-game,” Teo replied, but he felt the challenge burrow. He stepped in, tapped the rubber mat serving as home plate, imagined Liam’s cocky smirk. The machine wheezed. First pitch: thigh-high, inside. He ripped, ball skying foul beyond first. Sting sang in his palms.

“Stay gap-to-gap,” Coach called. “This ain’t the Home-Run Derby.”

Second pitch: belt high, middle. Teo’s hips fired early, swing uphill, ball topping toward short. He gritted teeth, stepped out, adjusted grip. Behind the L-screen, Coach’s eyes never left him. Third pitch: outside half. He shortened, drove through center. The ball smacked net with a sound like a pillow fight—no majestic arc, just a firm line drive. Exactly what Coach wanted. Exactly what he hated settling for.

He fouled the next two off, then laced another liner. Coach killed the machine. “That’s five. Good enough. Field Three, right field. Relay station.”

Teo started to protest—he wanted another round, wanted the feel of perfect contact—but Jayden was already jogging out, waving him along. They crossed the baseline together. Dust puffed, coating Teo’s cleats the color of drought.

In right, Marisol waved him to shallow center. “Shade toward gap,” she instructed. “Coach is simulating Rancho’s spray chart.” She spoke softly, voice always seeming to come from a cave of calm. Her brother’s Army bracelet flashed as she adjusted her cap. Teo wondered what it felt like to have a sibling overseas instead of underfoot. He almost asked, but Coach cracked a fungo and the first fly ball sliced their way.

Jayden called, “Got it!” Teo slowed, watching Jayden’s route—textbook angle, two hands, chest-high funnel. The next ball lofted to Teo. He camped under, felt sun glare, squeezed the catch. Leather felt expensive; he still used the nine-dollar Walmart model Mom bought on clearance. He fired the ball to cutoff man Jayden, arm slot low. Ball one-hopped the target. Jayden turned, mimicked a throw home. Routine complete. Team drill. No heroics.

They rotated: left, center, right. Each repetition a sentence in a paragraph Teo had never bothered reading before. After ten flies, Coach clapped. “Baserunning circuit. Vega, you lead off from second. Chen, signals. Let’s see a relay.”

Baserunning meant Willie, the team manager’s beagle mix, sprinted the paths too, tongue flapping. Teo laughed despite himself. Jayden pushed him toward second. “Time to use those jetpacks.”

Teo crouched, fingers drumming dirt. Coach hit a single to right. Teo burst, rounding third, eyes on Willow standing at the mound as cutoff. She flashed both palms—hold. He stutter-stepped, slid back to bag. Next hit: gap. Willow windmilled—go. Teo scored easily, popped up, brushed dirt. Routine. Simple. Team coordinating without words. He felt something click, like finding an extra gear in a video game.

When the horn finally blared—a wheeze from the marshal’s ancient Klaxon—sun was kissing the mesa rim orange. Players gathered at the bench. Coach removed cap, ran fingers through hair salted gray. “Good work. Remember, Saturday we bus to Los Lunas. Be here six sharp. No excuses.” His gaze lingered on Teo, not angry, just planting a flag. Teo nodded—message received.

Jayden slung an arm around him. “Ride? My mom’s grabbing Baskin-Robbins after she closes the drive-thru window. Extra scoop if you shotgun the waffle fries.”

Teo glanced toward the stands. Sofia sat hunched over a chemistry textbook, phone flashlight propped like a tiny lamp. She’d driven; etiquette said she drove home. But she also loved Baskin-Robbins. He hesitated, weighing obligation against sugar. “Let me ask.”

They walked across the infield together, cleats printing history behind them. Dust rose, caught the fading light, turned the air bronze. Overhead, the first bats flickered, chasing bugs Teo couldn’t see. Somewhere, a freight train moaned, steel wheels clicking like a metronome keeping time for a town that never quite left the station.

Sofia looked up as they approached, eyebrow arched. “Practice end early, or did you get cut?”

“Coach said six,” Teo answered. “Jayden’s mom has ice cream.”

Sofia’s stomach growled audibly, betraying her. She slapped textbook shut. “I’m not a taxi service, but I’m also not made of stone.” She eyed Jayden. “You sure your mom’s okay dragging an extra Vega?”

Jayden saluted. “Mom loves strays. She keeps chicken nuggets in the console for emergencies.”

Sofia laughed—real, surprised sound. “Fine. Shotgun calls stereo rights.”

They caravanned: Jayden’s mom in the Popeye’s-scented SUV, Sofia following, windows down, mariachi competing with top-forty. At the drive-thru, Jayden’s mom handed back a paper bag steaming grease. “For my outfielders,” she said. Teo accepted a fry like a trophy, salt stinging cracked lips. In the rearview, her eyes smiled the way his mom’s did when overtime ended early.

At the creamery, they claimed a picnic table under a string of solar lights flickering cricket shadows. Jayden stacked three scoops—Code Red, birthday cake, something neon—then offered the spoon to Teo. “Communion of future champions.”

Teo took a bite, brain freeze blooming like fireworks behind his eyes. He laughed—first time in weeks the sound wasn’t tied to hitting something. Willow and Marisol pulled up on bikes, helmets dotted with electrode pads from the batting cage sensor lab Willow had conned the science teacher into funding. They joined without asking, spoons clinking. Conversation orbited around Saturday’s opponent: Los Lunas’ lefty who supposedly threw 70 but only on odd-numbered days, their catcher who called every pitch a strike until the umpire disagreed aloud.

Sofia sat beside Teo, textbook forgotten. She licked mint-chip, gaze soft. “You looked good out there,” she said quietly. “Less like you were auditioning for SportsCenter.”

He shrugged, but warmth spread unrelated to sugar. “Felt… easier.”

“That’s called belonging, knucklehead.” She nudged his shoulder. “Try it more often.”

Across the lot, the marshal’s cruiser prowled slow, checking curfew. Deputy Ortega lifted a hand in recognition; Teo lifted his spoon back, a truce sealed in dairy. The lights flickered again, then steadied, tiny suns holding back the desert night.

Later, rides home dispersed like post-game fireflies. Jayden’s mom dropped Teo last. The Vega house glowed porch-yellow, twins quiet for once, Dad’s work van absent—probably a late plumbing call. Inside, Mom had left a plate of tamales under foil on the stove, note in her tidy script: For the slugger, heat 45 sec, flip halfway. Love you bigger than the tower. Teo smiled, microwaved, ate standing over the sink, grease running wrists. He thought about the relay drill, about Willow’s signals, about ice cream baptized in stadium light.

Upstairs, he pinned a new index card above his bed: Antenna + 5. He’d clear that fence someday—maybe Saturday, maybe next month—but for the first time the number felt like a conversation starter, not a solitary language. He brushed teeth, tied the frayed lace into a triple knot that would unravel by morning, and crawled under the sheet printed with tiny baseballs. Through the wall, Lucía stirred, cried once, quieted. Train horn drifted, long and low, carrying somebody else’s dreams toward Albuquerque or beyond.

Before sleep, Teo pictured Field Three under lights: his teammates scattered like constellations, each star connected by invisible lines of chalk and trust. He saw himself hitting the cutoff, saw the ball popping the glove, saw the runner held, saw the bench erupting—not for his glory but for their collective win. The image settled, warm as tamale steam, and held him until breathing slowed and the desert outside exhaled, finally, into cool night.

CHAPTER 2: Late Arrival, Early Tension
(1,800 words)
------------------------------------------------------------

Teo Vega’s alarm chirped at 4:47 a.m.—thirteen minutes early because he’d yanked the plug halfway out so the twins couldn’t smack snooze. The house on Calle Del Sol stayed dark, but the refrigerator hummed like a distant crowd and a freight train scraped metal through Las Piedras, the town’s own batting-cage coin drop.

He rolled off the couch—bottom bunk now belonged to the baby—and landed palms-first on cool tile. Left shoelace frayed; he triple-knotted the antennae ends. Clay smudge on his cheek—permanent passport—stayed put. In the dim kitchen he tiptoed past cereal bowls fossilized with Cheerios and Sofia’s geometry worksheet taped to the cabinet like another shut-off notice. He opened the junk drawer, fished past screwdrivers and pink gas bills until his fingers closed around the broomstick—Louisville Slugger stand-in, grip polished by a thousand backyard rocks.

Outside, desert night still owned the yard. Crickets sawed; the neighbor’s irrigation hissed static. Teo dragged the trash can to the cracked patio, lined three Coke cans on the lid—Diego, Lucía, baby M.J. in aluminum—and stepped off twenty paces. The mesa wind hadn’t clocked in yet; air felt held-back, like a pitcher shaking off signs.

Tap against his sneaker. Coach Ramirez’s phantom baritone: Load the hips, Teo. Don’t drift. You’re launching rockets, not swinging swords.  
Yeah, yeah. He palmed a ping-pong-sized rock, tossed it skyward, and whipped the stick. Crack. The rock screamed over the chain-link, kissed the water tower painted like a baseball, and vanished. Home run number—he’d quit counting weeks ago.

Second rock: liner past the imaginary second-baseman’s ear. Third: grounder up the middle—he sprinted anyway, tagging first: the broken sprinkler, second: the dead apricot, third: the leaning post, home: the lid that clanged like an aluminum crown. He slid, knees grinding grit into denim, pulse drumming. The crowd in his head roared.

“Teo?” Sofia’s voice cracked through the kitchen window. “Some of us non-superheroes need the bathroom.”

He waved her off. She stayed silhouetted, hair frizzed from twin warfare. “Practice starts at nine. Four hours to save the world, Babe Ruth.”

He wanted to snap—Babe Ruth never babysat five siblings—but another rock was already airborne. Crack. Moonshot. Sofia sighed; the porch light died, leaving him alone with scoreboard stars.

Four hours. Enough to polish the swing that would make Coach forget he was late, make Willow quit auditing launch angles, make Mom proud enough to ask for Saturday off. Enough to prove he didn’t need anybody.

Tap, load, explode. Rocks peppered the tower like hail. Somewhere during the seventh inning of imagination, the sky blushed lavender. A mockingbird stole signals. Teo’s shirt stuck to him, salt-stiff. He didn’t stop until the trash can wobbled empty and his shoulders buzzed like high-voltage lines.

Inside, the Vega house had shifted into morning chaos. Diego wore Teo’s old Rattlers cap, brim curved so tight it pinched his ears. The twins drummed pots while Sofia ironed her uniform shirt with a hair straightener. Mom flipped pancakes one-handed, texting her manager with the other, eyes flicking to the chili-pepper clock.

“Bag’s by the door, mijo,” she said without looking. “I swapped with Carmen, but I can only get you to Main. Diner by eight-thirty.”

Teo’s stomach dipped—Main was still two miles from Veterans Park—but he nodded like it was routine. He’d walked farther; he’d run if the mesa wind pushed.

Diego tugged his sleeve. “Front seat today?”

“Back’s safer,” Teo muttered, folding three pancakes taco-style and flooding the crease with syrup. Breakfast of champions—and kids who might miss lunch.

Dad appeared, boots on, tape measure clipped like a holster. “Van’s full of Mrs. Pacheco’s sink. Try to catch the last inning, cabrón.” He ruffled Teo’s hair, calluses snagging. The word “try” hung like an uncalled pop-up.

By six-forty they rolled down Calle Del Sol, windows down because the AC had surrendered last summer. Mom hummed to oldies, drumming the wheel in sync with her anxiety. Teo ran mental scenarios: two outs, bottom of the order, him at the plate, fences 225 left, 240 center—steps he’d counted on Google Earth.

“Remember,” Mom said, braking at Main, “ask if anyone’s heading past Walmart. Don’t walk the whole way.”

“Sí.” He kissed her cheek—flour and coffee—then slung his bat bag over his shoulder. The car pulled away, taillights dissolving toward the diner that always smelled of green chile and burnt coffee.

Sun crested the mesas, baking sidewalks. Teo started the two-mile march, sneakers slapping cracked concrete. He passed the boarded Santa Fe depot, murals of vaqueros fading under graffiti: SWIFT, KARMA, LOBO. He imagined his own tag, RATTLERS green, rolling across a boxcar—proof he’d existed.

At the Walmart light his phone buzzed—Sofia: Diego spilled syrup on your home-run chart. Taped it but the ink bled. Sorry.  
He pocketed the phone. Paper could be replaced; swings couldn’t.

Veterans Park appeared: four diamonds hacked into caliche so hard grounders skipped like stones. LED scoreboard glowed 8:06. Six minutes late. He sprinted the last block, bag thumping his spine.

Beyond the chain-link, teammates orbited Coach Ramirez like moons around a stubbled sun. Willow crouched behind the plate, purple notebook balanced on her knee, logging pitch velocities that didn’t exist yet. Jayden’s neon-red hair flashed as he pantomimed a windup, mouth running a mile a minute. Marisol stood on the mound, leather bracelet from her Army brother glinting each time she fake-pumped. Perfect attendance, every one.

Teo slipped through the gate, tasting dust and adrenaline. Coach’s whistle chirped. “Vega, glad you could join the living.” No anger—just the fatigue of a man who’d waited on too many ballplayers and plumbers.

“Sorry, Coach. Sink emergency.” Not technically a lie.

“Grab a partner. Circuit’s on the board.”

Teo hustled to the outfield where teammates stretched like precision instruments. He tugged out his glove—ancient black Mizuno, laces as frayed as his shoelace, patched with fishing line Dad melted over the stove. Jayden bounded over, freckles glowing.

“Dude, you missed it. Willow says your broomstick exit velo equals her dad’s skill-saw. Lethal-weapon status.”

Teo snorted. “Tell her to chart how far my patience travels.”

Jayden grinned, flipped him a ball. “Catch, lethal weapon?”

They spread out, white stitches zipping through dry air. Each smack felt like approval. Teo let the rhythm settle his lungs—back, step, throw—until park sounds blurred: sprinklers clicking, moms unfolding chairs, a food-truck radio spitting mariachi. For five minutes he was just another arm in the chorus.

Coach clapped. “Relay stations! Vega, right field. Chen, show him the drill.”

Willow jogged over, notebook tucked under her arm like scripture. “Standard tree, Teo. Ball in the gap, you field, hit me at second, hold the runner. Simple.”

He nodded, but “simple” snagged. In his backyard every throw home was a statement.

They took positions. Coach fungoed a ball into right-center. Teo sprinted, scooped clean, adrenaline spiking. Jayden screamed, “Cut two!” but the ball was already gone—a laser that sailed ten feet over the catcher and skittered to the backstop. Runner safe, drill blown.

Whistle screamed. “That’s why God invented relays, Vega. Again.”

Willow approached, pencil tapping. “Arm strength two sigmas above mean, but accuracy drops thirty-eight percent on max effort. Solution: hit me.”

Teo wiped sweat on his sleeve. “Solution: throw better.”

“Data doesn’t care about better. It cares about repeat.” She flipped to a fresh page, dotted a line from right to second, annotated velocities. “Humor me. Hit the cutoff.”

Second rep: he fielded, crow-hopped, spotted her glove chest-high. Zip. She spun and fired home, ball beating the runner by half a step. Out. Coach whooped. Teo felt an odd tug—satisfaction that wasn’t only his.

Jayden slapped his back. “See? Teamwork—like pizza, better shared.”

Teo forced a grin. He wanted fireworks, not pepperoni.

They rotated: bunt coverage, pepper, pop-ups. Sun climbed, torching shade until shadows huddled under their cleats. Water break became Black Friday at the cooler. Teo lagged back, refilling twice because pressure was weak and nobody shoved.

Coach gathered them under the scrawny cottonwood that passed for a dugout. “Scrimmage. Vega, right field, batting sixth. Chen, call pitches. Small ball today—bunts, hit-and-runs. Highlights are for YouTube; wins are for standings.”

Six—respectable but not cleanup. Teo told himself it was the tardy tax, not a lesson.

They took the field. Willow barked decimals: “Shift point-two pull, Marisol. Jayden, inch in.” Teo drifted deeper, craving room for his arm to breathe.

First batter looped a flare. Teo charged, slid, came up firing—another missile. This time the cutoff was Jayden; the ball flew three feet over his head. Coach’s groan carried across four counties.

Inning ended. Willow intercepted Teo. “You’re regressing to mean velocity instead of optimizing outcome.”

“I got him out,” he muttered.

“Runner took second on the overthrow. Hidden cost.”

Next pitch, he stayed back, served it the other way. The ball arced toward right, fielder backpedaling. Jayden flew around second, third, home as the throw skipped to the cutoff. Teo slid into second with another double, dirt erasing the footprint under new stains. 4-3. Parents clapped uneven rhythm, rain starting on a tin roof.

Rancho’s bullpen stirred. Gutierrez stayed in, throwing harder, anger twitching his cheek. He punched out the next two, ended the threat. Teo jogged to right, wind whipping his jersey tail like a flag trying to rip free.

Top seventh—last inning in Little League unless tied. Rancho’s big bats loomed. Coach Ramirez gathered the outfielders. “One-run game. No heroes. Hit the cutoff every time.” He looked straight at Teo. “We get it to the plate together.”

First batter looped a dying quail that fell fair in front of Teo. He charged, scooped, fired—this time he squared, hit Willow’s chest on the relay. She spun, threw to second, nail-close. Safe call. Teo clapped his glove, not minding—he’d done it right.

Next batter smashed a gapper to left-center. Jayden sprinted, laid out, snared the ball inches off the turf. Runner on first tagged, racing for third. Jayden popped up, lobbed a rainbow—perfect arc, runner doubled off. Two outs, bench screaming. Teo joined, voice cracking into falsetto.

Rancho’s final hitter fouled off six pitches before slicing one down the right-field line. Teo broke instantly, caught it chest-high a step inside the chalk. Inning over. He jogged in, slapped Jayden’s hand, then Marisol’s, then Willow’s. Contact felt natural, like stepping stones across a creek he finally trusted.

Bottom seventh, last licks. Scoreboard: 4-3 Rancho. Parents stood, clapping in uneven waves. The marshal leaned against the gate, badge glinting—curfew patrol.

Leadoff walked. One out later, Jayden slashed a single to left. Runner to third. Willow stepped in, took a borderline strike, then another. 0-2. Teo’s heartbeat filled his ears like crowd static.

Willow fouled one back, then another. On the sixth pitch she inside-outed a grounder to second. The fielder glanced at the runner, took the sure out at first. Runners held. Two outs, tying run ninety feet away.

Coach Ramirez sent Marisol to the on-deck circle, then waved Teo over. “You’re up. Deep breath. Put it in play.”

Teo adjusted his helmet, strode to the box. Bases weren’t loaded—third and second—but the winning run hopped at second like a cricket. Gutierrez looked gassed, shirt dark with sweat. The catcher visited, whispered, slapped the pitcher’s shoulder.

First pitch: fastball, chest-high. Teo fouled it back, pine tar stinging his palms. He stepped out, exhaled dust. Willow’s voice drifted: “Breathe, T.”

Second pitch: curve, dropping late. Teo recognized the spin, held up. Ball one. He stepped back, tapped cleats, noticed Liam Hughes at short clapping rhythm for his pitcher. The sound felt miles underwater.

Third pitch: another curve, hanging. Teo’s wrists begged to unload, but he remembered Coach: we get it to the plate together. He stayed back, shot the ball the other way—high, deep, fair. Right fielder drifted, camped, caught it a stride in front of the track.

Jayden tagged at third, sprinted. Teo stood at the plate a heartbeat, watching the arc, then bolted for first. The throw home beat Jayden by ten feet—except the catcher closed his glove too soon; the ball popped free, rolled toward the backstop. Jayden slid across the plate as the umpire swept both arms. Game over. Rattlers 5, Rancho 4.

The bench detonated—gloves and screams tangling. Teo reached first base just as the swarm hit: Jayden tackled him first, then Willow, then Marisol. They piled on, laughter muffled under polyester and dust. Someone’s elbow clipped his ear; someone else’s breath smelled of bubble gum and victory. Teo’s face pressed against Jayden’s sleeve, tears mixing with dirt he hadn’t known lived in his eyes.

Under the weight, something unclenched in his chest—an opening he couldn’t name. When they finally rolled off, he lay on his back staring at the scoreboard: the five blinked steady, official. Not a homer on ESPN, just a sac fly that meant everything.

Hands hoisted him up. Coach Ramirez appeared, eyes shining behind scratched sunglasses. “That’s how we do it, son. Nine hearts, one beat.” He hugged Teo—quick and awkward. Ramirez patted his back twice, hard enough to echo.

Parents spilled onto the field. Mom reached him first, pulling him into a sway that smelled of coffee and dish soap. “You did it, mijo.”

“We did,” he corrected, surprising them both. Sofia stood behind her, twins balanced on hips, Diego bouncing on sneaker toes. Her eyes were suspiciously bright; she ruffled his hair without a sarcastic follow-up.

Across the diamond Liam stood apart, cap in hand, red hair dark with sweat. Teo broke from his family and walked over. For a heartbeat they studied each other—rivalry shrunk to two eleven-year-olds sharing the same tired gravity. “Good swing,” Liam offered. “Not what I expected.”

“Good game,” Teo replied. “Not what I needed.”

They bumped fists, awkward but real, and Teo felt the last bolt of his old armor clatter off. No need to beat Liam; need to thank him for raising the wall Teo learned to climb instead of blast.

The trophy presentation blurred—plastic gold under phone torches, Coach lifting it while parents played paparazzi. Fireworks bloomed above the water tower, cheap sparklers burning the town’s budget for thirty seconds of awe. The team ringed the mound, arms draped like bunting, belting the Rattlers’ fight song that half-stole its lyrics from an old railroad jingle. Teo landed between Jayden’s chatter and Willow’s quiet sniff—she was crying, he realized, tears spotting the pages she’d finally closed. He nudged her. “Stats don’t cover this, huh?”

“They do,” she answered, voice wobbly. “I just never ran the numbers on happy.”

Teo’s fingers found the game ball Coach had slipped into his glove without ceremony: not for the fly ball, but for the season-long trip from me to we. He traced the stitches, remembering every walked mile, every accepted ride, every fouled bunt until it rolled true. The ball weighed more than any homer he’d launched, freighted with borrowed time and shared miles.

Bleachers emptied, chairs clacking shut like end-of-show applause. Families drifted toward minivans smelling of pizza and victory. The Vegas lingered, stuffing popcorn bags into trash sacks—cleanup that once felt like chore now looked like tending sacred ground. Teo stabbed papers with Sofia, metal picker moving in quiet sibling sync. “You’re not just hashtags anymore,” she teased, phone dead in her pocket. “You’re, like, actual team glue.”

“Glue gets stuck,” he fired back, but the word settled warm in his chest.

Dad arrived as twilight purpled the sky, plumbing van idling near the gate. He climbed out in jeans streaked with pipe grease that matched the infield dirt. “Heard the call on the radio,” he said, voice rough. “Diner had it on.” He didn’t apologize for missing; he didn’t need to. He simply placed a calloused hand on Teo’s shoulder, squeezed once. The weight felt steady, not asking.

They played catch in the fading light—soft tosses, father and son silhouettes against the water tower’s peeling baseball. The ball smacked leather with easy rhythm, no clock running. Teo’s throws hit Dad’s glove chest-high every time; Dad’s returns popped true into Teo’s webbing. Between throws they didn’t speak, yet the silence felt full, like a dugout after a win—crowded with invisible voices.

Over Dad’s shoulder, Teo watched teammates disperse: Jayden climbing into his mom’s SUV, Willow sharing earbuds with her little sister, Marisol lugging the team’s gear bag though her arm had to burn. They moved like planets still orbiting, gravity adjusted but unbroken.

Teo fired one last throw, watched it disappear into Dad’s glove. He thought about the season—solo homers that glittered but vanished, the lonely trot while teammates slumped. Then he thought of Jayden’s spreadsheet taped inside his locker, of Willow’s fingers twirling signals, of Marisol’s bracelet around his bat handle. He thought of the dog-pile that smelled of bubble gum and dust, of the scoreboard flashing five instead of one.

Dad tossed the ball back. Teo caught it, rubbed the seam, and smiled—small, certain. He finally understood: the highlight wasn’t the swing or the flight or even the run that crossed the plate. The highlight was the weight of teammates who no longer let him stand alone.

At the lot edge Coach Ramirez locked the gate. He paused, spotted Teo, lifted trophy in one hand, keys in the other. “Take these,” he called, jangling metal. “You earned the right to lock up. Tomorrow we start fresh.”

Teo crossed gravel, closed his fist around the keys. Responsibility wasn’t solo weight; it was a door you shut together, making sure everyone was inside before the click. He snapped the padlock, the clang a period at the end of a very long sentence.

Team vans pulled out in slow caravan, headlights drilling dust tunnels. Jayden hung out the window shouting next-year plans, Willow waving her notebook now fat with happy stats. Marisol saluted with her glove. Teo lifted the ball in answer, small white moon orbiting the convoy of his new galaxy.

When the last taillight vanished, he turned to his family. Mom jingled the car keys—diner shift in six hours, but she wasn’t rushing. Dad hoisted Diego into the backseat, promising pancakes to celebrate “the Vega sacrifice heard ‘round the county.” Sofia buckled the twins, humming the fight song off-key. The car smelled of chile and baby powder and something Teo couldn’t name until they were halfway home: belonging, uncluttered and free.

He buckled, ball still in hand, head cool against the window. Las Piedras rolled past in fragments: Walmart fortress, onion fields, library squatting beside the rails like a patient toad. Every mile used to feel like distance to conquer; now it felt like perimeter—boundary lines of a town that had decided he was worth showing up for.

Dad parked beside the porch, killed the engine. Nobody moved, as if the night’s spell might shatter under a door handle. Then Diego whispered, “We won,” and the car exhaled laughter. They spilled out, Vega chaos reassembling: Sofia chasing the twins, Mom balancing leftover pizza, Dad lifting the sleeping baby like softer trophy gold. Teo lingered on the cracked walk, looked up. Same stars, new lens. He tucked ball into glove, set both on the porch step, felt something loosen in his chest—the final knot in a frayed lace finally allowed to unravel.

Inside, the house greeted them with its usual symphony of minor disasters: TV shouting cartoons, microwave pinging, baby monitor crackling like distant crowd noise. He waded through, no longer swinging at the noise but letting it wash over him—stadium static proving the place was alive and he was part of the pulse. In his room he paused, eyed the index card still pinned above his bed—*Antenna + 5*—the solo record from another life. He unpinned it, folded it small, slipped it into the glove’s empty pinky. Room for new numbers, ones he wouldn’t count alone.

Sofia appeared, shoulder against the frame, arms crossed but eyes soft. “You gonna stare at your stuff all night, or you want pancakes?”

“Pancakes,” he answered, and followed her through the maze—over Diego’s cleats, around twins’ blocks—what used to feel like ankle weights now felt like warm-up cones for the heart. At the switch he paused, hand on the plate, thought of Coach handing over keys. Someone had to kill the lights when the game ended. Someone had to flip them back on when it was time to play.

He flicked off. Darkness settled, friendly and complete. Inside it he still heard the crowd, the crack, the thud of feet meeting plate in perfect trust. The sound would fade by dawn but not disappear; it would just make room for tomorrow’s music—spatulas on griddles, twins giggling, Dad’s truck coughing awake. Baseball woven into everyday fabric, no longer escape but texture.

He stepped into kitchen light, glove left on the bed, seams waiting. He sat elbow-to-elbow with siblings, syrup pooling like miniature base paths. Mom poured batter in slow circles, same motion she used wiping tables—careful, economical, loving. Dad told the sac-fly story quiet so the twins wouldn’t wake, every syllable heavy with wonder. Sofia snapped a photo, flash blooming, showed him the screen: not a solo shot, but a crowded table, faces lit gold. She didn’t post it. She let him look, then tucked the phone away, secret kept.

He lifted a forkful, steam curling like a tiny scoreboard. Tasted cinnamon, butter, the faint iron of the pan—a childhood flavor revised by victory but rooted in the same kitchen. He chewed, swallowed, felt the day settle inside him inning by inning until he was full. Not of fame. Of family. Of team. Of the beautiful, ordinary magic that happens when you stop trying to win everything alone and start trusting the relay.

In the living room the glove waited, ball curled inside like a heart. Tomorrow he’d oil the leather, maybe teach Diego to lace. Tonight he ate, answered, laughed when syrup dripped on Diego’s sock. And when the house finally dimmed and exhaled into sleep, he walked barefoot to the porch, keys in pocket, and looked over Calle Del Sol. The moon hung above the water tower, pearl against painted stitches. He lifted a hand, small salute, whispered thanks to field, team, town, rails. Then he went inside, closed the door soft, locked it—not to keep the world out, but to keep the warmth in.

The last thing he heard before dreams was the refrigerator humming, low and steady, sounding impossibly like a crowd holding its breath, ready to cheer. He smiled into the pillow, glove on the nightstand, ball in the pocket. First light would bring new chalk, new chores, new chances to depend and be depended on. He’d be there, cleats tied tight—laces finally whole—bat on his shoulder, heart synced to nine other heartbeats. Because he’d learned the secret the fences never told: home-run distance means nothing if no one’s waiting at the plate. But a sacrifice fly—just a simple sac fly—can carry a whole town home.

Outside, the stars kept score in silence, and for once Teo didn’t need to see his name in lights. He could hear it instead in the quiet rhythm of his family breathing around him—proof that the game ends, the cheers fade, but the team you build out of love and pancakes and shared miles goes extra innings forever. He closed his eyes, keys cold against his hip, ball warm in his palm, and let the rails sing him to sleep, no longer the sound of leaving but the lullaby of staying.

Good game, good game, good game.
