I've been rehearsing this conversation for eighteen years.

Not with Chen specifically — for most of those years I didn't know she existed. But with someone. Some hypothetical person sitting across from me in some hypothetical room, hearing the words that would make them understand or make them walk away. I've rehearsed it in the shower, in the car, lying awake at three in the morning with Elena breathing beside me. I've tried a hundred versions. The clinical version, the emotional version, the version where I lead with evidence and the version where I lead with the impossible thing and let the evidence catch up.

None of them are good. There is no good way to say what I need to say.

Chen's office at NEORAN is small and functional — a desk, two monitors, a whiteboard covered in atmospheric readings, and Boltzmann asleep on a dog bed in the corner. She's made coffee. Not offered me coffee, made coffee, which is how Chen signals that she's ready for a long conversation. She sets the French press between us and sits down and looks at me with that particular stillness she has, like a bird of prey that's spotted movement in the grass but hasn't decided yet whether to dive.

"You said you needed to tell me something," she says.

"Yeah."

"About the source of your predictions."

"Yeah."

She waits. Boltzmann shifts in his sleep, paws twitching. Outside the window, the parking lot is empty. It's Saturday. I picked Saturday on purpose — no staff, no interruptions, no one to overhear. I've spent eighteen years building toward this moment and now that it's here my mouth is dry and my hands are doing something complicated in my lap.

"I need you to let me get through it," I say. "All of it. Before you respond."

"All right."

"I mean it. No questions until I'm done."

"I understand."

I take a breath. It feels like the last breath before a high dive — that moment where the platform is solid under your feet and the water is very far below and the only thing left is the decision to jump.

"I didn't predict any of it," I say. "I remembered it."

---

I tell her everything.

Not the curated version I gave Elena, not the strategic disclosures I've parceled out over the years like a miser counting coins. Everything. The first timeline — the real one, or the original one, or whatever you call the life I actually lived before this one. FedEx Office in Waterloo. The drinking. The slow gray waste of my twenties. The way the world ended not with a bang but with a series of bangs that everyone kept insisting were something else until they couldn't anymore.

I tell her about the Kessari. Not the name — I didn't know the name then, nobody did, they were just the objects, the lights, the things in the sky that the news kept calling natural phenomena until the phenomena started dismantling infrastructure. I tell her about the particulate, how it hung in the air for weeks before the first ships became visible, how it tasted like metal and ozone. I tell her about the hum. Fourteen point two hertz. How the dogs went first — howling, then silent, then gone. How the power grid failed in sections, east to west, like someone was pulling plugs. How the shelters weren't ready because no one had believed the shelters would ever be needed.

I tell her about dying. Or not dying exactly — about the moment where the timeline ended and I was somewhere else, or nowhere, or everywhere, and then I was seventeen years old in a dorm room in Waterloo and the year was 2008 and I was screaming.

I tell her about the first weeks. The disorientation. The panic attacks. The absolute certainty that I had lost my mind, followed by the slower and more terrible certainty that I hadn't. The notebook. The first predictions — small ones, verifiable ones, things I could check against the world to confirm that what I remembered was real. The stock market crash. The specific date, the specific magnitude. The way the numbers matched and matched and matched.

I tell her about the decision to act. How it wasn't noble. How it was desperate and clumsy and driven by the memory of what the air tasted like when the particulate settled. How I built NEORAN not because I'm a visionary but because I'm a man who watched the world end once and couldn't bear to watch it happen the same way again.

I tell her about the cost. The friendships held at arm's length. The lies — God, the lies, layer upon layer of them, until some days I couldn't remember which version of myself I was performing for which audience. I tell her about Marcus, about the blackmail I still can't think about without my stomach turning. About Elena leaving and coming back and the harder terms we've built between us, which are better terms, more honest ones, but still not fully honest because until this moment there has been exactly one person on earth who knows the whole truth and that person is me.

I tell her it's been eighteen years. That I have been carrying this for eighteen years. That I am thirty-three years old and I have lived to thirty-five and I am so tired of being the only person who knows.

Then I stop talking. The French press is cold. Boltzmann has woken up and moved to lie against Chen's feet, as if even the dog can sense that the room has changed.

Chen is quiet for a long time.

I count the seconds because I can't help it. It's a habit from the early years, when I was still testing myself — how long can you sit with the silence, how long before you fill it with something that makes it worse. I get to forty-seven before she speaks.

"The mechanism is impossible," she says.

"I know."

"There is no framework in physics — not quantum mechanics, not general relativity, not any speculative model I'm aware of — that permits the transfer of consciousness backward along a worldline. The information-theoretic constraints alone—"

"I know."

She stops. Picks up her coffee, realizes it's cold, sets it down again. Her face hasn't changed. That's the thing about Chen — other people's faces do things when they hear impossible information. Chen's face does exactly nothing. She processes internally, like a computer running a calculation that takes longer than expected but will eventually output a result.

"The data is not impossible," she says.

"No."

"Eighteen years of predictions. Exposed to me over six years, documented before the events occurred, verified against public record. Atmospheric particulate confirmed independently by Pathfinder sensors and three ground-based monitoring stations. The fourteen-point-two-hertz signal detected by USGS arrays on dates consistent with your timeline. The objects—" She pauses. "The objects' trajectories consistent with your described sequence of events."

"Yes."

"I cannot accept the mechanism."

"I know."

"I can accept the evidence." She opens her laptop. "I need to cross-reference your timeline against my records. Start from the beginning. The atmospheric anomalies — when did they first become detectable in your original experience?"

This is Chen. This is so perfectly Chen that I almost laugh, except laughing feels impossible right now. She can't accept that I traveled through time. No honest scientist could. But she can accept that I have information that has been verified across eighteen years, and she can work with that information regardless of its source, because data doesn't care where it comes from. Data is data.

She doesn't hug me. She doesn't tell me she believes me. She opens her laptop and starts building a spreadsheet.

I talk for another hour. She types. Occasionally she asks me to repeat a date, clarify a sequence. Her questions are precise and technical — not "how did it feel" but "what was the particulate density at ground level" and "do you remember the interval between grid failures." I answer what I can. Some of it is clear, burned into memory the way trauma burns. Some of it is vague, degraded by seventeen years of living a different life on top of the old one. She notes the confidence levels without judgment. High confidence. Medium confidence. Low confidence. She treats my memory like any other data source — useful but imperfect, subject to decay and bias, valuable precisely because it can be checked against independent records.

When we're done the office is dark. Neither of us turned on the lights as the afternoon faded. The laptop screen casts blue-white shadows across Chen's face.

"Three questions," she says.

"Okay."

"In your original timeline, was there any organized resistance? Any effective response?"

"No. Not really. Some military, near the end. Nothing coordinated, nothing that worked."

"And the total duration from first visible contact to—" She doesn't finish the sentence.

"About seven months. From the first ships to—" I don't finish it either.

"And the particulate preceded first contact by how long?"

"Eighteen months. Maybe twenty. It started before anyone realized what it was."

She nods. Types something. Closes the laptop.

"I'll have a preliminary correlation analysis by Tuesday," she says.

"Chen."

"Yes."

"Are you okay?"

She considers the question with the same seriousness she gives everything. "I have new data that changes my model of the situation. I need time to integrate it. I would not describe myself as okay or not okay. I would describe myself as working." She reaches down and scratches Boltzmann behind the ears. "You should go home. Get some sleep. We have a lot to do."

I stand up. My legs are stiff. I've been sitting in this chair for three hours and I feel like I've been sitting in it for eighteen years.

"Thank you," I say.

"For what?"

"For not telling me I'm insane."

"The data doesn't support that conclusion," she says, and turns the laptop back on.

---

The paper comes out in late April. "Anomalous Atmospheric Particulate: Evidence for Non-Natural Origin," published in the Journal of Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics — not Nature, not Science, not anywhere that the physics establishment checks before breakfast. Chen doesn't care. She's not trying to start a revolution. She's trying to get the data on record.

The paper is meticulous. Twenty-three pages of spectroscopic analysis, particle morphology data, distribution patterns inconsistent with volcanic or industrial sources. She avoids the word "engineered" in the abstract, buries it on page sixteen in a paragraph about potential explanations, frames it as one hypothesis among several. Even so, it's the kind of paper that makes department chairs nervous. The kind of paper that gets forwarded around with subject lines like "thoughts?" and "have you seen this?"

Mainstream reaction: silence. Not hostile silence — the paper isn't prominent enough to attract hostility. Just the vast gray indifference of an academic establishment that has a thousand papers to read this week and no particular reason to prioritize this one. Two reviewers accepted it. A third recommended revisions. Chen made the revisions. It published. The world continued.

Three emails arrive within a week of publication. The first is from an atmospheric scientist at NOAA, carefully worded, deniable if forwarded: "Your spectroscopic data is consistent with anomalies we've been tracking since 2022. Would be interested in comparing datasets." The second is from a researcher at the Met Office in Exeter: "We've seen it too. Our group has been unable to account for a persistent fine-particulate signal in Northern Hemisphere stations. Published nothing because we couldn't identify a source." The third is from a retired professor at Caltech, less careful with his words: "Finally. Someone else."

Chen prints the emails and pins them to her whiteboard. Three people. Three out of thousands. It's not a movement. It's not vindication. It's three candles in a very dark room. But the room isn't empty anymore.

---

The pivot happens in stages. Not because I want it to — if I could flip a switch and turn NEORAN from a regional disaster preparedness organization into what it actually needs to be, I would have flipped it years ago. But organizations are organisms. They have immune systems. Push too hard, too fast, and the immune system attacks.

So I move carefully. I hire a food security specialist in March and frame it as expanding our agricultural resilience portfolio. In April I approve a grant application for a hydroponic research facility in partnership with the University of Guelph — good optics, good science, nothing that would raise an eyebrow at a board meeting. In May I begin conversations with the Svalbard Global Seed Vault about regional redundancy, because seed vaults are boring and responsible and exactly the kind of thing a disaster preparedness organization should care about.

The language stays boring. I have learned, over eighteen years, that boring language is a weapon. Exciting language gets questioned. Boring language gets approved. So our shelter-hardening initiative is a "structural resilience upgrade program." Our distributed food production network is an "agricultural continuity pilot." Our quiet stockpiling of medical supplies and water purification systems is "inventory optimization for sustained-duration response scenarios."

Every word is chosen. Every proposal is cited. Every budget line is defensible. And underneath the boring language, underneath the citations and the line items and the quarterly reports, the machine is turning. Slowly. Not fast enough.

I lie awake at night doing the math. How many shelters. How many people per shelter. How many months of food, water, air filtration. The numbers never come out right. They can't. You can't save everyone. I knew that at seventeen when I started and I know it at thirty-three and the knowledge hasn't gotten easier to carry. But you can save some. You can save more than zero. You can build the infrastructure that gives people a chance, even if the chance is small, even if the math says most of them won't make it.

That's what I tell myself. Most nights it's enough.

---

Elena finds me on the porch on a Tuesday evening in late May. The lilacs are blooming along the fence line and the air smells like spring and something else — that faint metallic edge that nobody else seems to notice but that I haven't been able to stop noticing since Chen's paper put numbers to it.

She sits down beside me. She's been reading Chen's paper — the real version, not the published one, the extended draft with the sections Chen pulled before submission because even a mid-tier journal wouldn't print them. Elena reads it the way she reads everything, carefully, with a pen in her hand, making notes in the margins.

"The fourteen-point-two-hertz signal," she says. "That's the whispering."

"Yes."

"The low-frequency hum people have been reporting. The sleep disruptions. The dogs."

"Yes."

"It's in the particulate data too? They're connected?"

"Chen thinks so. The particulate affects atmospheric acoustics. Changes the way low-frequency sound propagates. The hum gets louder in areas with higher particulate concentration."

Elena is quiet for a moment. She's looking at the lilacs. The light is doing that thing it does in late May, going golden and long, turning everything it touches into a painting of itself.

"That's real," she says.

"Yes."

"All of it. The particulate. The sound. The objects Chen's been tracking."

"Yes."

She takes my hand. Her fingers are warm and her grip is firm and she doesn't look at me. She looks straight ahead at the lilacs and the golden light and the street where our neighbors are walking their dog and the dog is pulling at the leash, ears back, the way dogs do now, the way they've been doing for months.

"What do we need to do?" she says.

I tell her. Not everything — not the first timeline, not the details I gave Chen. But the shape of it. The shelters. The food. The water. The plans that are already in motion and the plans that aren't. The math that doesn't come out right. She listens without interrupting, the same way Chen listened, but differently — Chen listened like a scientist absorbing data, Elena listens like a person absorbing weight, taking some of it onto her own shoulders because she can see that mine are bowing under it.

When I finish she says, "Okay."

"Okay?"

"Okay. I'm in. Tell me what you need me to do."

I don't say anything for a while. The golden light is fading. The neighbor's dog has stopped pulling and is sitting on the sidewalk, very still, ears pointed toward something none of us can hear.

"I need you to not be afraid," I say.

"I'm already afraid." She squeezes my hand. "That's not the same as stopping."

---

The weeks blur. I'm at NEORAN fourteen hours a day. Chen is publishing a follow-up paper, expanding the atmospheric dataset with contributions from her three new contacts — NOAA, Met Office, Caltech. The data is getting harder to ignore, though the establishment is still managing it. Four independent groups now. Four datasets. The same anomalous particulate, the same non-natural morphology, the same distribution patterns that don't match any known source.

I call Frank on a Sunday morning. He's in the garden, which is where Frank always is when the weather allows. I can hear the birds through the phone, and the scrape of his trowel.

"How's the heart?" I ask.

"Still beating. How's yours?"

"Busy."

"You sound tired."

"I am tired."

"Come for dinner this week. Bring Elena. I'll make something with too much garlic and we'll sit on the deck and you'll tell me what's keeping you up at night, or you won't, and either way you'll eat."

I say yes. I always say yes to Frank. He's sixty-two and he's had a stent put in and he's the most solid thing in my life, the foundation that everything else is built on, and I am afraid of losing him in a way that I can't articulate because the fear is too large for words.

Sophie texts me on Thursday. A photo of a sunset over Lake Huron, no caption, then a second text: *How are you, for real?*

*For real, I'm working too much and sleeping too little.*

*Same. Want to get coffee Saturday?*

*Yeah. I'd like that.*

The relationship with Sophie is still new in the way that genuine things are new — fragile not because it's weak but because it's honest. She's twenty-seven. She's building a career in social work. She doesn't know what's coming and I can't tell her yet and the weight of that silence is different from all the other silences I've carried because Sophie is my sister and she trusts me now, really trusts me, and trust is the thing I keep asking for from people and the thing I keep repaying with omission.

Marcus and I have lunch on a Wednesday. We don't talk about the blackmail. We haven't talked about it since it happened. What we talk about is his daughter, who is two and a half and has opinions about everything, and his wife, who is pregnant again, and the Leafs, who are terrible, and the weather, which is strange. He says it offhand — "weird spring, right?" — and I agree because it is weird, the lilacs blooming three weeks early and the rain coming in patterns that don't match anything on the forecasts, and I think about telling him and I don't.

Not yet. Soon. But not yet.

---

The call from Chen comes on a Thursday night in early June. I'm at home, halfway through a plate of pasta, Elena across the table with her laptop open. My phone buzzes and I see Chen's name and something in my chest contracts because Chen doesn't call. Chen emails. Chen sends data. Chen calls only when the data has changed in a way that email can't carry.

"I need you to come in," she says.

"Now?"

"Now."

Elena looks up. She can hear Chen's voice through the phone, or maybe she can read my face. Either way she closes her laptop and stands.

"I'm coming too," she says.

I don't argue.

The drive to the NEORAN office takes eleven minutes at night. I make it in eight. The building is dark except for Chen's window on the second floor, where the light has that blue-white quality that means she's been staring at screens for hours. We take the stairs two at a time. Boltzmann meets us at the office door, tail wagging, oblivious.

Chen is at her desk. Two monitors. On the left, the Pathfinder tracking data — the orbital plot I've seen a hundred times, the objects' trajectories marked in red against the black of mapped space. On the right, a velocity graph. A curve that should be smooth and predictable and isn't.

"Sit down," Chen says.

We sit.

"I've been running trajectory updates every seventy-two hours since we confirmed the Pathfinder data in 2018," she says. Her voice is flat. Not calm — flat, the way a voice goes when the person speaking has already processed the information and is now in the phase where processing becomes communication. "The objects have maintained a consistent deceleration profile for six years. Consistent with my models. Consistent with your timeline." She glances at me, the briefest flicker. Elena catches it but says nothing. "Forty-seven minutes ago, the latest tracking update completed processing."

She turns the right monitor toward us. The velocity graph. I look at the curve and for a moment I don't understand what I'm seeing, because the curve has changed. The deceleration was gradual — a long, shallow slope, the objects bleeding speed over years, approaching at a pace that gave us time. Years of time. Time I have been using, time I have been counting on.

The slope has steepened. Not by a little. The curve bends like a knee, the gradient doubling over the last six weeks, and I don't need Chen to explain what that means because I can read a graph and because I lived through this before. They were slowing down. They're still slowing down. But they're slowing down faster, which means they're arriving sooner, which means the timeline I've been working from — the timeline I've built everything on — is wrong.

"They've changed their deceleration profile," Chen says. "They are arriving faster than projected."

"How much faster?"

"I need more data points to refine the estimate. But initial calculations suggest a reduction of fourteen to eighteen months."

The number lands in the room like a body. Fourteen to eighteen months. A year to a year and a half of preparation, erased. Shelters that aren't built. Food stores that aren't stocked. Systems that aren't tested. People who aren't warned.

Elena's hand finds mine under the desk. Her grip is very tight.

"Is it possible it's an error?" I ask, because I have to ask, because that's the question you ask even when you already know the answer.

"I've checked the raw data three times. I've verified against the NOAA baseline. It is not an error." Chen pauses. "The deceleration profile change is not consistent with any natural phenomenon. Comets don't decide to slow down faster. Asteroids don't adjust their approach vectors. This is a course correction."

The word sits in the air. Correction. An adjustment made by something that can make adjustments. Something that is coming here on purpose and has decided to come here faster.

Elena is looking at the graph. I can feel her processing it — not the physics, but the meaning. The scale. The thing that I have known for eighteen years and that she has known for months and that is now, in this moment, becoming real in a way that knowledge alone can't make real. There is a difference between knowing the wave is coming and seeing it on the horizon.

"What do we do?" she asks.

The question is for both of us. Chen looks at me. I look at the curve on the screen, the bend in the line that means everything I've built is not enough, has never been enough, and now has less time to become enough.

"We work faster," I say.

Chen nods. She turns back to her monitors. Her fingers move across the keyboard, already running new calculations, already updating models, already doing what Chen does, which is face the impossible with data and precision and the absolute refusal to look away.

Elena and I leave an hour later. The parking lot is dark and quiet. Above us, the sky is clear, full of stars that look the same as they always have. You can't see them. Not yet. Not with the naked eye. But they're up there, somewhere in the black between the stars, and they are coming, and they are coming sooner than I thought.

On the drive home, Elena says, "We're going to be okay."

I don't answer. I don't know if she means it as a statement or a prayer. I don't know if there's a difference anymore.

Back in Chen's office, the screens cast their blue-white light across empty chairs. Boltzmann is asleep again, curled tight against her feet. Chen sits alone with the data, with the curve, with the numbers that have changed everything. The objects on the screen are small red points against a vast dark field. They are decelerating. They are decelerating faster. They are making choices.

"Oh," Chen says to Boltzmann. She reaches down and rests her hand on the dog's warm back. "Oh no."
