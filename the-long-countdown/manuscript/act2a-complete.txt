Chapter Eight: The Long Game

The guy teaching FEMA's Incident Command System certification is named Dale Puckett, and he has a mustache that belongs on a 1970s cop show. He's fifty-something, ex-fire service out of Dubuque, and he opens the three-day course by telling us that most people who die in disasters don't die from the disaster. They die from the response.

"They die from confusion," he says, clicking to a slide that shows an org chart. "They die because nobody knows who's in charge. They die because Engine 4 is on a different radio frequency than the county sheriff, and both of them think the other one evacuated Maple Street."

I'm sitting in the back row of a conference room at the Holiday Inn in Waterloo, Iowa. It's October 2010, and I'm the youngest person here by at least fifteen years. The woman next to me is a county health administrator from Grundy Center. The man on my other side runs a volunteer fire department in Jesup, population 2,500. They both looked at me when I sat down. They're still looking.

I know more than Dale Puckett about incident command. I know this the way I know a lot of things — because I lived through a world where incident command didn't matter anymore, where the structures collapsed and what replaced them was improvised, brutal, and mostly inadequate. I know what a unified command post looks like when it works. I know what it looks like when it doesn't. I have opinions about span of control that would make Dale Puckett's mustache curl.

I keep my mouth shut. I take notes.

This is harder than it sounds. There's a version of me — the one who showed up in 2008, raw and stupid with grief — who would have corrected Puckett on his characterization of the NIMS hierarchy. Who would have raised his hand and said, actually, the problem isn't interoperability, it's that your planning section chief is doing operations work and nobody has the guts to call it out. That version of me needed to be the smartest person in the room because being smart was the only card I had left.

I'm learning to hold cards.

Puckett moves on to resource typing, and I write down things I already know in a notebook I bought at Walmart, and the woman from Grundy Center stops looking at me like I wandered in from a fraternity party, and by the second day she's asking me to be in her tabletop exercise group because I'm "organized." By the third day Dale Puckett shakes my hand and says I should think about going for my Professional Emergency Manager credential.

"Most people your age are thinking about bars," he says.

"I think about bars too," I tell him. "Just different ones."

He doesn't get the joke. That's fine. It wasn't a good joke.

---

I spend a year learning to be useful. It turns out this is harder than learning to be clever.

Clever is a parlor trick. You see the pattern before anyone else, you say the right thing at the right time, you let people draw their own conclusions about how remarkable you are. I've been running that play since I got here. The stock predictions, the political calls, the quiet positioning with Chen — all of it is clever. All of it is me spending currency I didn't earn.

Useful is different. Useful is showing up at the Johnson County Red Cross chapter on a Thursday evening and learning how to fill out a Disaster Assessment form. Useful is sitting through a four-hour training on shelter management where the instructor spends forty-five minutes on cot spacing. Useful is boring. Useful is a man named Gerald who's been volunteering for thirty-one years and still shows up every week to teach people like me how to inventory a supply trailer.

Gerald is sixty-four. He has hearing aids in both ears and a Class B CDL. He drove a shelter supply truck to Parkersburg after the tornado in 2008, the one that killed seven people and flattened a town I'd driven through a hundred times in my other life. He doesn't talk about it much. When he does, the details are specific: "The high school gymnasium had two functioning toilets for three hundred people. We had to set up latrines in the parking lot. Nobody thinks about toilets until there aren't any."

I think about toilets now. I think about water purification and generator maintenance and how many calories a person needs per day when they're doing manual labor in July. I think about supply chains and their fragility, about how a single bridge closure can cut off a county, about how many Americans have less than three days of food in their homes at any given time.

These are not theoretical concerns for me. But I can't tell anyone that, so I learn them again, from the ground up, from Gerald and from Dale Puckett and from a shelf of FEMA Independent Study courses I work through on my laptop in the library when I should probably be studying for my statistics midterm.

I pass statistics anyway. I pass everything. My GPA is a 3.8, which is high enough to be useful and low enough that nobody asks questions.

The point is, I'm building something. Quietly, without a blueprint anyone else can see. An emergency manager who can organize a county-level response. A person with credentials and relationships and practical skills. A foundation that doesn't depend on knowing the future — that would still be worth something even if everything I remember turns out to be wrong.

Some nights I almost believe it.

---

Sunday dinner at the Callahan house has a structure as predictable as plate tectonics and roughly as slow to change.

Diane makes a roast. This is non-negotiable. The roast is beef, sometimes pork, and it goes in the oven at noon with potatoes and carrots and an amount of garlic that Frank considers "aggressive" but eats anyway. There's a salad that nobody asked for and everyone eats because Diane made it. There's bread from Hy-Vee. There's water and milk and, since I turned nineteen, Frank occasionally opens a beer and doesn't say anything when I open one too.

I drive down from Cedar Falls most Sundays. It's twenty minutes. I could skip it. I don't.

Today it's November, football weather, and Frank has the Iowa game on mute in the living room while we eat at the dining table because Diane insists we eat at the dining table. Sophie is texting under the table with the subtlety of a car alarm. Diane is pretending not to notice. Frank is eating his roast.

"So," Frank says. He cuts a piece of potato in half. This is how Frank opens conversations — with a conjunction and a root vegetable. "How's the school stuff."

"Good. I'm doing a capstone project on continuity of operations planning for rural hospitals."

Frank chews. Considers this. "So you're learning how to help people when things go bad."

"Yeah, Dad. That's pretty much it."

"That's..." He reaches for the salt. Diane slides it toward him without looking up. Thirty years of marriage in a single gesture. "Well. That's good."

I have been told I love you by people who meant it and by people who didn't. I have heard eloquent speeches and read beautiful letters. None of them hit the way Frank Callahan saying *that's good* hits, because Frank doesn't waste words any more than he wastes lumber. If he says it's good, he's been thinking about whether it's good, and he's concluded that it's good, and he is now conveying this conclusion with the minimum viable vocabulary.

"Thanks, Dad."

"You want more potatoes?"

"Yeah."

He passes the dish. The Iowa game goes to commercial, silent on the screen. Sophie puts her phone down long enough to eat three bites of roast, and Diane asks me if I'm sleeping enough, and I lie and say yes, and Frank doesn't ask any follow-up questions because follow-up questions aren't really his thing.

Later, I'm helping Diane with dishes. She washes, I dry. This is our arrangement. She tells me about a woman at her office who's getting divorced, and I make the appropriate noises, and then she says, "Your father talks about you at work."

"What?"

"He tells the guys at the plant about your emergency management program. Greg Hollister asked him about it and Frank went on for ten minutes apparently."

I set a plate in the drying rack. "Frank went on for ten minutes."

"I know." She smiles. "Greg called me to make sure he wasn't sick."

I laugh. It comes out sounding strange — raw, unguarded. Diane glances at me over her glasses, but she doesn't say anything, just hands me another plate.

---

Sophie is in the living room reading when I come downstairs with my jacket. She's on the couch with her knees up, and the book is thick — Bryson, *A Short History of Nearly Everything*, which is not what I expected. My FEMA manuals are on the coffee table where I left them last weekend. She's got one open next to her, face-down on the cushion, like she was comparing the two.

"You're reading my stuff?"

She doesn't look up. "Your stuff was sitting here. I got bored."

"And?"

"And it's incredibly boring. Who writes these? Do they get paid by the acronym?"

"Pretty much."

She turns a page of the Bryson. "Why do you care about this? Nobody our age cares about disaster preparedness. It's like you're forty."

"Maybe I'm just ahead of the curve."

Now she looks up. Those sharp Callahan eyes — Frank's eyes, actually, although she'd hate to hear it. "You're so weird now. You know that, right? You used to be normal-weird. Now you're like... purposefully weird."

"Is that better or worse?"

She thinks about it. Actually thinks about it, which is new. Six months ago she would have just said *worse* and gone back to her book. "I don't know yet," she says. "I'll let you know."

I pick up the FEMA manual she was reading. IS-700, the NIMS overview. She dog-eared a page. I look at it — it's the section on mutual aid agreements, which is honestly one of the more interesting parts.

"If you want," I say, "I can explain the parts that are boring."

"They're all boring."

"That's fair. See you next week, Soph."

"Whatever."

But she says it differently now. The word is the same. The shrapnel is gone.

---

Marcus calls on a Wednesday in March. I'm walking back from the library and the wind is doing that thing Iowa wind does in early spring where it can't decide if it's trying to kill you or just humiliate you.

"So I'm taking Structural Analysis II," he says, no hello, because Marcus doesn't believe in verbal preamble. "And we're doing bridge loading patterns and I swear to God, Nate, the math is going to end me."

"What kind of bridge?"

"Continuous beam. Three spans. Asymmetric loading. The professor acts like influence lines are self-evident, and I'm sitting there like a man watching his house burn down."

"Walk me through it."

He does. I don't know anything about structural engineering — not in either lifetime — but I know how to listen, which is a skill I've been practicing the way other people practice guitar. Badly at first, then less badly. Marcus talks for twenty minutes about moment distribution and shear diagrams, and I ask questions that are probably stupid but keep him talking, and somewhere around minute fifteen he says, "Oh. Oh, wait. I think I see it."

"See what?"

"The zero crossing. It's at the — hang on." I hear paper rustling. A pencil. Muttering. Then: "I'm an idiot."

"You're not an idiot."

"I've been looking at this for two days and all I needed was to say it out loud. You're like a rubber duck."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me."

He laughs. Then he says, "Hey. Your predictions."

I stop walking. The wind fills the silence.

"The oil spill thing," he says. "The Deepwater Horizon. That was number four."

"Marcus—"

"I'm not driving to Cedar Falls. Not yet. But Nate." His voice is careful, the way you're careful when you're trying not to spook something. "I've got a file. Virginia governor's race, Indiana Senate, GM bankruptcy, and now an oil rig in the Gulf of Mexico. Four things you told me before they happened."

"I read a lot."

"Nobody reads that much. Nobody reads *that specifically*." A pause. "I'm not asking you to explain. I'm just telling you that I notice. And when you're ready to talk about it, I'll listen."

The wind is pulling tears from my eyes. That's what it is. The wind.

"Thanks, man."

"Also, I need you to explain what a propped cantilever is because my textbook is garbage."

We talk for another half hour. I sit on a bench outside the library with my coat zipped to my chin and my ears going numb, and I listen to my brother describe the physics of things that hold weight, and for a little while I don't feel like I'm carrying mine alone.

June 2011.

I have a date circled on a calendar that I keep in the bottom drawer of my desk, under a folder of old syllabi. The calendar is from 2011, the free kind they give out at the Farm Bureau, and the date is June 22nd, and I've been counting down to it for seventeen months.

June 22nd is when the signal appears.

I told Chen about it in the fall of 2009 — gave her coordinates, a frequency range, and a timeframe. Eighteen months, I said. Give or take a few weeks. I was specific because I could afford to be specific. This is one of the things I actually remember with clarity: the detection date, the coordinates, the way the announcement rippled through the physics community before anyone understood what it meant. In my timeline, it was a curiosity. An anomaly in the data that took years to connect to the Kessari. By the time anyone made the connection, it was academic. They were already here.

In this timeline, Judith Chen is looking for it. Has been looking for it since I sat in her office and made a claim that sounded insane and gave her numbers that were too precise to dismiss.

June arrives and I stop sleeping.

Not metaphorically. I lie in bed in my apartment — a one-bedroom above a barbershop on College Street, $425 a month, perpetually smells like Barbicide — and I stare at the ceiling and I run the numbers. The coordinates were right. I'm sure the coordinates were right. But memory is a living thing, and I've been here for almost three years now, and sometimes I reach for a fact and find it softer than it used to be, worn smooth like a river stone. What if I transposed a digit? What if the frequency range was off by a fraction? What if the timeline itself has shifted enough that the signal comes late, or early, or not at all?

If I'm wrong, Chen writes me off. Not cruelly — she's too precise for cruelty — but completely. She'll file me under "interesting delusion" and go back to her sunset spreadsheet and her improbably fat cat, and I will have burned my one bridge to the one person who might be able to help me prove what's coming.

I can't eat. I try — I stand in my kitchen with a box of Cheerios and the milk carton and I pour a bowl and take one bite and my stomach folds in on itself like a lawn chair. I drink coffee. I drink water. I go to class and take notes I don't remember and come home and check my phone and check my email and check my phone again.

June 15th. Nothing.

June 18th. Nothing.

June 20th. I go for a run at 5 AM because if I don't move I'm going to lose my mind. I run along the river, past the campus, past the Hy-Vee, out to where the sidewalk ends and it's just gravel and soybeans. I run until my lungs burn and my legs feel like they belong to someone else, and then I walk home and shower and sit on my bed with wet hair and my hands shaking and I think: *what if this is all I am. What if I'm just a man who knows things that don't happen.*

June 22nd. I don't leave my apartment. I sit on the couch with my phone on the cushion next to me and I watch it the way you watch a door you know someone is going to walk through. Morning passes. Afternoon passes. I eat half a banana. I drink three glasses of water. I read the same page of a textbook eleven times without absorbing a single word.

At six PM I start to make deals with myself. Maybe the detection takes time. Maybe she's running verification. Maybe the signal is there and she hasn't looked yet today. Maybe, maybe, maybe. The maybes are a disease. They breed in silence.

At nine PM I decide she's not going to call. The signal isn't there. I was wrong — about the date, or the coordinates, or the frequency, or all of it. Three years of positioning, of careful work, of the most important relationship I've been building, and I was wrong. I get up and pour the Cheerios from this morning down the disposal and wash the bowl and set it in the rack and I think about what my life looks like now. Emergency management degree. FEMA certifications. Red Cross experience. A family that's starting to recognize me as someone worth knowing. It's not nothing. It's a life. It's just not the life that changes anything.

I brush my teeth. I set my phone on the nightstand. I lie down.

At 11:07 PM, the phone rings.

I answer it before the first ring finishes. I know who it is. I know it the way I know the signal was always going to be there — with a certainty that operates below thought, in the body, in the blood.

"Callahan." Her voice is flat. Not calm — flat. There's a difference. Calm is a choice. Flat is what happens when all the available emotions are too large to express.

"Dr. Chen."

"I found it." A breath. "It's exactly where you said it would be."

The room is dark. My apartment is quiet. Somewhere outside, a car passes on College Street, its headlights sweeping across my ceiling and disappearing.

"Frequency, coordinates, timing. All of it." Another breath. This one is unsteady. Judith Chen, who eats the same lunch every day and tracks sunsets in a spreadsheet and has never once in my experience been anything other than meticulously controlled, is unsteady. "I've verified it twice. I'll verify it again tomorrow, but I already know what I'll find."

I don't speak. I can't speak. My hand is gripping the phone so hard my fingers ache, and my heart is doing something arrhythmic and unhelpful, and there are tears on my face that I didn't authorize.

Silence. Five seconds. Ten.

"Who are you?" she says.

It's not a question. It's a door opening. It's a door I've been standing in front of for almost two years, waiting, building a case, building a life, building the kind of credibility that a question like this requires.

"Can I come to your office tomorrow?" I say.

"Seven AM," she says. "Bring coffee. Black."

She hangs up.

I set the phone on the nightstand. I stare at the ceiling. The Barbicide smell drifts up through the floorboards, chemical and clean, and the silence in my apartment is total, and I feel something I haven't felt since I arrived in this timeline three years ago.

Not hope — I've had hope. Hope is cheap. Hope is what you have before you have evidence.

This is the beginning of proof. And proof changes everything.

I don't sleep. But for the first time in weeks, it's not because I'm afraid.

Chapter Nine: The Thing About Trust

The thing about trust is that it's not a binary. It's not a switch you flip, on or off, believe or don't. Trust is a spectrum, and most of the interesting human drama happens in the gray middle where you trust someone enough to show up but not enough to stop watching the door.

Chen trusts me enough to answer the phone at eleven at night. She trusts me enough to run three independent analyses on a signal I told her to look for. She trusts me enough to list her name next to mine on a nonprofit application.

She does not trust me enough to stop asking how.

---

Her office at the University of Iowa is smaller than you'd expect for someone of her reputation. Two desks pushed together to form one large workspace, both buried under printouts and journals and the particular organized chaos of a mind that processes information faster than it can file it. Boltzmann occupies the only clear surface — the corner of the left desk — and watches me with the serene indifference of a creature who has never once doubted his own place in the universe.

It's a Tuesday afternoon in October. The leaves outside her window have gone the color of old pennies. Chen is sitting cross-legged in her office chair, which she does when she's about to say something she's been thinking about for days. I've learned her tells. She's learned mine. This is what four months of weekly meetings will do to two people who are fundamentally incapable of small talk.

"The scientific community dismissed it," she says. Not angry. Factual. The way you'd say the sky is blue or water is wet. "Ramirez at Caltech called it instrument noise. Whitfield at MIT didn't even respond to my email. Davidson responded, but only to suggest I recalibrate." She scratches Boltzmann behind the ears. He closes his eyes. "They're wrong."

"I know."

"You know." She studies me over the top of her glasses. Chen wears reading glasses now, thin silver frames that make her look like a librarian who could also dismantle your argument at the molecular level. "You know because you predicted it. You told me the frequency. You told me the coordinates. You told me the approximate amplitude. You were off by less than two percent on all three parameters."

"Yes."

"For a signal that no theoretical framework predicts."

"That's correct."

She uncrosses her legs. Crosses them the other way. Boltzmann adjusts without opening his eyes.

"Nate." She says my name like it's the first line of a proof she hasn't finished yet. "I've spent four months trying to find any possible way you could have derived that prediction from existing data. Any way at all. A model I haven't considered, a paper I haven't read, a dataset I don't have access to. I've gone through everything. ArXiv, JSTOR, internal preprints from three different research groups. I called in a favor from a colleague at CERN. Nothing. There is no path from publicly available information to the prediction you gave me."

"I know."

"Stop saying 'I know.' It's not helpful."

"Fair enough."

She takes off her glasses. Sets them on the desk. Boltzmann opens one eye, decides this doesn't concern him, closes it again.

"You're not guessing," she says. "You've never been guessing."

The room is quiet except for the hum of her desktop computer and the distant sound of undergraduates in the hallway, their voices carrying the particular carelessness of people who think the future is a long way off.

"No," I say. "I'm not guessing."

"Then what are you doing?"

I've rehearsed this. I've gone over it in my head dozens of times, standing in the shower, lying in bed, driving the forty minutes between Cedar Falls and Iowa City with the radio off and the window down. I've tried a hundred different phrasings. None of them are good enough for a physicist. None of them would be.

"I have foreknowledge of events I cannot explain the source of."

She stares at me.

"I know things that are going to happen. Some things. Not everything — there are gaps, inconsistencies, details I'm certain about and others I'm less sure of. But the signal was one of the certain ones. And I need you to trust the data, not the mechanism."

Chen doesn't move. Her face does something I've never seen it do before — it goes completely blank. Not angry, not skeptical, not curious. Just blank. The face of a woman whose entire framework for understanding reality has just been handed a variable it can't solve for.

"That," she says slowly, "is the opposite of science."

"I know."

"I told you to stop saying that."

"Sorry. But it's true. I know it's the opposite of science. I know it violates everything you believe about how knowledge works. I know you need mechanism, and I can't give it to you, and I know how maddening that is for someone who has spent her entire career insisting that the universe is knowable if you ask the right questions."

"Don't tell me what I believe."

"Okay."

"Don't tell me what I need, either."

"Okay."

She picks her glasses back up. Puts them on. Takes them off again. Boltzmann, sensing the disturbance in his immediate environment, stands up, turns in a circle, and lies back down facing the other direction.

"The data is clean," she says. "I've verified it three times. The signal is real. It corresponds exactly to what you described. And there is no conventional explanation for how you could have known it was there."

"That's all true."

"So either you have access to classified information from a source you can't reveal, which would make you some kind of intelligence asset, which frankly seems unlikely given that you're a twenty-year-old emergency management major from Cedar Falls—"

"Thanks."

"—or you're telling the truth, and you have foreknowledge of events through a mechanism neither of us understands. Which is, to put it mildly, not a hypothesis I can publish."

"I'm not asking you to publish it."

"Then what are you asking?"

"I'm asking you to keep working with me. To trust that the predictions I give you will continue to be accurate. To help me build something that can use that accuracy to save lives. I can't give you the why. I can give you the what."

She's quiet for a long time. Outside, the light has shifted. The pennies on the trees have gone copper, then bronze. Somewhere in the building, a door closes.

"I'm going to need more data points," she says.

"I can give you more data points."

"And I'm going to keep looking for the mechanism."

"I wouldn't expect anything less."

She reaches down and picks up Boltzmann, settling him in her lap like a gray, disinterested pillow. He tolerates this with the long-suffering patience of a cat who knows he is essential to his human's cognitive process.

"The thing that bothers me," she says, stroking Boltzmann's back, "is not that I can't explain it. I've spent my career working on problems I can't explain. That's the job. The thing that bothers me is that you're not surprised. You told me about a signal no one has ever detected, I confirmed it exists, and you were not surprised. Not even a little."

"No."

"Most people, if they made a prediction that extraordinary and it came true, would be... something. Excited. Scared. Vindicated. You were none of those things. You were relieved."

She's right. She's exactly right. When she called that night in June — eleven PM, her voice tight with something that wasn't quite excitement and wasn't quite fear — the feeling that went through me was the feeling of a load redistributed. Not lighter. Just shared.

"I've been carrying this alone for a long time," I say. "Having someone else see it — even one piece of it — that matters more than I can explain."

Chen looks at me over Boltzmann's head. Her expression has changed. The blankness is gone, replaced by something more complicated. The look of a woman who is choosing, deliberately and with full awareness of the cost, to step off solid epistemological ground.

"I don't trust you," she says. "I want to be clear about that. I don't trust people who withhold information. It's a professional hazard."

"That's fair."

"But I trust the data. And right now, the data says you're worth listening to. So I'll keep listening. But Nate—" She holds up one finger. Boltzmann bats at it. "If you're wrong about something, even once, in a way that suggests you've been fabricating or manipulating, we're done. Not angry done. Just done. I'll walk away and I won't look back."

"I understand."

"Good." She sets Boltzmann on the floor. He lands without sound and immediately begins grooming himself. "Now. You mentioned a nonprofit."

---

The thing about Frank Callahan is that he prepares.

He doesn't announce. He doesn't explain. He doesn't sit you down and say, "Son, I think we should spend some quality time together because I've been reflecting on our relationship and I believe there are things that need to be said." That's not how Frank operates. Frank operates by packing the truck the night before.

I come downstairs on a Saturday morning in late October and the cooler is already by the front door. The rods are in the bed of the truck, strapped down with bungee cords. There are sandwiches in a paper bag — ham and Swiss on white, mustard, no lettuce, because Frank considers lettuce on a fishing sandwich to be an affectation. The thermos is full. The truck is gassed up. He's sitting at the kitchen table in his canvas jacket, drinking coffee, reading the Courier, and he looks up when I come in and says, "Cedar River?"

Not "Do you want to go?" Not "I was thinking maybe we could." Just the destination. As if the decision has already been made and we're past the point of discussing it.

"Sure," I say.

"Coffee's on," he says.

I pour a cup. We don't say anything else. We don't need to.

---

The Cedar River at dawn in late October is a study in gray. Gray water, gray sky, gray mist that sits on the surface like something alive and breathing. The trees along the bank are mostly bare, their branches like cracks in the sky, and the few leaves that remain are the dull rust of things that have given up being beautiful and are just waiting to fall.

Frank backs the truck to within twenty feet of the bank, a spot he's been using since before I was born. There's a flattened patch of grass that might be natural and might be the accumulated memory of a hundred truck tires over thirty years. He kills the engine and we sit for a moment in the cab, listening to the metal tick as it cools, watching the mist curl above the water.

"Good morning for it," he says.

He means the overcast. He means the temperature — low forties, cold enough to keep the bugs down, not cold enough to make your fingers useless. He means the river is running at the right speed, which he can tell from the sound, which is a skill I've watched him exercise my entire life without ever fully understanding how it works.

We unload without talking. It's a choreography we've been performing since I was six years old — he takes the rods, I take the cooler and the tackle box, we both ignore the camp chairs because Frank considers sitting while fishing to be only slightly less offensive than lettuce on a sandwich. We stand. We've always stood.

He rigs my rod before his own. He's done this since I was a kid and he's never stopped, even though I've been capable of rigging my own line since I was eight. I don't mention it. He doesn't either. It's one of those father-son rituals that would disintegrate if you looked at it directly, like trying to see a faint star by staring straight at it.

We cast. The lines arc out over the gray water and land with small, separate sounds. The current takes them. We stand about ten feet apart, which is the distance Frank has always maintained — close enough to talk, far enough to not have to.

For the first hour, we don't say much. A comment about the water level. An observation about a heron that lands upstream and stands motionless in the shallows, a gray thing in a gray world, patient beyond any human capacity for patience. Frank adjusts his line. I adjust mine. The mist thins but doesn't lift. The sky brightens from charcoal to pewter.

The sandwiches come out at eight. We eat standing up, rods propped in the notched branches Frank cut and stuck into the bank thirty minutes ago, another piece of the ritual. The ham is good. The mustard is the yellow kind, French's, the only brand Frank has ever purchased. The coffee from the thermos is strong enough to dissolve a spoon.

"Your mother wants to know if you're eating enough," he says.

"Tell her yes."

"I did. She doesn't believe me." He takes a bite of sandwich. Chews. Watches the water. "She says you look thin."

"I look the same as I've always looked."

"I know. But that's thin, so she's not wrong." He balls up his sandwich wrapper, puts it in his jacket pocket. Frank has never littered in his life. I genuinely believe it would cause him physical pain.

We go back to fishing. The heron is gone. The mist is gone. The sky stays gray but the light is different now — less like dawn and more like the world has simply decided this is as bright as it's going to get today and you can take it or leave it.

"I was thinking about my dad the other day," Frank says.

He says it the way he says everything — evenly, without preamble, as if this is a normal thing to bring up on a riverbank at eight-thirty on a Saturday morning. But I know it isn't. Frank has mentioned his father maybe a dozen times in my entire life, and most of those were single sentences delivered in passing, the way you'd mention a road you used to drive on that's been closed for years.

I don't say anything. I've learned that the best response to Frank beginning a thought is to let the silence do the work. If you jump in, he'll retreat. If you ask a question, he'll deflect. But if you just stand there and keep your line in the water and let the quiet stretch out like the river itself, he'll keep going. Sometimes.

"He wasn't bad," Frank says. "I know I've probably made him sound bad. The way I don't talk about him — that makes people think the worst. But he wasn't bad. He was just—" He reels in a few feet of line. Casts again. The motion is so practiced it looks involuntary, like breathing. "He didn't know how to be good. That's different from being bad. Bad is a choice. He never chose to be bad. He just never figured out how to be the other thing."

The river moves. A fish jumps somewhere upstream — a small flash of silver that's there and gone.

"He grew up on a farm outside Jesup. His dad — my grandfather — was a piece of work. I never met him. He died before I was born. But from what my dad said, and from what he didn't say, it wasn't a house where anyone learned how to be soft."

Frank looks at his line. Adjusts his grip on the rod. His hands are large, cracked from decades of mechanical work, and they hold the rod with a gentleness that always surprises me, as if the rod is something more fragile than graphite and fiberglass.

"He hit me once."

The words drop into the morning like a stone into still water.

"Once. I was fourteen. He'd been drinking — not falling-down drunk, but enough. Enough that whatever wall he kept between his feelings and his hands came down for a second. Open hand, across the face. Not hard enough to leave a mark. Hard enough."

I reel in. Cast again. The line whispers through the guides, a small bright sound against the quiet.

"And the thing I remember — the thing I've never forgotten — is what happened after. He looked at his hand like it belonged to someone else. Like he'd just watched a stranger do something terrible and he was trying to figure out how to intervene. And he sat down on the kitchen floor. Just sat right down on the linoleum. And he put his face in his hands and he stayed there."

Frank is quiet for a long time. A minute. Two minutes. The river doesn't care about human silence; it just keeps moving, carrying whatever it carries, taking it somewhere else.

"He never apologized. Not in words. But the next morning he made pancakes, which he had never done before in his life, and they were terrible — just awful, burned on the outside and raw in the middle — and he set them in front of me and he stood there at the stove with his back to me and neither of us ate and neither of us talked and that was his apology."

I feel something in my throat that isn't a word and isn't a sound. It's just pressure. The accumulated weight of knowing your father's pain and not being able to carry it for him.

"I decided something that day," Frank says. "Standing at the bus stop after those pancakes, waiting to go to school. I decided I would never raise my hand to my kids. Never. Not once. Not even close."

"You never did."

"No. But here's the part I didn't figure out until later." He looks at me. His eyes are the same gray as the river, the same gray as the sky, and for a moment the whole world is one color — the water and the air and my father's gaze all the same shade of not-quite-silver. "I couldn't just put away the hand. I had to put away the whole volume. You understand? I couldn't figure out how to be loud without being him. I couldn't figure out how to raise my voice about anything — anger, sure, but also the rest of it. The good stuff. I turned the whole thing down because I didn't trust myself to know where the line was."

A fish takes my line. I feel the tug, the small electrical urgency of something alive at the other end, and I let it go. I don't set the hook. The fish takes the bait and moves on and I don't care because my father is telling me why he is the way he is and I have been waiting for this my entire life without knowing I was waiting for it.

"So when I didn't — when I don't—" He stops. Reels in. Casts. The mechanical motion giving him somewhere to put his hands while his voice does something it doesn't know how to do. "I want you to know it isn't because the feeling isn't there. The feeling is there. It's always been there."

"I know, Dad."

"Okay." He nods. Once. That's it. The conversation is over, because Frank has reached the end of what he can do, and pushing further would be asking him to be someone he isn't, and I would never do that. Not to him. Not to the man who packed the truck the night before and made sandwiches and drove us out here in the dark because this is how he says the thing he can't say.

We fish. The morning gets as bright as it's going to get. The cooler sits between us on the bank, sweating faintly in the cool air. At one point Frank catches a smallmouth bass, maybe two pounds, and holds it up for me to see with a look on his face that is as close to delight as Frank Callahan gets, which is a slight widening of the eyes and a barely perceptible uptick at the corner of his mouth. He releases it. It flashes once in the gray water and is gone.

Around noon, he starts packing up. The choreography in reverse — I take the cooler, he takes the rods, we load the truck and he straps everything down with bungee cords, checking twice because Frank checks everything twice.

We're in the cab, engine running, heat on because the afternoon has gone cold, and he's about to put it in gear when he stops. Looks out the windshield at the river, which is still doing what it's been doing since long before either of us was born and will be doing long after we're both gone.

"This is nice, bud," he says.

Not *I love you*. But the meaning is identical.

I open my mouth to respond and nothing comes out because the lump in my throat has been building for hours and it has chosen this particular moment to make itself immovable. So I nod. And he puts the truck in gear. And we drive home with the radio off and the heater on and the smell of river water and mustard and coffee in the cab, and it is the best day I will have for a long time, and I know it, and I let it be what it is.

The State of Iowa makes you jump through hoops to establish a nonprofit. The federal government makes you jump through flaming hoops while blindfolded. The 501(c)(3) application is twenty-eight pages long, not counting attachments, and it asks questions like "Describe your organization's planned activities in detail" and "List your organization's sources of financial support" and "Provide a narrative description of anticipated receipts and planned expenditures for your first three years of operation."

I fill it out at the kitchen table in my apartment in Cedar Falls on a Sunday afternoon in April. The table is a card table I bought at Goodwill. The chair is a folding chair. The pen is a Bic. The glamour of world-saving, as it turns out, looks a lot like doing your taxes.

**Northeast Regional Awareness Network.**

Mission: To advance community disaster preparedness, resilience education, and emergency response coordination across the Midwest and northeastern United States.

Board of Directors: Nathan P. Callahan, President and Executive Director. Dr. Mei-Lin Chen, Scientific Advisor.

I called Chen two weeks ago to ask if she'd agree to be listed. She was quiet for long enough that I thought the call had dropped.

"Scientific advisor," she said finally. "To a disaster preparedness nonprofit."

"Yes."

"Founded by a twenty-one-year-old emergency management student who has unexplained foreknowledge of future events."

"That's a characterization, but yes."

"And this is going to — what? Train communities? Distribute information?"

"Among other things."

Another long pause. Boltzmann made a sound in the background — not a meow, exactly, more like a comment. An editorial observation.

"You're building infrastructure," she said. "That's what this is. You're building something that will already be in place when—" She stopped. "When what, Nate?"

"When it's needed."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the best one I have right now."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true."

She agreed. She agreed because the data was clean and the signal was real and because Mei-Lin Chen, whatever her reservations about my epistemological shortcomings, recognized that preparation has value independent of its justification. You don't need to understand why a dam might break to see the sense in building on high ground.

I list the registered agent as myself. The principal office as my apartment, which I will need to upgrade at some point, because operating the most important nonprofit on the planet out of a studio apartment above a laundromat is going to present logistical challenges eventually. The financial support section is mostly aspiration at this point — grant applications I haven't submitted yet, fundraising strategies that exist only in my notebook, the vague promise of "community partnerships" that I will need to actually forge.

I sign where it says to sign. Date where it says to date. April 15, 2012, which is also Tax Day, which feels appropriate given the amount of paperwork involved.

Northeast Regional Awareness Network. Boring. Institutional. The kind of name that slides right off your brain, that you'd scan past in a list of local organizations without pausing, that a journalist would have to look up twice to get right. Exactly what the world needs and will never notice until it matters.

I put the application in an envelope. I put the envelope by the door so I'll remember to mail it in the morning. I sit back down at the card table and look at the kitchen of my apartment, which is small and beige and contains nothing of significance except a folding chair, a Goodwill card table, and the twenty-one-year-old who just filed the paperwork that will, if everything goes according to plan — and nothing ever does, which is the whole reason for the paperwork in the first place — put a network in place that will save lives on a scale he can't tell anyone about yet.

I open my notebook. The blue one, the one I keep locked in my desk drawer, the one no one has ever read and no one ever will.

*Every relationship I have is built on a lie I'm telling for the right reasons. Is that better or worse than the lies I told for selfish ones? Chen trusts the data but not me. Frank trusts me but doesn't know what I'm doing. Mom trusts everything because that's who she is, and that's the one that keeps me up at night — being trusted completely by someone who doesn't know what they're trusting.*

*The fishing trip was the best day. The application was the most important day. I don't think those will ever be the same day. I think that's the cost.*

I close the notebook. Lock the drawer. Go to bed in a room that smells faintly of laundry detergent from the machines running downstairs, a sound like breathing, rhythmic and impersonal, the world turning its clothes over and over in warm water and calling it clean.

Chapter Ten: The Woman Who Didn't Care About Charm

The Northeast Regional Awareness Network's first public event draws forty-three volunteers to the multipurpose room in Maucker Union on a Saturday morning in October, which is about thirty more than I expected and exactly forty-three more than deserve to spend their weekend learning how to set up a water purification station.

I've been organizing this for six weeks. Printed the flyers myself at the campus copy center, talked three professors into offering extra credit for attendance, sweet-talked the Student Government Association into co-sponsoring so I could use the room for free. Chen reviewed the training materials and sent them back with so many red marks it looked like a crime scene. I incorporated every correction. She sent a one-line email: *Acceptable.*

The room smells like industrial carpet cleaner and the coffee I brewed at six a.m. in a forty-cup percolator I borrowed from the Methodist church. I've got stations set up around the perimeter — water purification, first aid triage, emergency communication, supply-chain logistics — each with a posterboard, a handout packet, and a prayer that enough people show up to justify the four months I spent getting NEORAN from a filing with the state to something that exists in physical space.

Forty-three people. I keep counting them like they might evaporate.

I'm running the welcome speech when she walks in.

Late. Not apologetically late — not ducking-through-the-back-row late. She comes through the main door, lets it close behind her with its full pneumatic wheeze, and walks to the nearest empty chair with the posture of someone who was delayed by something more important and has already stopped thinking about it. She's carrying a backpack with a broken zipper that she's repaired with a carabiner, and she's wearing a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled to her elbows and jeans with actual mud on them. Not artful mud. Mud from mud.

I don't stop talking. I'm mid-sentence about the Federal Emergency Management Agency's Community Emergency Response Team framework, which is the kind of sentence that has never once in human history made anyone fall in love.

She sits down. Opens a notebook. Starts writing before I've said anything worth writing down.

I keep going.

Here is what I notice in the first thirty seconds, and I want to be precise about this because later I'll try to reconstruct it and I'll get it wrong, I'll sand it down into something that sounds like a story, but right now it's not a story, it's an inventory: dark brown eyes set wider than expected, a nose with a bump on the bridge from a break that healed without intervention, a braid of black hair thick enough to moor a boat, and hands that are already moving across the notebook page with the efficiency of someone who has never doodled in a margin in her life.

I notice all of this. It registers in the same way a four-top of new customers used to register at DiSanto's — quick scan, assess, file away. It does not change my heart rate. It does not alter the trajectory of my morning. I am not, at this point, in love. I'm giving a speech about emergency preparedness to forty-three people in a multipurpose room, and one of them is a girl in a muddy flannel taking notes.

I want that on the record.

---

After the welcome, I split people into groups and rotate them through stations. I'm running the emergency-comms station myself, walking groups of eight through how to set up a neighborhood communication tree when cell towers go down, when landlines are compromised, when the infrastructure you've spent your whole life leaning on suddenly isn't there and you find out what you actually knew versus what you'd merely been told.

She's not in my rotation first. I see her across the room at the supply-chain logistics station, which is being run by a junior named Todd who I recruited because he'd taken a business logistics course and could theoretically explain a flowchart. Todd is explaining the basics of supply staging and distribution points. She's watching him with her arms folded. Not hostile. Diagnostic.

Then she's at my station.

"Nate Callahan," I say, and offer my hand, because I'm twenty-one and I still think charm is a transferable skill. "I'm the president of NEORAN. Thanks for coming out."

She shakes my hand. Her grip is firm in a way that isn't performative — it's just how her hand works. There's a pale scar across her right palm, smooth and deliberate, like a signature.

"Elena Vasquez," she says.

"Elena. Great. So this station is about emergency communication networks — basically how communities stay connected when traditional infrastructure fails. I've been working with FEMA protocols and—"

"I read the handout."

"—right. Great. So you know the basics. What I want to walk you through is the practical application, because the gap between theory and execution is where—"

"Why do you talk so much?"

This is said without malice. Without flirtation. Without any of the tonal indicators that would let me classify it as banter and respond accordingly. She asks it the way you'd ask someone why they're taking the long route to a destination you can both see.

Three of the other people in the group look at me. One of them, a girl named Keisha who's been to two of my planning meetings, covers her mouth.

"I—" I start, and for maybe the second time in this life, I have no immediate follow-up. "Habit, probably."

"Okay," Elena says. She nods at the posterboard. "What's the failure rate on these communication trees in actual disaster scenarios?"

She's asking because she wants to know. Not because she's trying to make me uncomfortable, though she has accomplished that as a secondary outcome. I shift into the answer — real answer, not the polished version — and tell her that in post-event analyses, neighborhood communication trees fail about sixty percent of the time, usually because they depend on a single coordinator who is themselves affected by the disaster.

"So what's the redundancy?" she asks.

"Distributed nodes. You build in three layers of—"

"Show me."

I show her. I pull out the diagram Chen helped me redesign and walk through the redundancy architecture. Elena studies it. She bites the inside of her cheek while she thinks, a small lateral motion of her jaw that I will eventually know as well as my own breathing, but right now it's just a thing a stranger is doing while she looks at my diagram.

"This is better," she says. "Your handout doesn't have this."

"I know. I ran out of time to update it."

"You should update it."

"I will."

"Okay."

She moves on to the next station. The whole interaction takes four minutes. I have been assessed, found partially adequate, and dismissed. Keisha looks at me with undisguised delight.

"She's fun," Keisha says.

"That's one word for it," I say.

---

The rotation takes two hours. I'm breaking people into smaller working groups for the afternoon practical exercise — a simulated disaster scenario where each group has to build a response plan from scratch — when Todd comes up to me looking vaguely concussed.

"That girl Elena," he says.

"What about her?"

"She redesigned my entire station."

"She what?"

"My supply-chain station. I was doing the flowchart, the standard distribution-point model, and she asked if she could use the whiteboard. I said sure. She mapped out an alternative supply chain in—" He checks his phone, where he apparently noted the time. "Nine minutes."

"What kind of alternative?"

"I don't know, man. She was talking about hub-and-spoke versus distributed models and livestock feed supply chains and adaptive routing and I just kind of stood there." He pauses. "It was really good. I think it was really good. I didn't understand all of it, but it was really good."

I go look at the whiteboard.

She's drawn a supply-chain model that uses a distributed node system — not unlike the communication tree redundancy I showed her, which means she absorbed that in four minutes and applied it to a different problem — adapted for rural and semi-rural disaster response. The nodes aren't centralized distribution points. They're existing infrastructure: farms, churches, feed stores, veterinary clinics. She's drawn lines of supply and communication between them that route around likely failure points. She's annotated it in handwriting that is small and precise and entirely legible.

At the bottom, in smaller writing: *Based on Buchanan County feed distribution, 2009 ice storm.*

I stand in front of this whiteboard, and something happens that I don't have a strategic explanation for.

I've been running game theory on people since I was fourteen years old. Every interaction, every relationship, every conversation — I see the angles. I see what people want, what they're protecting, what lever moves them. It's not malicious. It's not even conscious anymore. It's just the operating system. You live one life and lose everything and come back, and the pattern recognition doesn't turn off. Everyone is a system. Everyone has inputs and outputs. Everyone can be modeled.

Elena Vasquez, standing across the room explaining something to another volunteer with one hand on her hip and the other pointing at a map, is not a system I can model. Not because she's complex — everyone's complex. Because there is nothing between who she is and what she shows. No gap between the interior and the exterior. No angle to find because there is no angle. She is a person who drew a supply-chain model on a whiteboard because the existing one was insufficient, and she is now explaining something to a stranger because the stranger asked, and both of these things are happening for the same reason: the work is in front of her, so she's doing it.

My chest does something I don't authorize.

---

I assign her to my group for the afternoon exercise. This is not strategic. I'm lying. It is strategic. I just don't know what the strategy is, which is a new and deeply uncomfortable experience.

The scenario: a Category 3 tornado has struck a small Iowa community. Power is out. Roads are compromised. The hospital is damaged. Each group has to build a forty-eight-hour response plan covering shelter, medical triage, supply distribution, and communication.

There are six people in my group. Within ten minutes, Elena and I are functionally running it together. Not because we planned to. Because the other four are doing what volunteers typically do in simulations — waiting for instructions, second-guessing, treating the exercise like a test with a right answer. Elena and I are doing what you do in an actual emergency, which is make decisions with insufficient information and fix them later.

She's good. She's really good. She thinks in systems, which I already knew from the whiteboard, but watching her do it in real time is different. She doesn't build plans top-down. She starts with what's available and works outward. What do we have? What do we need? What's the gap? How do we close it?

"Veterinary clinics," she says, pointing at the map of the fictional town.

"What about them?"

"Surgical supplies. Anesthesia. Antibiotics. Suture kits. If the hospital's compromised, every vet clinic within twenty miles has a secondary surgical facility that nobody's thinking about."

"That's—" I pause. "That's really smart."

"It's not smart. It's obvious if you've ever been in a vet clinic."

"Most people haven't."

"Most people don't know where their food comes from either. Doesn't make it a mystery." She's already writing it into the plan. "What about fuel?"

We work. She handles supply logistics. I handle communication and coordination architecture. We don't discuss the division of labor. It happens the way water finds a channel — each of us moving toward the part of the problem our brain is shaped to solve. When we disagree — and we do, twice, about evacuation routing and medical triage priorities — she argues with data, not volume. I find myself arguing back the same way. Not performing. Not charming. Just saying what I think and defending it until one of us is wrong.

I'm wrong about the triage priorities. She's wrong about the evacuation routing. We fix both.

At the end of the exercise, our plan is the best in the room by a margin that's almost embarrassing. The other groups present competent, textbook-adjacent responses. Ours looks like something that might actually work.

"Where did you learn logistics?" I ask her while the last group is presenting.

"My grandfather's ranch. Aurelio Vasquez. Runs about four hundred head of cattle on six hundred acres outside of Masonville."

"That's a lot of logistics for one ranch."

"It's a lot of logistics for five people, which is what we've had since I was thirteen."

She says it flat. Not inviting a follow-up question. Not closing the door on one either. Just stating a fact about the number of people who run a six-hundred-acre cattle ranch.

"What happened when you were thirteen?" I ask, because not asking would be a kind of cowardice, and I'm starting to suspect she can smell cowardice the way some people smell rain.

"My father was deported."

She says it the way she'd say the well pump broke or the fence line came down in a storm. A thing that happened. A problem that had to be solved.

"ICE came to the house on a Tuesday in March. He'd been here twenty-six years. Had a green card — expired, technically, but he'd filed for renewal. Didn't matter. My mother stopped functioning for about a year. My brother was nine." She bites the inside of her cheek. "So I learned logistics."

I don't say I'm sorry. I almost do, but I look at her face and I see that she has been offered that particular word by hundreds of people in the thirteen years since it happened, and that the word has never once made the situation better, and she knows that, and she would rather I didn't.

"Is he back?" I ask.

"No. He's in Puebla. We talk on Sundays." She pauses. Adds, with a precision that tells me she's thought about this exact phrasing: "My mother is better. Not the same, but better. She manages the books for the ranch now. She's good at it."

"And you're here."

"I'm here. Pre-vet. Large-animal surgery." She says it with the same matter-of-fact certainty I've heard from exactly one other person in my life, and that person was Chen talking about atmospheric physics. "Going to be the best large-animal surgeon in the state, then I'm going back to Buchanan County and opening a practice."

"That's specific."

"Why wouldn't it be?"

I don't have an answer for that. Most people our age are still trying on futures like clothes at a department store, holding them up in the mirror, putting them back. Elena has apparently selected her future with the same efficiency she used to redesign Todd's supply chain, and I believe her in a way I rarely believe anyone.

---

The event ends at four. People filter out. I'm breaking down stations, stacking chairs, emptying the percolator that still has three cups of burnt coffee in it, when Elena comes back from the restroom with her backpack on.

"You're tearing down alone?"

"Todd had a thing."

"Todd had a thing." She repeats it without inflection, which somehow makes it devastating. She sets her backpack on a chair and starts stacking the remaining chairs along the wall.

We work in silence for several minutes. It is the most comfortable silence I have experienced with another human being since Marcus and I sat on the tailgate of his truck last spring and didn't talk for twenty minutes.

"You want to get coffee?" I ask when we're done. "There's a place on College Hill that doesn't completely ruin it."

"Okay."

We walk to the Cup of Joe on College Hill. The air outside is October in Iowa — that particular quality of cold that isn't winter yet but knows winter's number and has already made the call. The trees along University Avenue are turning, and the light comes through them at the angle that makes everything look like a photograph someone's parents would hang in a hallway.

Elena walks like she's going somewhere. Not fast, not slow. Purposeful. She doesn't fill the silence between Maucker Union and College Hill, and I realize, with a kind of vertigo, that I'm not filling it either. I am walking next to a person and not performing. Not narrating my own life in my head. Not running angles. Just walking.

It's terrifying.

---

The coffee shop is half empty, which in a college town on a Saturday afternoon means it's at capacity for people who actually want to be there versus people using it as a backdrop for their social media presence. We order — black coffee for her, which I should have predicted — and sit at a table by the window.

"Why emergency management?" she asks.

"Because I think something bad is coming and I want people to be ready for it."

I say it before I've decided to say it. It comes out plain. No humor. No qualification. No thirty-percent-true version wrapped in self-deprecation. Just the thing.

Elena looks at me. Not with the expression I usually get when I say something like this, which ranges from polite concern to active suspicion. She looks at me the way she looked at the whiteboard. Assessing the information. Checking it against what she already knows.

"What kind of something bad?"

"I don't know the specifics." This is a lie, but it's the same lie I told Chen, and it's the closest I can get without sounding insane. "I know that our emergency infrastructure is inadequate for what's coming. I know that the gaps are predictable and fixable if someone starts fixing them now."

"And you're that someone."

"I'm a twenty-one-year-old with FEMA certifications and a registered nonprofit. I'm the someone who's available."

She drinks her coffee. Considers this.

"That's an honest answer," she says.

"You seem like you'd know if it wasn't."

"I would."

This is not flirtation. I want to be clear about that. This is two people establishing the terms of a conversation, and the terms are: say what you mean or don't talk.

I have never in my life — either of my lives — had a conversation on these terms. Every interaction I've had since I was fourteen has involved some layer of performance, some calibration of how much truth to show, some angle. Even with Marcus, even with Frank, even with Chen — there's a room they can't see into, a gap between who I am and what I present, and the gap is where I live.

Elena doesn't seem to have a gap. And sitting across from her, I find that mine is shrinking.

She talks about veterinary medicine. Not the romantic version — not the James Herriot rolling-hills-and-baby-lambs version. The real version. The four a.m. calving emergencies. The economics of large-animal care in rural communities. The fact that there are fewer large-animal vets every year because the money's in small-animal practice, in urban clinics with designer dogs and payment plans, and meanwhile ranchers are driving their horses two hours for surgery because there's nobody closer.

"The whole system is collapsing from the edges in," she says. "Nobody notices because it's rural. Rural problems are invisible until they become urban problems."

"That's also true of emergency management."

"I know. That's why your whiteboard diagram was interesting." She pauses. "Not your handout. Your handout needs work."

"You mentioned that."

"I'm mentioning it again."

I laugh. Actually laugh, not the performative version I've been deploying since high school. She doesn't smile, but something shifts at the corner of her mouth, a micro-expression that I will later learn is the Elena Vasquez equivalent of a standing ovation.

She talks about Aurelio. The ranch. The way her grandfather manages cattle — not as inventory but as a system, each animal a variable in an equation that includes grass and water and weather and economics and the particular stubbornness of a twelve-hundred-pound Hereford that has decided it doesn't want to go through a gate. She talks about her brother, Miguel, who's sixteen now and wants to study engineering, and she says it with a pride that she doesn't dress up or explain.

She talks about her mother's depression. Not at length. In one sentence: "She sat in the kitchen for eight months and I couldn't fix it, so I fixed everything else." And then she moves on, because the sentence is complete, because it contains everything necessary, and because Elena Vasquez does not waste language.

I listen.

I want to emphasize this. I listen. Not the way I usually listen, which involves running predictive models on what the other person is going to say and preparing my response before they've finished. I listen the way you listen to weather, or water, or something that doesn't require your input to be real. She is talking, and I am hearing her, and I am not constructing a version of her in my head that serves some strategic purpose. She is just here. Talking about cattle and surgery and a ranch in Buchanan County and a father in Puebla and a future she has defined with the specificity of a blueprint.

This is new.

I have spent seven years — my entire second life — knowing more than everyone around me, seeing further, planning better, manipulating outcomes. I have been the smartest person in every room by the cheat code of having already lived through the answers. And Elena Vasquez knows things I don't know. Not because she's been given information from the future, but because she's been paying attention to the present with an intensity I've never managed. She knows what a collapsing supply chain looks like because she's held one together with her bare hands. She knows what loss looks like because she watched her mother dissolve and decided to become solid instead.

She is nineteen years old and she is more competent than anyone I've ever tried to be.

The coffee gets cold. Neither of us notices. The window light shifts from afternoon to early evening, and College Hill does that thing it does in October where the streetlights come on before you're ready for them, and the world outside goes amber and specific.

"I should go," Elena says, eventually. She stands up and puts her backpack on — the one with the carabiner holding the broken zipper shut — and pushes her chair in. "I have a physiology exam Monday."

"Sure. Yeah." I'm standing too. I don't remember standing. "Thanks for today. You were — the whiteboard thing was—"

"I know."

I almost laugh again. She knows. Of course she knows. She doesn't need me to tell her she was good. She was there.

She gets to the door. Opens it. The October air comes in and rearranges the warmth of the coffee shop. She turns back.

"Same time Thursday?"

"Thursday?"

"You're doing this again, right? The training events. There's more to cover. Your triage station was weak."

"I—" I hadn't actually planned a recurring schedule. I'm planning one now. "Yeah. Thursdays. Same time."

"Okay." She nods. Leaves. The door closes behind her with the same pneumatic wheeze it made in Maucker Union this morning, and I watch her walk down College Hill with that purposeful stride, braid swinging against her backpack, and she doesn't look back because why would she, she's going somewhere.

I stand in the doorway of the Cup of Joe on College Hill in Cedar Falls, Iowa, on a Saturday evening in October 2012, and I am grinning. I can feel it on my face, this stupid, unstrategic, completely indefensible grin, and I don't know what to do with it. I have no plan for this. I have no angle, no model, no prediction, no advantage.

A girl who doesn't care about charm told me my handout needs work and asked if I'd be here Thursday, and I am standing in a doorway grinning like a man who has just discovered that the future he's been so carefully engineering has a variable he never accounted for.

Thursday.

I'll be here Thursday.

---

Chapter Eleven: The Letters We Don't Send

The attic smells like cardboard and dust and something older — mothballs, maybe, or the particular staleness of air that hasn't moved in years. Diane stands at the bottom of the pull-down ladder, hands on her hips, squinting up into the rectangle of dark.

"I've been saying I'd do this since 2009," she says.

"And yet."

"And yet." She gestures upward. "You first. If something's living up there, I'd rather it bite you."

I climb the ladder. The bulb works, barely — forty watts of yellow light that turns everything the color of old photographs. The attic runs the length of the house, low-ceilinged, the rafters close enough to scrape my head if I stand straight. Boxes everywhere. Stacked, labeled in Diane's careful handwriting, some in my father's blocky print. Christmas decorations. Tax records 2001-2005. Baby clothes — Nate.

That one I leave alone.

We're cleaning out because Diane wants to convert part of the attic into usable storage — shelves instead of chaos. It's a Saturday in early December, cold enough that I can see my breath when I first get up there, before the space heater she hands up starts taking the edge off. We work for two hours, sorting boxes into keep, donate, and trash. She stays downstairs mostly, receiving what I hand down, making decisions. I'm the labor. She's the authority on what matters.

It's in the third hour that I find the journal.

It's not hidden. It's in a box labeled "Diane — personal," wedged between a stack of old birthday cards and a ziplock bag of loose photographs. A composition notebook, the black-and-white marbled kind, with her name written on the inside cover in ink that's faded to brown.

I should put it back. I know that immediately, the way you know not to touch a hot stove — the knowledge arrives before the temptation. But my hand is already opening it, not to the first page but to the middle, where the spine is softest, where it falls open naturally.

The entry is dated March 14, 2010. Two years before now. A year before the overdose that, in another version of events, would have killed me.

Her handwriting is smaller than I expected. Tight, controlled, each letter formed completely before the next one starts. She writes the way she speaks — precisely, with a kind of determined clarity that doesn't waste space.

*I'm losing him. I can see it happening and I don't know how to make it stop. He came home last night at 3 AM, not drunk but not sober either, and when I asked where he'd been he smiled at me. That smile. The one that's supposed to make me forget the question. It works on everyone else. It used to work on me.*

*I watch him choose the easy thing every time and I don't know how to stop it. He's smart enough to coast and charming enough that coasting looks like effort. Who taught him that? Frank? Me? Did I make it too comfortable to fail? Is that the thing — that I caught him so many times he stopped believing in the ground?*

*I don't know what to do. I've tried talking. I've tried not talking. I've tried being angry and I've tried being patient and I've tried pretending I don't notice. Nothing changes. He slides a little further every month and covers it with that goddamn smile and I love him so much I could break something.*

*Sometimes I think about writing him a letter. Putting it all down. Everything I see, everything I'm afraid of. But what would that do? Add my fear to his pile? He doesn't need my fear. He needs something I can't figure out how to give him.*

I read it standing up, bent slightly under the rafters. Dust floats in the light from the single bulb. The space heater ticks. Downstairs, Diane is on the phone with someone — I can hear the murmur of her voice but not the words.

I read it again.

*Did I make it too comfortable to fail?*

In the timeline I came from, she eventually wrote that letter. Not this gentle, frightened version — a harder one. The one that said she couldn't watch anymore. The one that closed the door between us for almost a year, until the overdose cracked it open again, and by then the door was a different shape, and so were we.

She never has to write that letter now. Not because I spared her the fear — the fear was real, is real, was always going to be real because she loved a son who was drowning and she couldn't find the rope. She doesn't have to write it because I changed. Because the version of me who made that smile into a tool, who coasted on charm and called it living — that person made a different choice. Not a perfect one. Just a different one, early enough that the current shifted.

I close the journal. I put it back in the box exactly where I found it, between the birthday cards and the photographs. I fold the box flaps down. I stack it behind two other boxes, where it was before I moved them.

I don't tell her. Not now, not later, not ever. This is hers. The fear, the love, the handwriting that got smaller when she was scared — all of it belongs to her. My need to say *I read it, I understand, I'm sorry, you weren't the reason* — that need is real but it's mine to carry. Telling her would make me feel better. It wouldn't make her feel anything but exposed.

I pick up the next box. Christmas lights, tangled beyond reason.

"These are a lost cause," I call down.

"Throw them out," she calls back. "I'll buy new ones."

I throw them out. We finish by four. She makes coffee and we sit at the kitchen table and she talks about her plan for the shelves and I listen and I don't say anything about the journal and the coffee is too hot and I drink it anyway.

---

The ice storm hits on January 8th.

It starts as rain, which is worse than snow because people don't prepare for rain. Rain is inconvenient, not dangerous. Except when the temperature drops six degrees in two hours and every surface in three counties turns to glass.

I'm at the NEORAN office — which is still just a room in Chen's building with a donated desk and a phone that rings too loud — when the power goes out. It's 4:17 PM. The light doesn't fade; it just stops, like someone pulled a plug. Then the phone starts ringing.

Mrs. Halverson on County Road 12 — no heat, no generator, husband uses oxygen at night. The Mendez family on Pine — four kids, youngest is eight months, house temperature already dropping. Tom Bridger, who lives alone at the end of a dead-end road and whose landline is the only phone that works because he never trusted cordless. He calls to report that the road is impassable and also to ask if anyone can bring him cigarettes.

Elena arrives twenty minutes after the power goes out, already moving with purpose. She's in a coat too thin for the weather and boots meant for rain, not ice, and her first words to me are "The community center has a backup generator and room for a hundred. Who do we need to move?"

We don't discuss it. There's no meeting, no planning session, no consensus-building. There are people who are cold and there are buildings that are warm and between those two facts is logistics.

I start making calls. Elena starts making lists. Chen — who I called first — arrives with printed maps of the county showing every road, every house that's on our volunteer rolls, every location with backup power. He spreads them across the desk and starts marking routes with a red pen. "Ice is worst on the north side," he says. "Elevated roads. We'll need four-wheel drive for anything past Miller Creek."

"Who do we have with trucks?"

He checks his list. "Danny Yates. Jorge Paez. Maybe the Whitfield kid, the one with the F-150."

"Call them."

By six o'clock, we have the community center open. By seven, we have thirty-two people inside, most of them elderly, a few families with small children. The generator runs the lights and the heat but not the stove, so Elena organizes a cold-food station — sandwiches, granola bars, juice boxes that someone's church group donated. By eight, we have blankets from the Baptist church two blocks over, carried by hand because no one's driving on these roads unless they have to.

I coordinate the phone tree. This is the part no one thinks about — the calling. Not the dramatic rescue runs, not the shelter setup, but the hours of sitting with a phone pressed to your ear, working down a list, checking on people who say they're fine and sometimes are and sometimes aren't. Mrs. Halverson says she's fine. Her voice says she's scared. I send Jorge.

The cold is real. The community center is warm enough — sixty-four degrees, which feels tropical compared to outside — but every time someone opens the door, the air that comes in has teeth. The parking lot is a skating rink. Two volunteers fall just getting from their cars to the door. One of them, a college student named Amy, twists her ankle badly enough that she spends the rest of the night on a cot with a bag of frozen peas strapped to it.

Elena handles triage. Not medical triage — people triage. Who needs what, who's anxious, who's calm, who can help and who needs to sit down. She moves through the room like she's been doing this for years, talking to an eighty-year-old man about his cat (which is fine, which is at home, which he's worried about anyway) and then turning to bark at a teenager who's playing music too loud on his phone. She does this without a break in stride. She doesn't have a clipboard or a plan. She has attention.

Around midnight, someone brings a case of water bottles and two pizzas from the one place in town that still has gas ovens. I eat standing up, two slices, and don't taste either one. Elena takes one slice, eats half, wraps the rest in a napkin, and puts it in her coat pocket. "For later," she says.

"When is later?"

"When I'm hungry again."

At two AM, I call Chen. He went home at ten to sleep in shifts — his idea, and a good one. "How many?" he asks.

"Sixty-one in the center. Another forty confirmed sheltering with neighbors. I've got twelve addresses I can't reach by phone."

"Weather service says it'll warm above freezing by noon."

"That's ten hours."

"I know." A pause. "You sound tired."

"I'm fine."

I'm not fine. I'm running on coffee and adrenaline and the particular focus that comes from knowing that a mistake isn't theoretical — that if I forget Mrs. Halverson's oxygen, if I lose track of the Mendez baby's formula supply, if I fail to check on Tom Bridger at the end of his dead-end road, the consequences are immediate and physical and real.

By four AM, we've accounted for everyone on our list except three households. Two turn out to be empty — families who left town before the storm. The third, a woman named Grace Okoye, doesn't answer her phone for six hours, and when she finally picks up at 4:30, she says she fell asleep with her phone on silent and she's perfectly warm, thank you, because she grew up in Minnesota and owns four space heaters.

Elena finds me at five AM. I'm sitting in a folding chair near the back of the community center, phone in my lap, staring at the wall. Not sleeping. Not awake in any useful way. Just sitting.

"When did you last sleep?" she asks.

I have to think about it. "Yesterday. The day part of yesterday."

"That's not heroic, that's stupid. Sleep."

"There's still—"

"Sleep. Chen's back at six. I've got the phone tree. The generator's holding. Mrs. Halverson's oxygen is topped off. The Mendez baby is asleep. Tom Bridger has cigarettes. Sleep."

She lists it all. Everything I would check, every loose thread I'd pull before letting go. She lists it so I can't argue, because every argument I'd make she's already answered.

I sleep. Right there in the folding chair, which is uncomfortable enough that my body tries to refuse, and then exhaustion wins and I'm gone. At some point during the hour and a half before Chen arrives, someone drapes a blanket over me. I don't see who. When I wake up, it's there — a scratchy red wool blanket from the pile the Baptist church sent over, tucked around my shoulders with a care that goes beyond practical.

Elena is across the room, talking to someone on the phone. She doesn't look at me when I wake up. She doesn't need to.

---

The storm breaks at noon, just like the weather service promised. The temperature rises to thirty-four degrees, which is enough to start the thaw, and by evening the roads are passable. People go home. Volunteers clean up. Someone returns the blankets to the Baptist church, washed and folded.

The final count: sixty-one people sheltered, zero injuries beyond Amy's ankle, fourteen supply runs completed. NEORAN's first real test, and we passed it through stubbornness and phone calls and cold pizza.

I write it up for Chen, who wants data. He reads my report, nods, says "Good," and then, after a pause that suggests the word costs him something: "Really good."

From Chen, this is a standing ovation.

---

I call Marcus on a Thursday in late February. No reason. Just calling.

He picks up on the third ring. "Hold on," he says, and I hear him close a door. "Okay, I'm in the hallway. Priya's studying and she does this thing where she can't concentrate if anyone's talking. Even in another room. She says she can feel the vibrations."

"The vibrations."

"Her words, not mine. She's an engineer too, so she should know better, but love is blind to physics."

"How is she?"

"Terrifying. Smart. She made me reorganize my entire apartment by function instead of category, and I'm angry because it actually works better. My socks are with my shoes now. I hate it. I love her."

I laugh. It comes easy, which is the thing about Marcus — it always comes easy. Not because we avoid the hard stuff. Because the hard stuff sits underneath like bedrock, and on top of it we built this: phone calls where he tells me about his girlfriend's theories on sock organization and I tell him about the ice storm and we both laugh at Mr. Patterson, our eleventh-grade history teacher, who pronounced "Charlemagne" with a hard G every single time and none of us ever corrected him.

"Charlemagney," Marcus says, and I lose it for a full thirty seconds.

"Do you think he knew?" I ask.

"Absolutely not. That man had the confidence of someone who has never been corrected in his life."

"Maybe no one ever loved him enough."

"Deep, Nate. Really deep."

We talk for an hour. He tells me about his senior project — something with load-bearing calculations that I pretend to understand and he pretends not to notice I'm pretending. I tell him about NEORAN, about the ice storm, about the sixty-one people in the community center. I don't tell him about Elena. Not because it's a secret but because there's nothing to tell yet, and Marcus would hear something in my voice and make it into a thing and I'm not ready for it to be a thing.

"You sound different," he says near the end.

"Good different or bad different?"

"Just different. Busy. Like you're actually doing stuff instead of talking about doing stuff."

"Was I that bad?"

"You were a philosophy major, man. Talking about doing stuff was the whole degree."

"Fair."

"I gotta go. Priya's done studying, which means she's going to want to tell me about whatever she studied, which means I need to clear my brain for approximately ninety minutes of structural analysis."

"You love it."

"I love it," he agrees. "Talk soon?"

"Yeah. Talk soon."

I hang up. The apartment is quiet. Outside, February is doing what February does in the Midwest — holding the world in gray, promising nothing, delivering less. I sit with the phone in my hand and think about how easy that was. How an hour passed without effort or strategy or calculation. How I didn't perform. How he didn't ask me to.

This is what maintenance looks like. Not the dramatic repair, not the apology, not the grand gesture. Just a call on a Thursday. Just an hour. Just showing up consistently until consistency becomes invisible, until the friendship stops being something you work on and starts being something you live in.

---

The community center stays open as a warming station through the end of January, even after the ice storm passes, because the forecast keeps threatening and Chen — who trusts data — says the probability of another freeze is high enough to justify the cost.

Elena and I run it in shifts. This means we see each other every day, sometimes twice, always in the context of work. She coordinates volunteers on Tuesdays and Thursdays. I handle logistics on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays. We overlap on Saturdays.

She doesn't flirt. She doesn't signal. She argues with me about supply lists and scheduling and once, memorably, about whether canned soup counts as a meal or a supplement. ("It's a meal if it has protein," she insists. "If it's just broth and noodles, it's a suggestion.") She treats me the way she treats everyone — with directness that borders on impatience, with an attention that doesn't linger, with a competence that makes lingering unnecessary.

I am so far gone.

Not because she's beautiful, though she is. Not because of any single moment or gesture or word. Because I watched her spend thirty hours keeping sixty-one people safe and comfortable and calm, and she did it without performance, without needing anyone to notice, without once turning to check if someone was watching. She did it because it needed doing. When it was done, she went home and slept for fourteen hours and came back the next day and asked what was next.

I don't tell her any of this. There's a version of me — an older version, the one who died — who would have. Who would have turned the attraction into a move, the admiration into a line, the genuine feeling into a performance of genuine feeling. That version couldn't tell the difference between wanting someone and wanting them to want you.

I can tell the difference now. I think. Most days.

---

The warming station closes in mid-February. NEORAN keeps running. Chen publishes a report on the ice storm response — data on response times, resource allocation, volunteer hours. He emails it to the county commissioner's office. They don't respond, but Chen doesn't care. The data exists. It's on record.

Spring comes in slow. The gray lifts. The ground softens.

In March, Elena and I drive out to the Halverson farm to check on the oxygen setup. Mrs. Halverson makes us sit in her kitchen and drink coffee that's strong enough to dissolve a spoon. Her husband, Dale, shows me his workshop — a clean, bright room where he builds birdhouses from scrap wood. He gives me one. It's painted blue. "For your mother," he says, though he's never met Diane.

On the drive back, Elena is quiet. This isn't unusual — she's not someone who fills silence — but the quality of the quiet is different. Settled, almost.

"She's a good person," Elena says, meaning Mrs. Halverson. Or maybe meaning something larger. I don't ask.

"Yeah," I say.

The road unwinds. The fields on either side are brown and wet, waiting. Everything is waiting.

---

June. A Saturday. NEORAN's quarterly volunteer meeting, held in the community center because it's the only space big enough. Forty people show up, which is double what we had in October and triple what Chen projected. He looks at the room with an expression I've never seen on him before and I realize it's surprise. Chen doesn't get surprised by data. He gets surprised by people exceeding it.

The meeting runs long. Afterward, the diehards stay to clean up — folding chairs, sweeping, breaking down tables. Elena and I work opposite sides of the room. It's warm. Someone propped the doors open and the evening air comes in smelling like cut grass and the particular sweetness of early summer in the Midwest, when the heat is still a promise and hasn't yet become a threat.

By nine, everyone else has left. Elena sits on the edge of the small stage at the front of the room, legs dangling, drinking water from a bottle. I'm finishing the chairs.

"Leave them," she says.

"Three more."

"Leave them."

I leave them. I sit next to her on the stage. The room is wide and empty and the light coming through the windows has turned that shade of blue-gold that only happens in June, when the sun takes forever to set and the sky can't decide what color to be.

She's tired. I can see it in the way she holds herself — the straightness gone out of her spine, her shoulders dropped. She's been here since seven AM. Twelve hours. For a volunteer meeting that didn't pay her anything and won't show up on any resume and matters only in the way that unglamorous things matter, which is completely.

We sit for a while. She finishes her water. I don't say anything, because the silence is working and I don't want to interrupt it.

Then she leans over, just slightly, and her head comes to rest on my shoulder.

She doesn't say anything. Her breathing changes — slows, deepens. Within two minutes, she's asleep. Not pretend asleep, not resting-her-eyes asleep. Actually, genuinely asleep, the way you can only fall asleep when your body decides it trusts where it is.

I don't move.

My arm goes numb after twenty minutes. By forty minutes, it's tingling. By an hour, I can't feel it at all, and I shift my weight just enough to keep from falling over without disturbing her. The windows darken. The blue-gold turns to blue, then to something deeper. I can hear crickets starting up outside, tentative at first and then committed.

She sleeps for two hours.

I sit there the entire time, arm dead, back aching, completely still. I think about Diane's journal in the attic. I think about the ice storm, the sixty-one people, the blanket someone draped over me at five AM. I think about Marcus and Priya and sock organization and Mr. Patterson saying "Charlemagney" with absolute conviction. I think about Dale Halverson's blue birdhouse, which is sitting on Diane's porch railing right now, and how she smiled when I gave it to her and said "Tell the man I said thank you" and I did.

Three relationships. The mother whose private fear I'll carry without ever letting her know. The friend I called on a Thursday for no reason. The woman asleep on my shoulder who trusts me enough to be unconscious in my presence.

None of this is strategy. None of it is the mission. The mission is out there — the countdown, the preparation, the long and patient work of building something that might matter when the world needs it. That work continues. It has to.

But sitting here, in a darkening room, with a numb arm and a quiet mind, the personal work feels more real. More present. The journal I didn't mention. The call I didn't skip. The shoulder I'm not moving.

For the first time, maintaining the life feels like the point, not the cost.

Elena stirs. Lifts her head. Blinks. Looks at me with the confused clarity of someone surfacing from deep sleep.

"How long?" she asks.

"Not long."

She looks at the windows. Dark. She looks back at me.

"Liar," she says.

She stands. Stretches. Picks up her coat. At the door, she turns back.

"Your arm's going to hurt," she says.

"It already does."

Something crosses her face — not a smile, exactly. An acknowledgment. Then she's gone, and I'm alone in the community center with three unfolded chairs and an arm that's returning to life in painful increments, and outside the crickets are fully committed now, filling the dark with sound.

I sit there a while longer. There's no reason to. I just do.

Chapter Twelve: What Mud Reveals

The Cedar River doesn't give warnings. That's the thing people from elsewhere don't understand. It's not like a hurricane where you get five days of breathless weather coverage and time to board up and evacuate or be stupid about it. The Cedar just rises. You check the gauge in the morning and it's at twelve feet, which is fine, which is normal, and you go to class or work or whatever and by the time you're eating lunch someone's texting a photo of the Eighth Avenue bridge and the water's at sixteen feet and climbing and the National Guard is already stacking sandbags along the downtown flood wall.

April 2014. I'm twenty-two, three weeks from graduating, and I'm standing in the NEORAN office — which is really just a rented room above a vacuum repair shop on Main Street — watching the gauge updates on my laptop. Eighteen feet. The flood stage is fifteen. The record from 2008 is thirty-one, and nobody thinks we're going there, not this time, but the forecast has three more inches of rain tonight and the Waterloo gauge upstream is already at twenty-four and the math isn't hard.

Elena calls at noon. "Cedar Heights neighborhood. They're not on the priority list."

"I know."

"So what are we doing about it?"

That's how she frames things. Not *is there something we can do* or *should we get involved* or *what do you think.* What are we doing about it. The verb tense assumes action. The pronoun assumes partnership. Six months of working together and she still catches me off guard with the efficiency of it.

"I'm calling the volunteer coordinator now," I say.

"Already called. They need bodies on the sandbag line by two. I'm picking you up in twenty minutes."

She hangs up. She always hangs up first. I have never once ended a phone call with Elena Vasquez.

---

Here's what a sandbag line is actually like: it's not heroic. It's not cinematic. It's bending and lifting and passing and bending and lifting and passing until your lower back turns into a single fused rod of pain and your hands crack open from the wet burlap and you can't feel your feet because you've been standing in four inches of cold water for three hours and the bags weigh forty pounds each and there are a thousand of them and it's not enough. It's never enough. You're just trying to buy the river a reason to go around the houses instead of through them.

The Cedar Heights line has maybe forty people when we arrive. Town residents, a few UNI students, a church group from Dike, some National Guard kids who look about seventeen and are working twice as hard as anyone because they think someone's watching. The rain has stopped but the sky is the color of old pewter and the air smells like mud and diesel from the pump trucks.

Elena ties her braid up into a knot, rolls her sleeves, and steps into the line without a word. I take the spot behind her. We're passing bags from a flatbed truck to the wall, which is a double-stacked barrier along the property line of four houses on River Road. The water is already in the street. Not fast. Just there. Creeping up over the curb, pooling in the lawns, absolutely patient.

By four o'clock my hands are bleeding. Not dramatically — just the skin between my thumb and forefinger split open from the friction, and the mud gets in and stings. Elena's got the same thing. She wraps her hands in duct tape without stopping. A guy from the church group sees her do it and starts taping his own hands, and within twenty minutes the whole line is wrapped in silver tape, passing bags like a factory of robots.

We don't talk much. There's nothing to talk about. The river is coming and we are stacking bags against it and that is the entire universe. Occasionally someone shouts a number — the updated gauge reading, relayed down the line from someone's phone. Twenty-one feet. Twenty-two. Twenty-three.

At six o'clock a section of the barrier on the east end lets go.

It doesn't collapse all at once. That would be dramatic and therefore manageable. What happens is one bag at the bottom shifts — you can hear it, this wet sucking sound — and then the bag above it tilts, and then water starts threading through the gap, just a trickle at first, and then the trickle becomes a steady pour and the pour starts undermining the bags on either side and you've got maybe ninety seconds before the whole section unzips.

I'm fifteen yards away when it starts. There's a kid — sixteen, maybe seventeen, stringy blond hair, rubber boots three sizes too big — standing right in front of it, trying to shove a bag back into place with his hands, which is exactly what you should not do. The water coming through the gap is waist-high now and accelerating. It catches him at the knees and he goes down.

I don't decide to move. My body just does it. Three steps into the water, which is brown and shockingly cold and full of debris — sticks, garbage, a plastic lawn chair tumbling past like it's late for something — and I grab the kid by the back of his jacket and haul him up. He's coughing, flailing, gets a hand on my arm and grips hard enough to leave bruises I'll find tomorrow. The current is pushing us both toward the gap. I get my feet planted against something solid — a curb, maybe, or a buried sandbag — and shove him toward the dry side. Hands reach down and pull him up.

Someone pulls me out too. I don't see who. I'm on my knees on the wet grass, breathing hard, and there's mud in my mouth and my left ear is ringing for no reason I can identify.

Thirty yards down the line, Elena is already reorganizing. She's got six people pulling bags from a reserve stack and she's directing them into the gap with hand signals — actual hand signals, like she's done this before, which she has, because ranch work in Buchanan County means floods happen and you deal with them. She's knee-deep in water and she's not panicking. She's building.

It takes forty minutes to restabilize that section. Elena engineers a redirect — she angles a row of bags to channel the overflow into a drainage ditch instead of trying to stop it outright. It's smarter than what we were doing before. The Guard lieutenant watches her do it and nods and starts copying the approach on the next vulnerable section.

By nine o'clock the gauge has crested at twenty-five feet. The barrier holds. The four houses on River Road stay dry. A few basements on the next block flood anyway, but the main structures are okay. Somebody brings coffee in thermoses and we stand around in the headlights of parked trucks and drink it. The coffee is terrible. My hands are shaking too hard to hold the cup steady, so I grip it with both palms and drink like a child.

Elena is thirty feet away, talking to the Guard lieutenant. Her braid came undone hours ago and her hair is hanging in wet ropes over her shoulders. She's got mud up to her elbows and a streak of it across her forehead. She looks like she's been in a war. She looks completely at home.

---

The parking lot of the community center. Eleven at night. They opened the building for volunteers to warm up and use the bathrooms but most people have already gone home. My truck is parked under a light that's buzzing and flickering because of course it is. Elena's car is next to it. We walked out together, not talking, because we've been talking for fourteen hours and there's nothing left to say and also because something has shifted and we both know it and neither of us is acknowledging it.

I'm fishing my keys out of my pocket with numb fingers when she turns.

She steps forward. She takes my face in her hands. Her palms are cold and rough with dried mud and I can feel the tape still on her left hand. She kisses me.

It's not gentle. It's not tentative. It's not a question. It's direct and firm and tastes like bad coffee and river water and it lasts exactly long enough to be unambiguous and then she pulls back and looks at me and says:

"I've been waiting for you to do that for six months. You were taking too long."

I don't say anything. I genuinely cannot think of a single word in the English language. My mouth is open slightly. I probably look like I've been hit in the head with a sandbag, which, given the evening, would not be implausible.

"Okay," she says. "Say something."

"I was going to," I manage. "Eventually."

"Eventually." She almost smiles. Almost. "You run a disaster preparedness network and you can't act on obvious information in a timely manner."

"That's... accurate."

"Are we together? Yes or no."

"Yes."

"Good. Don't waste my time."

She kisses me again — shorter this time, punctuation rather than statement — and walks to her car. She gets in. Starts the engine. Rolls down the window.

"You should put antibiotic ointment on your hands. They'll get infected."

She drives away.

I stand in the parking lot for probably three full minutes, alone, my hands still shaking but not from the cold anymore, and I start laughing. Not at anything funny. Just because the universe has handed me something so clearly, so unequivocally good that the only appropriate response is the nervous laughter of a man who doesn't believe his luck.

Anyway. That's how it starts.

---

Dating Elena Vasquez is like being seen through a window you didn't know was transparent.

She doesn't play games. I don't mean this in the way people usually say it, which generally means *she plays different games* or *she plays games so well you can't tell.* I mean she genuinely, constitutionally does not know how to be indirect about what she wants. She wants to see me Tuesday, she says "I want to see you Tuesday." She thinks I'm being evasive, she says "You're being evasive." She's angry, she tells me she's angry and why. She's happy, she says so, and it lands like a declaration of fact rather than a confession.

I have no framework for this. None. In my first life I dated — well, I dated the way everyone dated. You text and then you wait the right amount of time to text back and you pretend not to care as much as you do and you interpret silences like they're encrypted messages. I was good at it, actually. The gamesmanship. I could time a text response to the minute, modulate enthusiasm, maintain the exact right amount of ambiguity to keep someone interested.

Elena makes all of that not just irrelevant but embarrassing. Like showing up to a conversation carrying a bag of tricks and realizing the other person is just standing there, open-handed, waiting for you to talk.

The first time I try the delayed-text thing — waiting forty-five minutes to respond to something she sent me — she calls me.

"Your phone broken?"

"No, I was just—"

"You read my text forty-three minutes ago. I can see the read receipt. If you're busy, say you're busy. If you're playing some game where you wait to respond, don't. I'm not interested in that."

"I'll respond faster."

"Respond when you want to respond. Just don't perform at me."

*Don't perform at me.* Five words. I think about them for a week. I think about how much of my life has been performance — the careful modulation, the managed image, the Nate Callahan who always has a sarcastic deflection loaded and ready. Elena doesn't want that person. She wants whatever's underneath, and the problem is that I'm not entirely sure what's underneath, because I've been performing so long the stage and the self have gotten confused.

But I try. I start responding to texts when I read them. I say "I miss you" when I miss her instead of wrapping it in a joke. I tell her when something scares me, which is new, which is terrifying, which she receives with the same practical calm she brings to everything.

"Good," she says, when I tell her I'm afraid NEORAN is going to run out of funding. "Now we can figure out what to do about it."

Not *don't worry about it.* Not *it'll be fine.* Just: now we can address this, together, because you've said it out loud and it's real now. She treats fear like information. Useful. Not shameful.

---

The dinner happens in May.

Diane has been asking to meet her for three weeks. I've been stalling because — well, because bringing your girlfriend to your parents' house is normal and that's exactly the problem. Normal things feel dangerous when your normal is a lie. Every honest moment I build with Elena is also a dishonest one, because she doesn't know why I started NEORAN, doesn't know why I came back to Cedar Falls, doesn't know the thing that sits at the center of everything I do. She thinks I'm a passionate guy who cares about disaster preparedness. She doesn't know I'm a man who's seen the end and can't figure out how to say so.

But Diane asks, and Elena says "Yes, I'd like that," and so on a Sunday evening in late May I park in my parents' driveway and sit in the truck for ten seconds doing nothing.

"Are you okay?" Elena asks.

"Yeah. My family is a lot."

"I grew up with eight cousins in a three-bedroom house. I can handle a lot."

Frank opens the door before we knock. He's wearing a clean flannel — which tells me Diane made him change — and his reading glasses are pushed up on his forehead, which tells me he forgot they were there. He looks at Elena. He looks at me. He extends a hand.

"Frank Callahan. You're the one who did the sandbagging redirect during the flood."

Elena shakes his hand. "You heard about that?"

"My son doesn't shut up about it. Come in, Diane's been cooking since noon."

Diane has, in fact, been cooking since noon. The kitchen is a disaster of the good kind — pots on every burner, the oven timer going, the table set with the actual good plates that come out maybe twice a year. She hugs Elena with both arms, the kind of hug that says *I've been waiting for you* in a way that's completely genuine and a little overwhelming.

Sophie, home from college for the weekend, is sitting on the counter eating a carrot. She looks at Elena. She looks at me. She eats the carrot.

"She's way too good for you," Sophie says.

"I know," I say.

Sophie points the carrot at Elena. "He knows. That's a good sign."

Dinner is pot roast, which is Diane's best thing and she knows it. Frank asks Elena about her family. She tells him — the ranch in Buchanan County, her grandfather Aurelio and the cattle, her mother Rosa's job at the clinic. She doesn't mention her father's deportation. Not yet. She will later, with me, privately, and that's her right.

But here's the thing that happens that I don't expect: Frank and Elena start talking about water drainage.

It starts with the ranch. Aurelio's been having trouble with a low pasture that floods in spring, and Elena describes the grade and the soil composition and the problem with the existing drainage tile, and Frank — Frank who can fix anything, Frank who has opinions about pipe grade the way other men have opinions about football — leans forward in his chair and starts asking questions. Real questions. Not polite dinner conversation, but the kind of engaged curiosity that means he's actually interested.

They talk about French drains for twenty minutes. Elena draws a diagram on a napkin. Frank disagrees with her about gravel depth and she pushes back and he pushes back harder and then she concedes one point and he concedes another and they arrive at a solution that's better than what either of them started with.

"You should come look at it," Elena says. "When you have a weekend."

"I might do that," Frank says, and he means it.

I am watching this — this man who spent my entire childhood at a volume somewhere between muted and silent, this man who told me on a fishing dock that he'd turned the whole volume down, leaning forward, animated, drawing pipe configurations on a napkin with a woman he met an hour ago — and I feel something in my chest that I don't have a name for. Not joy exactly. Something older. Like watching a bone set properly after being broken for years.

Diane is at the sink. She's washing a pot that doesn't need washing. I get up to bring my plate over and she has her back to me and she's crying. Quietly. The way she does it — just tears, no sound, her shoulders perfectly still.

"Mom."

She shakes her head. Wipes her eyes with the back of her wrist. Turns around with a smile that's mostly real.

"I'm fine. She's lovely, Nate. She's really lovely."

She's not crying because Elena is lovely, though Elena is lovely. She's crying because her son brought someone home and that someone made her husband talk for twenty minutes straight about gravel, and for Diane, who has been loving Frank through decades of silence, this is a gift she didn't know how to ask for.

I hug her. She holds on for a beat longer than usual. From the table, I can hear Frank explaining the difference between Schedule 40 and Schedule 80 PVC, and Elena asking follow-up questions that prove she already knows the answer but wants him to feel useful, and I think: this woman is a genius. Not an academic genius, though she's that too. A human genius. She knows how to make people feel seen.

After dinner, on the porch. Sophie and Elena are talking about something I can't hear. Frank sits down next to me.

"She's solid," he says.

From Frank, this is the highest compliment available. He doesn't say *nice* or *pretty* or *seems great.* Solid. Load-bearing. Reliable under stress.

"Yeah," I say. "She is."

"You tell her about your—" He waves a hand vaguely toward the sky. Toward everything. Toward the thing he doesn't have language for, the thing I told him on the fishing dock that he received with the quiet acceptance of a man who has heard worse and survived it.

"No."

He nods. Doesn't push. Looks out at the yard.

"You will, though."

Not a question.

---

She notices things.

That's the danger of dating someone like Elena. She files observations the way other people file taxes — methodically, on a schedule, with attention to discrepancies.

"Why disaster prep?" she asks one night. We're in my apartment, the one I rented after graduation — two rooms above a hardware store, which is a step up from the vacuum repair shop only in the sense that the sounds from below are more interesting. She's sitting on my couch with her legs folded under her, reading a veterinary pathology textbook. She asks the question without looking up, which means she's been thinking about it for a while and has decided to deploy it casually.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean, why. Most twenty-two-year-olds aren't running nonprofit emergency networks. What got you into it?"

I have answers for this. Practiced ones. I trot out the Parkersburg tornado story, which is true but not the truth. I talk about growing up in Iowa, about how rural communities are underserved in disaster response, about the gap between state emergency management and local readiness. All true. All incomplete.

She listens. She nods. She goes back to her textbook.

Ten minutes later, without looking up: "That's the what. I asked why."

"I just told you why."

Now she looks up. Her eyes are dark brown and steady and there's nothing in them that's angry but there's also nothing that's fooled.

"You told me the public version. The elevator pitch. It was good — I've heard you give it to donors. I'm not a donor."

"Elena—"

"You don't have to tell me now." She goes back to her book. "But you're going to tell me eventually. Whatever it is you're not saying."

The conversation ends there. She doesn't press. She doesn't sulk. She just — knows. She knows there's a room in me she hasn't been invited into, and she's patient enough to wait for the door to open but honest enough to point at it and say *I see that door.*

Marcus said almost the same thing to me once. *There's a room in you I'm not allowed into.* The difference is that Marcus said it with hurt. Elena says it with certainty. She's not asking for access. She's informing me that she's aware of the architecture.

---

NEORAN, meanwhile, is becoming a real thing.

In June I hire the first paid staff member — Jenny Okafor, a social work grad from UNI who can write grant applications in her sleep and has opinions about database management that border on the religious. She comes on at twenty hours a week, funded by a small grant from the Iowa Community Foundation that she wrote herself as part of her interview.

"I wrote the application to get the job that the application would fund," she tells me. "Circular, but effective."

Jenny handles the operational side — volunteer coordination, training schedules, equipment inventory, the grant pipeline. This frees me to do the part I'm apparently good at, which is standing in front of church groups and Rotary clubs and city council subcommittees and explaining why their community needs a communication network that works when the cell towers don't.

We file for 501(c)(3) status. We get it in four months, which Jenny says is unusually fast and which I attribute to the fact that our application was written by someone who treats paperwork as a competitive sport.

By the end of 2013 we have a functioning radio network covering Black Hawk, Bremer, and Buchanan counties. We have forty-seven trained volunteers. We have a relationship with the county emergency management office that is still skeptical but no longer adversarial. Chen is publishing a paper on our communication protocols in a regional emergency management journal, which gives us academic credibility we couldn't buy.

It's working. The thing I started in a dorm room because I saw a future I couldn't prevent is actually working. Not the preventing part — I can't stop the asteroid. But the infrastructure part. The network that will, when the time comes, be the difference between coordinated response and chaos. Between survival and the alternative.

Some nights I drive the county roads and check on the repeater stations — the radio towers we've installed on grain elevators and water towers with the permission of farmers who think we're preparing for tornadoes. Which we are. Among other things. I sit in my truck and listen to the test transmissions and think about timelines. 2039, maybe. Maybe later. The signal didn't come with a date, just a certainty. Somewhere in my lifetime. Somewhere in Elena's.

That thought is new. Elena's lifetime. It used to be abstract — the future, the impact, the population of Cedar Falls, Iowa, and its surrounding communities. Now it's specific. Now it has a face and a broken nose and a braid that comes undone in floods and hands that are calloused from ranch work and exam-steady from veterinary training and warm when they hold my face in a parking lot.

I am building something to save her life and she doesn't know it.

The point is: that's a terrible foundation for a relationship. I know that. I know it every time she looks at me with those steady brown eyes and I look back with something truthful sitting behind something false. I love her — I haven't said it yet, but I do, in the way that you love someone who has made the world larger by being in it — and the love is real but the context is a lie and I don't know how long a real thing can survive inside a false one.

---

Late September. My apartment. Two in the morning. The window's cracked open and the air coming in smells like burning leaves and the distant chemical sweetness of the ADM plant. Elena is beside me, her breathing slowing toward sleep, her hair spread across the pillow in the chaos it always becomes when freed from the braid.

"Nate."

"Yeah."

"You're going to tell me eventually." Her voice is thick, half-gone. "Whatever it is you're not saying."

I stare at the ceiling. The streetlight outside throws a stripe of yellow across the plaster. I can hear the building settle — old wood, old pipes, the particular complaints of a structure that has endured a hundred Iowa winters and will endure a hundred more, probably, unless it doesn't.

"Yeah," I say. "I will."

But she's already asleep. Her breathing is even and deep and her hand is resting on my chest, over the heartbeat she doesn't know is counting down.

She's already right. She's always already right. And the ceiling above us is blank and white and tells me nothing about how to say the thing I need to say to the person who deserves to hear it.

Outside, a truck shifts gears on University Avenue. The refrigerator hums. Elena breathes. The future sits in the corner of the room like a patient animal, waiting to be named.

Chapter Thirteen: Bridges and Thresholds

Marcus calls from a gas station in Waterloo, twenty minutes out, and I can hear the grin through the phone.

"Bro, Iowa is flat. Like, aggressively flat. Like the land is making a point."

"You've been driving through Iowa for three hours. This is just occurring to you now?"

"I was in denial. I kept thinking there'd be a hill. Just one. A bump. Anything."

"There's a river valley when you hit Cedar Falls. That's the best I can offer."

"I'll take it." A pause, the sound of a gas pump clicking off. "Twenty minutes?"

"Twenty minutes. Sophie's already made a casserole."

"Sophie cooks now?"

"Sophie's fifteen. She does a lot of things now."

He pulls into the driveway in a green Civic with a cracked windshield and Iowa State plates. The car looks like it's been through something — road salt damage, a dent in the rear quarter panel, a parking sticker from an apartment complex in Ames. Marcus unfolds himself from the driver's seat, all six-three of him, and the gap-toothed smile hasn't changed in six years. The Nike high-tops are new — bright white, fresh out of the box — but the Casio on his wrist is the same one, still running three minutes fast.

"Callahan." He grabs me in a hug that lifts me off the ground. Marcus has always been stronger than he looks, which is saying something because he looks strong.

"Put me down."

"Not until you admit Iowa is flat."

"Iowa is flat. Now put me down."

He does. Steps back. Looks at me the way he does every time — cataloging changes, filing them away. "You look good, man. Healthy."

"You look like you haven't slept in a week."

"Finals." He pops the trunk, pulls out a duffel bag. "Structural dynamics, fluid mechanics, and a capstone project that's actively trying to kill me. But I'm here. For the whole weekend."

Frank comes out the front door wiping his hands on a dish towel. He's fifty-three now and moves a little slower than he did when I first met him, but he's still built like a man who's spent his life under houses and inside walls. "You must be Marcus."

"Yes, sir." Marcus extends his hand. Frank takes it, and I watch them measure each other the way men do — grip, eye contact, the whole silent negotiation. Whatever Frank sees, he likes.

"Nate talks about you. Come on in."

---

Dinner is Sophie's casserole — a chicken and rice thing she learned from a YouTube video — plus a salad Diane made and bread Frank picked up from the bakery on Main. Marcus eats two plates and compliments Sophie until she turns red, which takes commitment because Sophie does not embarrass easily at fifteen.

"This is genuinely good," Marcus says, and he means it. Sophie knows he means it, which is why she thaws.

"Nate said you're studying engineering?"

"Structural. So bridges, buildings, load-bearing walls. That kind of thing."

Frank leans forward. This is his territory. "You know anything about residential foundation work?"

And they're off. Marcus and Frank spend twenty minutes talking about soil compaction and frost lines and the specific challenges of building on Iowa clay, and I watch it happen — the way Marcus draws Frank out, asks the right questions, treats the knowledge of a plumber with the same respect he'd give a professor. It's not performance. Marcus is genuinely interested. He's always been genuinely interested.

"Your boy here helped me replace a sewer main last summer," Frank says, gesturing at me with his fork. "Dug the trench himself."

"Nate dug a trench?"

"Eight feet of it."

Marcus looks at me with open delight. "Nathaniel Callahan dug a trench."

"Don't make it a thing."

"It's a thing. It's absolutely a thing. The kid who used to calculate the optimal path across campus to avoid walking on grass — dug a trench."

Sophie grins. "He complained the whole time."

"I didn't complain."

"You said, and I'm quoting: 'I went to college for this.'"

"That's not complaining. That's observation."

Diane laughs — quiet, watching everything. She likes Marcus. I can tell because she brought out the good napkins, the cloth ones she saves for holidays and people she's decided matter. She hasn't said much tonight, but she put her hand on Marcus's shoulder when she set his plate down, and that's Diane's vocabulary. Touch. Proximity. The language of a woman who came back from a very dark edge and learned to say things without words.

After dinner, Sophie corners Marcus in the kitchen while he's helping clear plates. I'm in the hallway and I hear her say, low: "Are you actually his friend, or are you one of those people who just shows up when things are going well?"

Marcus, to his credit, doesn't flinch. "I've known your brother since he was seventeen and angry about everything. I've seen him at his worst. I'm still here."

Sophie studies him. Whatever test she's running, he passes.

"Okay," she says. "You can stay."

---

The back deck after dark. Cedar Falls in July, which means the air is thick and the crickets are running a shift rotation. The deck boards are pressure-treated pine, screwed down with stainless steel — Frank and I built this in the summer after I arrived, my first real project, and the wood has aged into a silver-gray that looks deliberate even though it's just weather.

Marcus stretches his legs out, a beer in his hand. I've got water. He noticed an hour ago but didn't say anything. Now, settling in, he looks at the glass.

"You stopped drinking."

"Yeah."

"Good."

He takes a pull from his beer. I drink my water. The crickets fill the silence and neither of us rushes to break it.

This is the thing about Marcus — the thing that six years has built. We don't need to narrate. We've passed the point where absence requires explanation or silence requires filling. He drove three hours to sit on a deck and drink a beer next to me, and that's enough. That's the whole thing.

"Priya wants to meet you," he says after a while.

"Bring her next time."

"She's convinced you're fictional. She says no one is as loyal as I describe you."

"You describe me as loyal?"

"I describe you as a pain in the ass who happens to also be loyal. There's a distinction." He picks at the label on his beer. "She's good, man. She's a mechanical engineer, which means she's smarter than me, which means I'm terrified all the time. But she's good."

"I'm glad."

"I want you to be there. When I propose."

I look at him. "You're proposing?"

"Not yet. Maybe next year. But when I do — I want you there. You'll be my best man, right? I mean, that's not even a question, that's a statement."

Something catches in my chest. Best man. The kind of thing people ask when they've decided you're permanent. "Yeah. Of course."

"Good." He grins. "You'll have to give a speech."

"I'll manage."

"A good speech. Not that thing you do where you're technically eloquent but emotionally evasive."

"I don't do that."

"Nate. You absolutely do that."

He's right. He's always right about me, which is both the comfort and the cost of being known.

We sit for a while longer. A car passes on the street. Frank's left the kitchen light on — the window throws a yellow rectangle across the lawn, and I can see Diane's shadow moving behind the glass, cleaning up, her movements steady and practiced. The Callahan house at night. The most ordinary thing in the world, and I have never stopped being grateful for it.

"You're different, man," Marcus says.

I wait.

"Focused. Reliable. Whatever you're building with NEORAN — it's real. I can see it." He pauses. The Casio glints on his wrist. "But there's something you're carrying. Something underneath."

The air doesn't change. The crickets don't stop. But the moment shifts, the way a load redistributes when you add weight to a beam — invisible unless you know what to look for.

"I don't need to know what it is," Marcus says. "You don't have to tell me. But I see it. I've seen it for a while."

Six years. He's been watching for six years, patient as bedrock, and now he's naming it. Not demanding. Not prying. Just laying the observation on the table between us and letting it sit there.

"When I can tell you," I say, "I will."

He nods. Files it. The promise is logged in whatever system Marcus uses to keep track of the people he loves — and he does love me, in the way men who grew up without easy affection learn to love: through presence, through return, through the accumulated evidence of years.

"Okay," he says.

He finishes his beer. I finish my water. We go inside.

---

Three months later, different room, different frequency entirely.

The Cedar Falls Community Development Board meets in the basement of the public library, which someone has tried to make professional with folding tables and a projector screen that keeps curling at the bottom corner. I'm here representing NEORAN — we've been tracking housing access data for the county, and tonight's agenda includes a proposal for a new residential development on the east side.

I know the developer's name before he walks in. Derek Holt. I've been reading the proposal documents for a week.

He enters the room and it recalibrates around him. He's wearing a charcoal suit that fits like it was cut for his body — which it probably was — and the Rolex catches the fluorescent light. His hair is shorter than I remember, sandy blond and styled with the kind of precision that's meant to look effortless. He's twenty-three and he moves like he's forty, like he's already arrived at some destination the rest of us are still traveling toward.

He scans the room, assessing sight lines and allegiances in one sweep, and when his eyes land on me, something sparks. Recognition. Not surprise — Derek doesn't get surprised — but interest. Genuine interest.

"Nate Callahan." He crosses the room and extends his hand. His grip is firm and calibrated. "I heard you were in Cedar Falls, but I assumed you'd be doing something boring."

"Community development nonprofits aren't boring?"

"They're usually boring. But you're here, so I'm reassessing."

The meeting unfolds. Derek presents his development plan — forty-eight single-family homes on a parcel east of the university. The renderings are polished, the financials are tight, and his delivery is flawless. He knows his audience: retirees, small business owners, a city planner who's been in the role since 1998. He speaks their language without condescending, references local concerns without pandering, and handles questions with the easy authority of someone who did his homework and enjoyed doing it.

He's good. That's the uncomfortable part. He's very good.

When it's my turn, I present NEORAN's analysis. The proposed development sits on a parcel that's currently zoned for mixed use, and the residential conversion would eliminate the possibility of affordable housing units that the east side actually needs. I have the data — income demographics, commute patterns, the gap between median rent and median wage. I present it clean. No rhetoric, just numbers.

Derek watches me present the way I watched him. Measuring. Cataloging. Filing away.

The board asks questions. Derek and I end up in a back-and-forth about density requirements and tax increment financing that goes four rounds deeper than anyone else in the room can follow. The city planner looks between us like he's watching tennis. We're both enjoying ourselves, and we both know the other one is enjoying it, and that shared awareness is its own kind of currency.

The board tables the decision for further review. Derek catches my eye as the room clears and tilts his head toward the door. An invitation.

---

There's a bar two blocks from the library — dark wood, brass fixtures, the kind of place that calls itself a pub and means it. Derek orders a bourbon neat. I order club soda.

He notices. He doesn't ask.

"Nonprofit housing advocacy," he says, settling into the booth. "That's your play?"

"It's not a play."

"Everything's a play, Callahan. The question is whether you know you're making one." He sips his bourbon. "You were good in there. The data was solid, the delivery was clean. You made me look like the guy who doesn't care about poor people, which is impressive since I actually do care about poor people. I just also care about making money."

"Those things are in tension more often than you'd think."

"They're always in tension. The question is whether you let the tension paralyze you or you use it." He leans back. Studies me. The blue eyes are the same ones I remember from Berkeley — sharp, assessing, constantly running calculations. "You went from burnout to nonprofit. That's a hell of a rebrand."

"It wasn't a rebrand."

"It was a pivot, then. A strategic repositioning. You left tech — which, for someone with your brain, was the obvious move — and you landed here. Cedar Falls, Iowa. Running a nonprofit that barely has funding. And yet you're the most interesting person in that room by a factor of ten. Why?"

"You're also in that room."

He grins. Takes the compliment like a man who's used to receiving them. "I am. Which makes it even more interesting. Two people from the same program, same cohort, same IQ bracket — and we ended up on opposite sides of a zoning dispute in Cedar Falls. The universe has a sense of humor."

I drink my club soda. Derek drinks his bourbon. The jukebox in the corner plays something by Springsteen — the quiet stuff, not the anthems.

"Tell me about the real estate," I say.

"Residential development. I've got three projects in the pipeline — Cedar Falls, Waterloo, and one in Des Moines that's going to make me a lot of money." He says it without apology, without performance. He's just stating facts. "My dad lost everything when I was eighteen. You remember."

"I remember."

"So I learned the lesson. You build assets. You build equity. You build things that can't be taken away by a market correction or a bad quarter."

"Your dad didn't lose everything because of a market correction."

"No. He lost everything because he trusted people and they screwed him. Which is a different lesson but the same result." He turns the bourbon glass in his fingers. The Rolex — his grandfather's, the only thing that survived the ruin — glints in the low light. "I build things, Nate. That's what I do. I look at a piece of land and I see what it could be, and then I make it that. You do the same thing, except your raw material is communities instead of real estate."

"Those aren't the same."

"They're more similar than you want them to be."

He's not wrong about that either, which is the problem with Derek. He's not wrong often enough to dismiss.

"What are you building?" I ask.

"Wealth." He takes a drink. "What else is there?"

"A lot of things."

"Name one that doesn't require wealth to sustain it."

I think about NEORAN. The grant applications. The conversations with foundations. The reality that every mission-driven organization runs on money, and the money has to come from somewhere, and the somewhere is usually people like Derek. He sees me thinking it.

"Exactly," he says.

"That doesn't make you right."

"It doesn't make me wrong, either."

We talk for another hour. I learn that Derek is meticulous about his projects — he knows every subcontractor, every permit timeline, every variance in the building code. He's not a speculator. He builds carefully and he builds well. I learn that he reads urban planning journals and follows housing policy debates, not because he cares about policy but because policy creates opportunity, and opportunity is his native language. I learn that he's lonely in a way he'll never say out loud — that the Rolex and the suit and the charcoal confidence are load-bearing walls, and behind them is a twenty-three-year-old who watched his father come apart and decided he'd never be that vulnerable.

None of which I'd say to him. Derek doesn't want to be understood. He wants to be respected.

"I heard you're dating someone," he says as we're closing out. "Elena something. Runs a water engineering firm?"

"How do you know that?"

"Cedar Falls is a small town and I pay attention. Is it serious?"

"Getting there."

"Good. You need someone who can keep up with you." He finishes his bourbon. Sets the glass down with precision. "Most people can't."

"Including you?"

He laughs. Real, genuine, surprised. "Including me. But I keep up differently. I keep up because I see the machine, same as you." He stands. Puts on his coat — dark wool, well-fitted. "You know what I like about you, Callahan?"

I wait.

"You're the only person I've met who's as strategic as me but pretends not to be."

The words land in the space between us and sit there. I want to deny it. I want to say he's wrong, that what I do isn't strategy, that the choices I've made are driven by conviction and not calculation. But the denial won't come. Because there's a version of what he said that's true — the version where every careful step I've taken, every relationship I've built, every skill I've developed has been in service of something planned. Something I haven't told anyone about.

Marcus sees the weight. Derek sees the architecture. They're both looking at the same thing from different angles.

"That's not a compliment," I say.

"It's an observation. Compliments are for people who need validation." He buttons his coat. "I'll see you at the next board meeting. Bring better data — you almost had me tonight."

"I did have you. The board tabled it."

"Tabling isn't winning. Tabling is overtime." He grins. "Goodnight, Callahan."

He walks out into the Cedar Falls night — October cold, early frost — and I sit in the booth with my empty club soda and the residue of a conversation that left marks.

---

I drive home. The house is dark except for the kitchen light, which Frank always leaves on. A habit from his years of early morning service calls — the light meant someone was awake, or at least that the house was expecting you back.

I sit in the driveway for a minute. The engine ticks as it cools.

Two men looked at me this year and told me what they saw. Marcus saw something carried. Derek saw something calculated. The difference between them is that Marcus said it because he cares about me, and Derek said it because he recognized himself.

Both of them are right.

I go inside. Lock the door. Check on Sophie — asleep, headphones still in, laptop open to a chemistry textbook. I close the laptop, remove the headphones, pull the blanket up. Fifteen years old and already smarter than most adults I've met. She doesn't need me to do any of this. I do it anyway.

Frank's boots are by the back door. Diane's reading glasses are on the kitchen counter, folded on top of a novel — she's been reading again, which is something she couldn't do a year ago, when the concentration wouldn't hold. The house holds its small evidence of the people living in it, and I move through these artifacts like a curator, noting each one, filing it under proof.

Proof of what?

That this is real. That the family I chose chose me back. That whatever Derek saw — the strategy, the calculation, the architecture beneath the surface — it doesn't negate what Marcus saw. The weight I carry isn't a performance. It's a cost. And the fact that I carry it strategically doesn't make it lighter.

Elena texts at eleven: *How was your week? I missed you.*

I type back: *Complicated. Two old friends. Very different conversations.*

*Tell me about them.*

I start to. I describe Marcus on the back deck, the comfortable silence, the beer and the water. I describe the board meeting, the bourbon, the verbal chess match with someone who reads the room the way I do. I don't name the thing that both of them noticed — the gap between what I show and what I carry — but Elena is sharp enough to read the outline of it in what I don't say.

*You're going to tell me eventually,* she writes. She's said this before. It's becoming a refrain.

*I know.*

*I'm patient. But I'm also paying attention.*

I set the phone down. Lie in the dark. The ceiling of my room in the Callahan house, the same ceiling I've stared at since I was eighteen, and I catalog the people who are waiting for me to be ready.

Marcus, who sees the weight and trusts me to carry it until I can set it down.

Elena, who sees the gap and trusts me to close it when I'm able.

Derek, who sees the strategy and doesn't care about the rest.

Frank. Diane. Sophie. Who see a son and brother and don't need to see anything else.

I'm twenty-three years old, and the countdown I started when I was seventeen is still running. The number changes every year — the deadline shifts as the plan sharpens — but the fundamental architecture remains. I'm building something. Multiple things. The nonprofit is real. The relationships are real. The person I've become in Cedar Falls is real.

But underneath all of it, structural and silent, is the thing I haven't said.

Derek is right. I am strategic. The question — the one that keeps me up at night, the one that Marcus almost asked and Elena keeps circling — is whether the strategy serves the people I love or only serves the plan.

I don't have the answer yet.

The kitchen light stays on. Frank's habit, my inheritance. A house that waits up for you, even when you're already home.

Chapter Fourteen: The Sealed Envelope

Chen doesn't bother with small talk anymore. That's one of the things I've come to respect about her — she treats pleasantries like unnecessary variables, noise in the signal. So when she calls me into her office on a Tuesday afternoon in July 2015 and closes the door, I know the conversation has already started.

"The 2011 data is clean," she says. She's standing behind her desk, not sitting. That's the tell. Chen sits when she's comfortable. She stands when she's building toward something. "I've run it through every filter I have. Independent verification from two colleagues who think I'm working on solar wind anomalies. The signal is real, the timing matches your prediction to within six hours, and I have no explanation."

"Okay."

"'Okay' is not a response, Nate. 'Okay' is what you say when someone tells you the coffee's ready." She pulls her glasses off and sets them on a stack of papers. Without them, her face looks younger and angrier. "You gave me a date and a frequency four years ago. You were a twenty-year-old kid who barely passed Physics 101. The signal arrived exactly when you said it would, on exactly the frequency you specified, and it is clearly artificial in origin. I need more than 'okay.'"

"What do you need?"

"An explanation."

"I've given you—"

"You've given me a story. A story about foreknowledge you can't source, predictions you can't explain, and a timeline you won't elaborate on. I'm a scientist. I don't work with stories. I work with testable hypotheses." She leans forward, palms flat on the desk. "Give me something I can test."

This is the moment I've been building toward for four years. The 2011 signal was proof of concept — proof that I wasn't delusional, that the information in my head corresponded to something real. But one data point isn't science. Chen knows that. I know that. One correct prediction could be coincidence, could be lucky guessing, could be — in Chen's more frustrated moments — an elaborate hoax she hasn't figured out yet.

She needs a second data point. And I have one.

"Sit down," I say.

"Don't tell me to—"

"Please."

Something in my voice gets through. She sits.

I pull a chair around to her side of the desk, which is a violation of the unspoken geography of her office, and she lets me do it, which tells me how seriously she's taking this. I grab a legal pad from her shelf and a pen from the cup next to her monitor.

"In approximately three years," I say, "between late 2017 and mid-2018, multiple objects will be detected entering the outer solar system. They won't be comets. They won't be asteroids. They'll be decelerating."

Chen doesn't move.

"They'll be coming from the direction of the Cygnus constellation, roughly." I start writing. The orbital parameters I remember aren't perfect — I'm working from a knowledge base that was never designed for astronomical precision, filtered through a brain that's better with people than with math. But the broad strokes are clear. I write down the approximate coordinates, the expected velocity range, the date window. I write down that there will be more than one object. I write down that initial observations will mistake them for trans-Neptunian objects until someone notices the deceleration profiles don't match any known orbital mechanics.

I fill two pages. My handwriting deteriorates toward the bottom of the second page because my hand is shaking, and I don't try to hide it.

When I finish, I set the pen down and push the legal pad toward her.

Chen reads it twice. She's slow, methodical, and I watch her eyes track the same lines three or four times the way they do when she's checking math in her head.

"Decelerating," she says.

"Yes."

"Objects — plural — entering from interstellar space. Decelerating."

"Yes."

"You understand what you're describing."

"I do."

She looks at me over the legal pad, and for the first time since I've known her, I see something in her expression that isn't skepticism or curiosity or professional detachment. It's fear. Not of me. Of the implications.

"If this is real," she says, and stops. She sets the legal pad down carefully, the way you'd set down something fragile. "If this is real, the implications are—"

She can't finish.

"I know," I say.

We sit there for a while. Her office has a window that faces the campus green, and through it I can hear someone playing frisbee and laughing about something. It's a beautiful day. It keeps being a beautiful day while we sit in that office with two pages of handwritten coordinates that describe the end of every assumption our species has ever held about being alone.

"I'm going to seal this," Chen says finally. She's reached the other side of whatever she was processing, and her voice has that particular flatness it gets when she's decided something. She takes the legal pad, folds the pages carefully, and puts them in a manila envelope. She writes the date on the outside. "Sign it."

I sign it.

"Witness," she says, and signs below my name. She opens the small safe under her desk — I didn't even know she had a safe — and locks the envelope inside. "This stays sealed until the prediction window opens. If objects matching this description appear, I'm in. Completely. No reservations, no hedging, no 'further study needed.' I commit everything I have."

"And if they don't appear?"

"Then we're done. I burn the 2011 data, I forget your name, and I go back to publishing papers about magnetospheric substorms that nobody reads." She almost smiles. "Those are the terms."

"Those are the terms," I agree.

We shake hands. It's absurdly formal given that we've just made a bet about the arrival of extraterrestrial objects in our solar system, and the absurdity of it cracks something loose in both of us. Chen lets out a breath that's half laugh, half something else.

"You're either going to be the most important person in human history," she says, "or the most committed con artist I've ever encountered."

"Can't I be both?"

"Get out of my office, Nate."

I get out of her office. I walk across the campus green, past the frisbee players, and sit on a bench near the library and put my head in my hands for about ten minutes. A girl walking by asks if I'm okay. I tell her I'm fine. I'm not fine, exactly, but I'm also not not-fine. I've just placed the biggest bet of my life on a memory that might be wrong, against a woman who will absolutely hold me to the consequences.

Anyway.

The thing about building something real is that it happens in the boring middle. NEORAN's first two years were scramble and prayer — me and Jenny in a borrowed office, writing grants at midnight, running programs on goodwill and caffeine. But somewhere in 2016 the scrambling stops and the machinery starts. Not all at once. There's no morning when I wake up and think, *we've made it.* It's more like — you're pushing a boulder uphill, and then one day you realize the ground leveled out a quarter mile back and the boulder's been rolling on its own.

By spring of 2016 we have ten employees. That number still amazes me sometimes. Ten people who get up in the morning and drive to an office and do work that I imagined in a spiral notebook when I was nineteen. Jenny runs operations with the specific, terrifying competence of someone who was born organized and has only gotten more so with practice. She hires a program coordinator named David who is better with spreadsheets than any human being should be. She hires a field outreach director named Marisol who is beloved by every school district in northeast Iowa within six weeks of starting.

I learn to trust them. This is harder than it sounds. My instinct — the old instinct, the one that comes from knowing too much and needing to control every variable — is to micromanage, to hover, to make sure every decision runs through me. But I watch Jenny handle a crisis with a donor who's threatening to pull funding over a misunderstanding about our curriculum, and she handles it better than I would have. Cleaner. Less ego. I watch David redesign our reporting system and it's genuinely good. I watch Marisol walk into a school board meeting in Waverly and walk out with a partnership agreement I'd been chasing for months.

So I do the thing that doesn't come naturally. I step back. I let them run. I focus on the parts only I can do — the vision, the fundraising, the occasional public talk where I stand in front of a room and explain why critical thinking matters in an age of information overload, which is a speech I believe in completely and which is also, in a way I can never explain to my audience, a speech about survival.

We open a satellite office in Waterloo. Then one in Dubuque. The federal grant comes through in August — $340,000 over three years, which is real money, institutional money, money that means someone in Washington read our outcomes data and decided we were worth betting on.

The point is, NEORAN becomes a thing that exists independently of me. It has employees and offices and a website that David redesigned twice and a logo that Jenny's cousin made and that I pretend to like. It has inertia. And that inertia means that even when I'm distracted, even when I'm up at two in the morning thinking about sealed envelopes and orbital parameters, the work continues. People are being helped. The mission is real. That part wasn't a lie, even if the reason behind it is something I can never fully explain.

Elena moves in on a Saturday in October 2016. She arrives with a rented trailer, four boxes of veterinary textbooks, and a carrier containing the angriest cat I've ever encountered.

"You said you didn't have a cat."

"I didn't. Marta found him behind the clinic last week. He needs a foster home."

"He's hissing at me."

"He hisses at everyone. That's his personality."

The cat's name is Gerald. He hisses at me for two weeks, then abruptly decides I'm acceptable and starts sleeping on my chest at night like a furry, judgmental paperweight. Elena treats this as evidence of my character. "Gerald doesn't like anyone," she says, scratching behind his ears while he purrs on my ribcage. "He likes you. That means something."

"It means I'm warm."

"Take the compliment, Nate."

Three weeks after Elena moves in, we adopt a dog. This was supposed to happen in an organized way — research breeds, visit shelters, have adult conversations about responsibility and vet bills. What actually happens is that Elena comes home from the teaching hospital with a mud-colored mutt she found limping in the parking lot, already cleaned up and splinted and looking at me with the specific expression of an animal who knows it's already won.

"His name is Hazard," Elena says.

"You already named him?"

"He walked through four lanes of traffic on University Avenue with a broken leg. He earned the name."

Hazard is not a well-behaved dog. He chews shoes. He barks at the mailman with a conviction that suggests he believes the mailman to be his mortal enemy. He once ate an entire stick of butter off the counter and showed no remorse. But he follows Elena from room to room like she invented gravity, and when I come home at night he does this full-body wiggle that starts at his nose and works all the way back to his tail, and I have never once in my life felt as thoroughly welcomed as I do by this sixty-pound mutt who loves me with his whole broken, healed-wrong, perfectly adequate body.

We settle into the apartment. It's a two-bedroom in a complex near campus — nothing special, beige carpet, a kitchen so small you can touch both walls if you spread your arms. Elena organizes the closets by season because she is fundamentally a person who believes in systems. I cook dinner three nights a week because I read somewhere that relationships need equitable distribution of domestic labor, and also because Elena once ate cereal for dinner four nights in a row during finals and I became genuinely concerned.

I am not a good cook. I make the same six meals in rotation: chicken stir-fry, pasta with whatever sauce is on sale, tacos, soup from a can that I add vegetables to so it counts as homemade, omelets, and something I call "rice bowl" that is rice with things on it. Elena eats all of it without complaint, which is either love or an extremely high tolerance for mediocrity.

We fight about dishes. This is the thing nobody tells you about living with another person — it's not the big stuff that tests you, it's the dishes. Specifically, my habit of leaving them "soaking" in the sink, which Elena correctly identifies as leaving them dirty in the sink with water over them and pretending that constitutes progress. We have the dish argument maybe once a month. It always ends the same way: I apologize, do the dishes, and then we order Thai food and sit on the couch with Hazard between us and Gerald on the back of the couch like a sentinel, and it's fine. It's better than fine.

She studies late. I mean late — two, three in the morning, her desk lamp the only light in the apartment, textbooks spread in a semicircle around her while she memorizes the arterial system of large breed dogs or whatever it is that vet students need to know at three a.m. I bring her coffee sometimes. She doesn't look up when I set it down, but her hand finds the mug without looking, which means she knows I'm there, which means something.

"You're going to tell me eventually," she says one night. We're in bed, lights out, Hazard snoring at our feet. She says it into the dark, not as a question.

"Tell you what?"

"Whatever it is you're carrying."

I don't say anything for a while. In the dark I can hear the apartment building settling, the soft tick of the baseboard heater, Gerald jumping onto the kitchen counter which he knows he's not supposed to do.

"Yeah," I say. "I will."

"Okay," she says, and rolls over and goes to sleep, and that's the whole conversation. No pressure, no ultimatum, no *you owe me the truth.* Just the quiet statement of a person who has decided to wait. Elena doesn't waste words. She also doesn't waste patience. When she tells me she'll wait, she means it, and the fact that she means it makes me want to tell her everything, which I can't, which sits in my chest like a stone I've learned to breathe around.

Sophie comes home for Thanksgiving 2017. She's twenty-one now, a junior, studying environmental science at Iowa State, and she's turned into someone I almost don't recognize from the furious sixteen-year-old who told me I didn't get to pretend we were family. She's still sharp — that hasn't changed and I hope it never does — but the sharpness has found a direction. She argues about policy now instead of about me.

We're in the kitchen, me washing dishes (I've gotten better about this, mostly), Sophie leaning against the counter eating raw carrots from the vegetable tray Diane made.

"You know what's weird?" she says.

"Many things."

"You. Still." She crunches a carrot. "You really did change. Like, fundamentally. I've taken enough psych classes to know that people don't do that. Not the way you did."

"People change all the time."

"No, they adjust. They modify. They put up new wallpaper over the same walls. You knocked the whole house down and built a different one." She's watching me with Frank's eyes — that same steady, assessing look. "I still don't understand how."

"I had good reasons."

"Yeah. You still won't tell me what they are."

"Nope."

She shakes her head, but she's almost smiling. "You're infuriating."

"It's part of my charm."

"You don't have charm. You have whatever the opposite of charm is, where people like you anyway despite your personality."

"That might actually be charm, Sophie."

She throws a carrot at me. I catch it, and we both laugh, and Diane calls from the living room that if we eat all the carrots before dinner she's disowning both of us.

Thanksgiving. The table.

Frank carved the turkey twenty minutes ago and has been standing over it ever since, adjusting slices, rearranging the platter, performing the specific male ritual of Guarding the Meat. He's sixty now, his hair more gray than brown, and he's wearing the flannel shirt he always wears for holidays, the one Diane bought him in 2009 that he'll wear until it disintegrates. He's got a dish towel over his shoulder. He looks settled. He looks like a man standing in the middle of a life he's decided to be proud of.

Diane runs the kitchen the way air traffic controllers run airports — calm voice, absolute authority, zero tolerance for deviation from the flight plan. She has assigned everyone a task. Marcus is mashing potatoes. Priya is assembling a salad she insisted on bringing because, as she put it, "someone at this table needs to eat a vegetable that isn't covered in butter." Elena is on gravy duty, which she accepted with the competence of a woman who can perform surgery on a horse but is mildly terrified of Diane's kitchen. Sophie is setting the table with the good plates, the ones Diane keeps on the high shelf, the ones that only come out when the table is full enough to deserve them.

I'm doing nothing useful. Diane has banned me from cooking after what she calls "the sweet potato incident of 2016," which involved a casserole dish, a malfunctioning oven, and a small amount of smoke damage that I maintain has been exaggerated in family retellings. So I stand in the doorway between the kitchen and the dining room, holding a beer I'm not really drinking, watching.

Marcus. He's twenty-six now, thick through the shoulders from the work he's doing at John Deere, and he's got his sleeves rolled up and he's mashing potatoes with more force than potatoes require. Priya stands next to him, close, the way people stand when proximity is habit. They're getting married in the spring. I'm the best man. When Marcus asked me, in Frank's garage over a beer, his voice cracked on the word and we both pretended it didn't.

Elena. She stirs gravy with the focus of someone defusing a bomb, and Diane touches her shoulder as she passes behind her, casual and warm, the kind of touch that means *you belong here.* Elena glances at me across the kitchen and raises an eyebrow that means *I'm doing fine, stop hovering,* and I raise my beer in response, and that's a whole conversation in two gestures.

Frank. He finally stops adjusting the turkey and carries the platter to the table with both hands, ceremonial, and sets it in the center with the satisfaction of a man who has fulfilled his sacred duty. He catches my eye and nods. Just that. A nod. But it carries the weight of seven years — of the fishing trip where he told me about the draft, of the deck we watched storms from, of every quiet conversation where he gave me the thing I didn't know I needed, which was a father who was there.

Sophie. She finishes the table and stands back to look at it, and there's something on her face I haven't seen before: contentment. Not happiness, exactly, but the specific peace of a person who has come home and found it the way she left it. She's wearing a sweater that's too big for her — one of Frank's, I think — and her hair is pulled back, and she looks young and old at the same time, the way twenty-one-year-olds do.

Diane. She stands at the head of the kitchen, her kingdom, and surveys the table through the doorway. The table with the good plates and the turkey and the sweet potatoes (made by someone other than me) and the salad Priya brought and the mashed potatoes Marcus is still mashing. She takes a breath and I watch her face do the thing it does when she's trying not to cry about something good.

"That's enough mashing, Marcus," she says. "Bring them in."

We sit. Frank at the head, Diane at the other end, the rest of us filling the sides. Hazard lies under the table, positioning himself for maximum dropped-food interception. Gerald sits on the buffet behind Diane's chair because he is a cat and therefore believes all surfaces belong to him.

Frank says grace. He's not a particularly religious man, but he says grace at Thanksgiving because his father did and his father's father did, and tradition is the connective tissue of this family. He keeps it short: thanks for the food, thanks for the people at the table, thanks for another year. Amen.

Then the noise starts. The beautiful, chaotic, overlapping noise of seven people passing dishes and talking over each other and reaching for the salt and arguing about politics and laughing at something Marcus said that I missed because I was listening to Elena tell Priya about a three-legged dog she treated last week. Sophie and Frank are debating something about football. Diane is telling Marcus to take more turkey, he's too thin, which is objectively incorrect but is the kind of thing mothers say because saying *eat more* is another way of saying *I love you* and *stay close.*

I eat. I laugh at the right moments. I pass the cranberry sauce. I put my hand on Elena's knee under the table and she puts her hand over mine without looking away from her conversation with Priya, and the gesture is so automatic, so habitual, that it almost undoes me.

Because here's the thing about this table. Here is the thing about every person sitting at it.

I have eight years left.

Eight years until the sky changes. Eight years until everything I've built, everything I've repaired, every relationship at this table, gets tested in ways none of them can imagine. Eight years until the Pathfinder objects — the ones I described to Chen on a legal pad in her office, the ones sealed in an envelope in her safe — arrive close enough for the world to notice, and after that, nothing is ever the same.

This is what it costs to know the future. Not the knowing itself — I've made peace with that, mostly, the way you make peace with a chronic illness. It's this. Sitting at a table full of people you love, in a room warm with food and noise and the particular amber light of Diane's dining room in November, and carrying the knowledge that all of it is temporary. Not in the abstract way that all things are temporary. In the specific, dated, counted way. I know the year. I know what's coming. I know that this table, this evening, this exact configuration of people and warmth and cranberry sauce, is a snapshot of the best it will ever be.

Frank refills my glass. "You're quiet tonight," he says.

"Just grateful," I say, and I mean it in every possible direction.

We lived three years in that apartment. The first was discovery — learning the geography of another person, the way Elena always sleeps on the left side, the way she hums when she's concentrating, the fact that she cannot function before coffee and should not be spoken to before her first cup. The second was routine — the dishes and the dog walks and the Sunday mornings when we stayed in bed until noon and ordered delivery and watched bad movies with Hazard wedged between us like a furry referee. The third was the one where I started to understand that this wasn't a phase of my life. This was my life. Elena wasn't someone I was dating while the real story happened elsewhere. She was the real story, and the countdown and the preparations and the sealed envelope in Chen's office safe — those were the elsewhere.

I don't know when I figured that out. There wasn't a moment. There was just a gradual reorientation, like a compass needle settling, and one day I looked at Elena asleep with her textbook open on her chest and Hazard curled against her legs and I thought: this. Whatever else I'm doing, whatever else I'm building, this is the part that matters.

New Year's Eve, 2017. Our roof.

It's not really our roof. It belongs to the apartment building and there's a sign on the access door that says AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, but the lock has been broken since before we moved in and half the building comes up here in summer. Tonight it's just us. Everyone else is at parties or downtown or inside where it's warm, because it's eleven degrees and the wind is coming off the plains with the particular hostility that Iowa wind has in December.

Elena is wearing my coat over her coat, which makes her look like a very small person buried in a very large amount of fabric. She's got a bottle of champagne she bought at Hy-Vee, the cheap kind with the foil that never peels off cleanly. We're sharing it, passing it back and forth because we forgot glasses.

Below us, Cedar Falls is doing its best impression of a city on New Year's Eve. There are fireworks from the park — not the professional kind, the kind that local service clubs put on, a little uneven, a little too long between bursts, but bright against the flat winter sky. Someone down the block is setting off bottle rockets. A car alarm goes off and stops. The sounds of a small city ringing in a new year.

Elena takes a drink of champagne and passes me the bottle. "What do you want this year?"

She asks this every New Year's. It's her thing — not resolutions, not goals, but *want.* What do you want. It's a better question than it seems, because it forces you to be honest about desire rather than discipline.

I look at her. Her nose is red from the cold. Her hair is escaping from her hat in the way it always does, because Elena's hair has never once in its life cooperated with a hat. She's looking at the fireworks, waiting for my answer, patient the way she always is with me, and I think about the sky above the fireworks. The dark between the stars. The vast, empty-looking distance between here and the outer solar system, where right now, right at this moment, objects are drifting inward. Decelerating. Coming.

Chen's envelope sits in a safe two miles from here. In a few months — maybe sooner, maybe later, but soon — someone with a telescope and enough curiosity will notice something that doesn't move the way it should. And then Chen will open the safe. And then the second data point will land, and everything accelerates.

"More time," I say.

Elena looks at me. She smiles — not her full smile, but the smaller one, the private one she saves for moments when she thinks I've said something that matters.

"Me too," she says. She takes the champagne back and drinks, and leans into me, and we watch the fireworks throw light across the snow-covered rooftops of our small, ordinary city.

More time. She thinks I mean us — more mornings, more arguments about dishes, more nights on this roof, more years of this life we're building in a two-bedroom apartment with a dog and a hostile cat and a closet organized by season. And I do mean that. I mean that completely.

I also mean: more time before the sky opens up and the world learns what I already know. More time before the countdown reaches a number small enough to taste. More time in this narrow, warm, impossible space between knowing what's coming and living as though it isn't.

A firework bursts — a big one, the finale, gold and white cascading down like something spilling — and in the temporary brightness I can see every rooftop, every snow-covered street, every lit window where someone is kissing someone or counting backward from ten or holding a glass up to a year they hope will be better.

2018 starts in the cold and the dark, the way years do.

I hold Elena closer. Hazard barks somewhere below us, muffled through the ceiling, upset about the fireworks or the noise or the particular injustice of being left inside while his people stand on a roof in the freezing cold.

More time. That's what I want. That's all I've ever wanted, since the day I woke up at fifteen with a head full of futures and a second chance I never asked for.

Eight years. That's what I have.

I drink the cheap champagne, and I watch the sky.
