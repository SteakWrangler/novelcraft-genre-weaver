Chapter Six: The Bookshelf and the Debt

The first board comes up with a groan that sounds almost human.

Frank works the pry bar under the decking plank and leans his weight into it, and the nails that have held this board down since 1994 give up one at a time — pop, pop, pop — rusted fasteners pulling free of joists that have gone soft with fifteen years of Iowa weather. The board splinters at one end. Frank tosses it onto the pile in the yard without comment.

"Next one," he says.

I get the flat bar under the adjacent plank and start working it loose. The sun is already brutal at nine in the morning, the kind of wet Midwestern heat that sits on your chest like a second shirt. My T-shirt is soaked through at the back and under the arms. Frank is wearing a long-sleeve work shirt buttoned to the wrists because that's what he wears, regardless of season or temperature or common sense.

We've been at this for three days. The back deck is twelve by sixteen, pressure-treated pine over a simple joist frame, and the surface boards have cupped and split and gone gray-green with age. Frank decided in May that it needed replacing. He priced out contractors, did the math, and told Diane he'd do it himself. What he meant was we'd do it together, but he didn't say that part out loud. He just told me to be up by seven on Monday.

So here I am. Up by seven on Monday, and Tuesday, and now Wednesday.

The demolition is the easy part. Ripping out the old deck is just leverage and stubbornness, and Frank has plenty of both. The hard part comes later — checking the ledger board, sistering any joists that have gone punky, laying new decking straight and true. Frank has a library book on deck construction that he's read twice. He has a torpedo level and a chalk line and a speed square that belonged to his father. He does not have experience building decks, but he has the conviction that a man should be able to build a deck, and for Frank Callahan that's close enough.

I already know how to build a deck. I worked a summer for Vogel Construction in a timeline that doesn't exist anymore — framing houses in Waterloo, learning to read a tape measure by getting yelled at every time I cut a board wrong. I can toe-nail a joist and set a post and run a string line in my sleep.

I don't tell Frank any of this.

He shows me how to hold the pry bar at the right angle to get maximum leverage without splitting the wood. I nod. He shows me how to pull nails with the claw hammer, rolling the head against the board to protect the surface. I nod and do it exactly the way he shows me. He tells me to measure twice and cut once, and I say "Got it" like I've never heard this before, like it's wisdom being passed from father to son instead of something every tenth-grader in shop class has on a poster above the band saw.

The restraint costs me nothing. That's what I've learned. Letting someone teach you something you already know — it's not dishonesty. It's just making room.

Frank doesn't talk much while we work. He gives instructions. He points. He makes sounds of approval or disapproval that are nearly identical in tone but distinguishable after eighteen years of exposure. We drink water from a plastic jug that Diane refills and sets on the porch steps. We eat lunch at noon — sandwiches, chips, whatever she's made — sitting on overturned five-gallon buckets in the shade of the garage. We go back to work. We stop at five when the heat has baked itself into the wood and every surface is too hot to touch without gloves.

It's the most time I've spent with my father in either lifetime.

---

By Thursday we've got the old decking stripped and stacked. The joist frame is exposed like ribs, and two of the joists have gone soft near the ledger board where water pooled and sat. Frank prods them with a screwdriver, frowning.

"These need to come out."

"Can we sister them? Run new lumber alongside and bolt through?"

He looks at me. It's a quick look, assessing. I've said something that suggests I know more than a kid who's never built anything.

"Where'd you learn that?" he asks.

"Library." Which is true in the broadest sense. I learned it in a book once, even if I learned it better with a nail gun and a hangover.

Frank considers this. Then he nods, once. "That'd work. We'll need carriage bolts. Half-inch, at least."

We drive to the hardware store in his truck, a 2004 F-150 with a cracked dash and a passenger seat that doesn't adjust anymore. The truck smells like pipe dope and old coffee. Frank drives with one hand on the wheel and the other resting on the window frame, and he doesn't turn on the radio.

At the hardware store he takes his time. He reads the labels on every box of bolts. He picks up lumber and sights down the edge to check for bow and twist, setting aside the ones that don't pass. He asks the kid in the orange apron a question about joist hangers and listens to the answer with the patience of a man who has all day, even though it's ninety-two degrees and we've been working since seven.

In the truck on the way home, stuck at the light on University Avenue, he says: "My old man could build anything."

I wait.

"Patrick Francis Callahan. Pipe fitter, Local 125. Hands like — I don't know. He could do things with his hands that didn't make sense. Fix a furnace, build a cabinet, wire a house. Never read a book about any of it. Just knew."

The light changes. Frank drives.

"He built the kitchen cabinets in the house on Tremont. The house I grew up in. Built them out of oak he got from a guy at the union hall who was tearing down a barn. They were beautiful. My mother said they were the nicest thing in the house, and they were."

I've heard Patrick Callahan's name before. Twice, maybe three times in eighteen years. Frank doesn't talk about his father the way some men talk about their fathers — with either reverence or resentment. He mentions him the way you'd mention a weather event. Something that happened. Something you lived through.

"He drank," Frank says. Not a question, not a confession. A fact, stated in the same tone he'd use to say the man was left-handed.

"Every night. Beer after work, then whiskey after dinner, then more whiskey after my mother went to bed. Functional. Went to work every day, never missed a shift, never got written up. Came home and built things and drank until he fell asleep in his chair."

He pulls into the driveway. Turns off the truck. Doesn't move to get out.

"Died at sixty-one. Liver. They opened him up and the surgeon told my mother there was nothing to do. Cirrhosis so advanced that his liver looked like a piece of — something. I don't remember what she said the surgeon said. Something ugly."

He's looking through the windshield at the garage door, which needs painting.

"I was twenty-three. Your mother and I had just started dating. I went to the funeral and I didn't cry and nobody expected me to because Callahan men don't do that. We just — " He stops. Flexes his hand on the steering wheel. "We just show up. That's what we do. We show up and we work and we don't talk about the rest."

I don't say anything. I know better. There's no response to this that doesn't make it smaller.

Frank opens his door. "Let's get this lumber out of the bed before it gets any hotter."

We carry the boards to the backyard in silence. We measure and cut the sister joists, drill the bolt holes, tighten everything down until the frame is solid. We work until five. We drink water. We don't talk about Patrick Callahan or liver failure or the things men pass to their sons without meaning to.

---

On Saturday I'm in the garage looking for the box of deck screws Frank bought at the hardware store. He said he put them on the shelf by the paint, which describes approximately forty percent of the garage's surface area because Frank's organizational system is based on the principle that he'll remember where he put something, which he never does.

I move a five-gallon bucket of exterior latex. Behind it, two cans of wood stain that have been here so long the labels have yellowed. Behind those, a string of Christmas lights wound around a cardboard tube. Behind that, shoved against the wall and half-hidden by a drop cloth, is a bookshelf.

Pine. Four shelves. About three feet wide, maybe four feet tall. The joints are — not perfect. The shelves sit in dados that were cut a little too deep on the left side, so the whole thing leans slightly, a degree or two off plumb. The surfaces have been sanded smooth by hand; I can see the scratch pattern where the sandpaper went against the grain in a few spots. There's no finish on the wood. It's bare pine, gone golden with age, dusty but not damaged.

I pull it out from the wall. It's lighter than it looks. One of the shelf supports on the back has come loose where a nail worked its way out. Easy fix. Ten minutes with a hammer and some wood glue.

I stand there holding this bookshelf in the garage with sawdust on my arms and sweat running down my neck and I know exactly what this is.

Frank built this when I was twelve. I know because I remember the summer he spent evenings in the garage — Diane told me he was working on something, and I didn't care because I was twelve and didn't care about anything that wasn't a book or a video game. He never gave it to me. I don't know why. Maybe he looked at it and saw the imperfections — the lean, the rough spots, the missing finish — and decided it wasn't good enough. Maybe he meant to give it to me and the moment never came. Maybe he put it in the garage meaning to sand it one more time, apply a coat of polyurethane, make it right, and then a week turned into a month turned into six years.

In the original timeline, this bookshelf stayed in this garage until — I don't know when. I never found it. I never looked.

I carry it inside.

Up the stairs. Through the hall. Into my room. I set it against the wall next to my desk, where the afternoon light hits it. The lean is barely visible when it's loaded. I take the books off my desk — the astrophysics texts I checked out from the library, the emergency management handbook, the dog-eared copy of *Cosmos* I've had since freshman year — and put them on the shelves. I add a few paperbacks from the box in my closet. A Stephen King. A Michael Crichton. The copy of *Hatchet* I must have read fifteen times in middle school.

The bookshelf holds them fine. The lean doesn't matter. The rough spots don't matter. It is a solid, functional piece of furniture built by a man who noticed his son was reading everything and decided to do something about it.

I go back to the garage and find the deck screws where Frank actually left them, on the workbench under a rag.

That evening, after dinner, Frank walks down the hallway toward the bathroom and passes my open door. I'm at my desk reading. He stops.

I don't look up right away. I let him see it — the bookshelf against the wall, the books on the shelves, the thing he built fitting into the room like it was always meant to be there.

"Looks good in there," he says.

Three words. He keeps walking. I hear the bathroom door close.

I wait until I can hear the TV downstairs, the muffled play-by-play of a Twins game. Then I put my face into the pillow and press hard and stay there for a while.

---

Mondays and Wednesdays I go to the library.

The Cedar Falls Public Library is air-conditioned, which in late July makes it the most civilized building in town. Gail Andersen works the reference desk. She's in her fifties, reads two novels a week, and has the particular gift of helping you find what you need without asking why you need it.

I've been coming here since January. First it was emergency management — FEMA publications, disaster response frameworks, the kind of reading that makes sense for a kid about to study the subject in college. Gail helped me navigate the interlibrary loan system to get textbooks that UNI didn't stock. She never asked why an eighteen-year-old was reading *Principles of Emergency Management and Homeland Security* for fun.

Now I'm reading astrophysics.

Not casually. I'm working through published papers with the slow, grinding effort of someone who has the curiosity but not the math. I took AP Physics and got a four on the exam, which means I understand force and motion and energy but I am functionally illiterate when it comes to the kind of mathematics that astrophysicists use the way plumbers use wrenches.

It doesn't matter. I'm not trying to become an astrophysicist. I'm trying to understand enough to be taken seriously by one.

Dr. Judith Chen. I found her name in February, searching the UNI faculty directory. Associate professor of physics. Her published work is mostly in radio astronomy — signal processing, noise reduction, the technical problem of distinguishing a meaningful signal from the background static of the universe. She's published in *The Astrophysical Journal* and *Icarus* and a handful of smaller publications. She co-authored a paper on anomalous signal detection that I've now read four times.

I don't understand all of it. I understand the abstract and the introduction and the discussion section. The methodology is dense with equations I can't follow. But the core argument is clear: Chen believes that current SETI protocols are too narrow, too focused on a specific type of signal, and that a genuine extraterrestrial transmission might look like noise under conventional detection frameworks.

This matters because what's coming in 2031 won't look like what anyone expects.

I have a notebook — spiral-bound, college-ruled, bought at Walmart for eighty-nine cents. In this notebook I am building a script. Not a screenplay, not a sales pitch. A script for a conversation. How do you walk into a professor's office and say something extraordinary without sounding like a lunatic? How do you establish credibility when you're eighteen and your evidence is impossible?

You don't lead with the impossible part. That's what I've figured out. You lead with the questions. You ask about her work. You demonstrate that you've read it, that you understand it, that you take it seriously. You build a relationship based on legitimate academic interest. And then, slowly, over months or years, you introduce the ideas that matter.

It's manipulation. I know that. I'm engineering a relationship with a specific outcome in mind. The fact that the outcome is saving the planet doesn't make the method clean.

I write in the notebook: *Approach window — September/October, first semester. Attend office hours. Ask about the anomalous signal paper. DO NOT mention predictions. DO NOT mention the countdown. Be a curious student, nothing more.*

I underline "nothing more" twice.

Gail comes by the table where I'm working, a stack of returned books in her arms. She glances at the papers spread out in front of me — printouts from JSTOR, a textbook chapter on radio frequency interference, my notebook.

"Branching out?" she says.

"Just interested."

"You know UNI has a physics department. Real professors. They'd probably love a student who reads papers for fun."

"That's the plan."

She smiles and moves on. Gail is good at this — offering exactly one sentence more than necessary, then letting it go.

---

The deck gets finished on a Tuesday.

It takes us two and a half weeks total. The new decking is pressure-treated southern yellow pine, screwed down with coated deck screws every sixteen inches. Frank insisted on a consistent screw pattern — two screws per joist, lined up across the width of the deck so they form clean parallel rows. It took twice as long as random placement. It looks right.

We stand in the backyard and look at it. It's not beautiful the way a contractor's deck would be beautiful. The cuts at the border aren't perfectly tight. One of the stair stringers is a quarter-inch off. But it's square and level and solid, and when Frank steps onto it and bounces on the balls of his feet, testing, it doesn't move.

"Not bad," he says.

Diane brings out lemonade. She stands on the new deck and puts her hand on the railing and says it's wonderful, and Frank says it just needed doing, and Diane catches my eye and smiles in a way that means she knows what this was.

Sophie comes out and looks at it. "It's a deck," she says, with the withering accuracy of a fourteen-year-old, and goes back inside.

---

The evening is warm enough to sit out. Diane and I are on the front porch. Frank is inside watching something. Sophie is in her room. The neighborhood is doing what neighborhoods do on summer evenings — sprinklers, the distant complaint of a lawn mower, kids on bikes at the end of the street.

Diane has a glass of iced tea. I have water. She's in the porch swing, moving it with one foot, and I'm on the steps.

"You're quiet tonight," she says.

"Just tired. The deck."

"You did good work on that. Your father — he won't say it, but he's proud."

I know he is. I know it in a way I didn't know it the first time through, because the first time through I wasn't paying attention. I was looking for grand gestures, for explicit declarations, for a father who would say the words. I missed the bookshelf. I missed all of it.

"Nate."

I look back at her. She's stopped swinging.

"I don't know what happened to you," she says. Carefully, like she's been thinking about how to say this. "But you're different. This last year, since — I don't know when exactly. Since last fall, maybe. You're different."

The light is going. Her face is half in shadow, half in the amber glow from the window behind her.

"Better," she says. "You're better. And I don't just mean your grades or the drinking or any of that. You're — kinder. You notice things. You helped your father build a deck and you didn't complain once, and the Nate from two years ago would have fought me for a week before he even picked up a hammer."

She takes a sip of her tea.

"I'm proud of you. I wanted to say that."

My mouth opens. I can feel it — the whole story, sitting right there, ready to spill. The countdown. The future. The version of me that wasted this, wasted her, wasted Frank, wasted everything. The weight of knowing what I know and carrying it alone.

I close my mouth.

If I told her, she'd believe me. That's the thing. Diane Callahan has a capacity for faith that borders on reckless. She'd listen to the whole story and she'd look at me and she'd decide that her son was telling the truth, because her son doesn't lie to her anymore, and then she'd carry this weight too. It would sit behind her eyes every time she looked at me. Every time she looked at the sky.

I won't do that to her.

"Thanks, Mom."

She reaches over and squeezes my arm. Then she picks up her iced tea and goes back to swinging, one foot pushing off, the rhythm steady and slow, and we sit there together as the streetlights come on.

---

Later. My room. The window is open and the air is thick and still, the kind of July air that doesn't move, that just lies on top of you. Cicadas outside, electric and relentless.

I'm lying on my bed looking at the bookshelf. Frank's bookshelf, with my books on it. *Cosmos* next to *The Stand* next to *Introduction to Emergency Management* next to a three-ring binder of printed astrophysics papers. The shelf leans just slightly to the left. In the dark I can barely see it, but I know it's there — the weight of books pulling it into its imperfection, settling it into place.

In the original version, I spent this summer getting drunk with Derek Holt. We drove to Waterloo and bought cheap beer with his older brother's ID and sat in parking lots and talked about nothing. I didn't build a deck. I didn't read astrophysics. I didn't sit on the porch with my mother and hear her say she was proud of me, because there was nothing to be proud of.

In that version, the shelf stayed in the garage. Year after year after year, behind the paint cans and the Christmas lights, while the pine went gray and the nails worked loose and whatever Frank was trying to say with it went unsaid and unreceived and eventually, I assume, rotted.

That's the thing about the people who love you quietly. You have to be paying attention. You have to know the language. And by the time most people learn it, the speakers are gone.

I reach over and turn off the lamp. The room goes dark. The cicadas keep going. Through the floor I can hear the murmur of the television and the low, settling sounds of a house with people in it — Frank locking the front door, Diane running water in the kitchen, Sophie's music bleeding through her bedroom wall.

Tomorrow I'll go to the library. I'll keep building the case, the careful architecture of questions and knowledge that will let me walk into Judith Chen's office in September and be taken seriously. I'll keep preparing for a conversation that has to go right because I don't know how many chances I get and I've already wasted more than I deserved.

Tomorrow I start building something else.

But tonight, the shelf holds.
