THE LONG COUNTDOWN

A Novel

---

Chapter One: The End of Everything

The alarm goes off at 6:15 because I never changed it. I haven't worked a morning shift at the copy shop in three weeks — Carl moved me to closing after the thing with the paper cutter, which was not as dramatic as he made it sound — but the alarm doesn't know that. The alarm is optimistic about me in a way that nothing else in this apartment is.

I slap at my phone until it stops. The screen says June 14, 2026, which means rent was due fourteen days ago, which means the envelope on the kitchen counter — the one I've been using as a coaster — is probably not a love letter. I know what it is. I've gotten them before. The trick is, if you don't open it, it's just an envelope. Schrödinger's eviction.

Anyway.

The hangover is a five, maybe a six. Not the kind that pins you to the mattress and makes you negotiate with God — those were Thursdays, back when I still went to O'Malley's — but a steady, workmanlike headache that says *you did this to yourself and you will do it again.* I count the Busch Light bottles on the coffee table without lifting my head. Four. No, five. The fifth is on the floor, tipped on its side, a small puddle of backwash darkening the carpet. My carpet is the color of nothing. It was beige once. Now it's the color of things that have given up.

I sit up. The apartment does what it always does: it sits there, being my apartment. Third-floor walkup on Sycamore Street, Waterloo, Iowa, which is exactly the kind of place you end up when you've made every correct decision in reverse order. Dishes in the sink — Wednesday's, maybe Tuesday's. A disconnected phone charger plugged into the wall, its cord hanging like a dead vine. The couch has a sag in the left cushion that fits my body exactly because I've spent more time on it than any human should spend on a piece of furniture that was already on the curb when I found it.

On the windowsill, there's a plant. Some kind of succulent. It was a gift from — okay, I don't remember her name. Laura? Lauren? She brought it over the second time she stayed the night, set it on the sill, said something about how taking care of a living thing might be good for me. She meant it as a gentle suggestion. I heard it as an indictment. The plant is mostly brown now, its leaves curling at the edges like old paper. I forget to water it. I don't forget, actually — I see it every day, I think *I should water that*, and then I don't. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole problem.

The point is, I'm thirty-five years old. I work at a FedEx Office making twelve dollars and sixty cents an hour. I have a bachelor's degree in English from the University of Northern Iowa that I finished in five and a half years and have used exactly never. I drink four to six beers a night, more on weekends, and I've gotten good enough at it that most people can't tell. My last relationship ended eleven months ago when Marcus left his key on the kitchen counter — not this kitchen counter, the one at his place, the nice apartment with the exposed brick that I was essentially squatting in — and said, "I can't keep doing this, Nate." He didn't say what *this* was. He didn't have to. *This* was me. *This* was all of it.

I haven't talked to my parents in four years. My father is dead, so he has an excuse.

I swing my legs off the couch — I stopped sleeping in the bed sometime in March, no reason, just entropy — and go to the kitchen. The coffee maker has a crack in the carafe that I've been meaning to replace. I fill it from the tap, spoon in the grounds, and stand there while it drips, watching the parking lot through the window above the sink. Mrs. Delgado is walking her dog, a fat beagle named Roscoe who always looks like he's one flight of stairs away from cardiac arrest. The sky is clear. It's going to be hot.

I drink the coffee. I take a piss. I consider showering and then don't. This is the routine. This is the shape of my days. I could narrate it with my eyes closed: coffee, phone, couch, hours of nothing, work at four, home by midnight, beer, couch, alarm at 6:15 that I won't need. Repeat. The Greek myths had a guy who pushed a boulder up a hill for eternity and they called it punishment. They could've just given him a studio apartment and a retail job. Same thing, less cardio.

My phone has three texts from last night. One from Carl, reminding me that I'm closing Tuesday and to please not be late again. One from a number I don't have saved that says "hey stranger :)" which could be anyone. One from Marcus, timestamp 11:47 PM: "Saw a thing that reminded me of you. Hope you're doing okay." I read it twice. I close the thread. I don't respond. This is what I do. Marcus texts every couple months — not enough to be desperate, just enough to keep the door cracked — and I don't respond, because responding would mean being a person who is part of someone's life, and I have proven, repeatedly and with great consistency, that I am not that.

I'm cataloging the specific failures — the Thanksgiving I showed up to my mother's house drunk and told her that her casserole tasted like cardboard, the morning I missed my father's chemo appointment because I was sleeping off a bender, the night Marcus asked me if I loved him and I said "I don't know" because the truth, which was *yes, more than I've loved anyone, and that's exactly why I'm going to ruin this* — when the light changes.

Not like a cloud passing over the sun. Not like that at all.

The light in the kitchen goes from morning-white to something else. Something that doesn't have a name. Gray-yellow, except that's not right either — it's the color of a bruise that hasn't committed to being a bruise yet, a sick non-color that sits on every surface and makes the coffee in my mug look like something drained from an engine block. I look out the window. The sky, which was clear two minutes ago, is the color of the inside of an old envelope. Not storm clouds. Not overcast. Just *wrong.* Like someone adjusted the white balance on the entire atmosphere and got it exactly, specifically incorrect.

"Huh," I say, to the apartment, which does not care.

On the windowsill, the plant does something I have never seen a plant do. Its leaves — the ones that were already half-dead, already curled — fold inward. Not slowly, the way plants usually move, in increments you can't detect with the naked eye. Fast. I can see it happening. The leaves fold in like fingers making a fist, and the green — what little green was left — drains out of them in real time, replaced by a gray-brown that looks less like death and more like something that was never alive to begin with.

I watch it for five seconds. Six. The plant goes still. It doesn't look dead. It looks *canceled.* Like the concept of that plant has been revoked.

"What the fuck," I say.

Outside, the grass in the apartment complex's courtyard is browning. Not in patches — uniformly, all at once, like someone poured bleach across the whole lawn. The elm tree by the parking lot is dropping leaves. Full, green leaves, falling in June like it's October, except they're not green anymore by the time they hit the ground. They're the same gray-brown as my plant. Mrs. Delgado has stopped walking. She's standing in the middle of the courtyard, looking up, and Roscoe is pulling at his leash, trying to go back inside.

My phone buzzes. Then buzzes again. Then doesn't stop buzzing.

I pick it up. Texts. Coming in fast. My mother — a number I still have memorized even though I deleted the contact three years ago, even though she might have changed it, I always kept the digits in some back room of my brain like a fire extinguisher behind glass — *Call me please.* Marcus: *Are you seeing this?* Carl: *Store's closing early, don't come in.* Numbers I don't recognize. People I haven't heard from in years — college acquaintances, a girl I dated sophomore year, someone who might be my cousin Derek. Everyone reaching out at once, which is something that only happens when someone dies or the world ends, and as far as I know no one has died.

The emergency broadcast hits every phone on the block at the same time. That tone — you know the one, the one they test on the first Wednesday of every month, the one you've heard so many times it's become background noise. Except it's not the first Wednesday. And the message doesn't say *this is a test.*

ATMOSPHERIC ANOMALY DETECTED. SHELTER IN PLACE. CLOSE ALL WINDOWS AND DOORS. AVOID PROLONGED EXPOSURE TO OUTDOOR AIR. THIS IS NOT A TEST. FURTHER INFORMATION TO FOLLOW.

I read it three times. *Atmospheric anomaly.* That's the language you use when you don't know what to call something, or when you do know and the real word would cause a panic. I look at the dead plant on my windowsill. I look at the trees shedding leaves in the courtyard. The sky is darker now, that gray-yellow deepening into something that looks like the sky before a tornado except it's everywhere, horizon to horizon, no rotation, no wind. The air is perfectly still.

I try to call my mother.

I punch in the number from memory — 319-555-0147 — and hit dial. It rings once. Then: *The number you have dialed is not in service. Please check the number and try again.* A robot's voice. Polite, indifferent.

She changed her number. Of course she changed her number. I called her seventeen times in one night, two Christmases ago, drunk enough to confuse honesty with cruelty, and said things I won't repeat because I don't need to — I remember every word, I always remember every word, that's the particular punishment of being smart enough to know exactly how much damage you're doing while you do it. She changed her number because of me. She changed her number the way you change a lock. I earned that.

I try again anyway. Same recording. Same robot. Same polite indifference.

On the TV — I turned it on without thinking, the way you do — CNN is cycling through aerial footage of cities. The trees are dying in New York. In London. In Tokyo. Everywhere. Every tree, every bush, every blade of grass, all at once. The anchors are trying to hold it together. One of them, a woman with blond hair whose name I should know, keeps touching her earpiece and pausing mid-sentence, listening to something off-camera, and each time she comes back her face is a little more wrong. Not scared, exactly. The look of someone processing information that doesn't fit into any category they have.

"We are receiving reports," she says, "of a widespread — what is being described as a cessation of photosynthetic activity across — across all observed plant species. Scientists at NOAA are currently—" She stops. Touches her earpiece. "I'm sorry, I'm being told we need to — we're going to take a brief—"

The feed cuts to a graphic. BREAKING NEWS. The graphic holds for ten seconds, twenty seconds, thirty. Then static.

I flip channels. Static. Static. A local station, still running, a man at a desk who looks like he hasn't slept, saying "remain indoors, authorities are assessing the situation, remain indoors." Then static.

My phone buzzes again. Marcus: *Nate, please respond. Please just tell me you're okay.*

I look at the message. I look at it for a long time. The cursor blinks in the text field. I could type *I'm here.* Two words. Two syllables. The bare minimum of human connection. I could do that.

I don't.

This is who I am. The world is ending and I still can't send the text. I still can't be the person who reaches back when someone reaches for him. I set the phone face-down on the coffee table and I stand at the window and I watch the sky, which is now the color of a thing I don't have a word for, and I think: *If this is it, then this is it. And I deserve to be alone for it because I chose to be alone for everything else.*

The point is, I'm not a good person. I'm not evil — I've never hit anyone who didn't swing first, never stolen anything that mattered, never set out to destroy anyone's life. I just... didn't show up. Didn't call back. Didn't try. My father died of pancreatic cancer on March 3, 2022, and I went to the funeral drunk enough that my mother could smell it from three feet away, and I sat in the pew and I cried, and she didn't touch me. She sat on the other side of the aisle with my aunt Linda and she didn't touch me, and I understood. I understood completely.

There was a woman in a coffee shop once. Two years ago, maybe three. Dark hair, reading a book I couldn't see the cover of, and she looked up and smiled at me. Not a polite smile — a real one. The kind that's an open door. And I didn't smile back. I looked away and I ordered my coffee and I left. Because I had already decided, somewhere in the machinery of whatever's broken in me, that I didn't deserve it. That the kindest thing I could do for a stranger was to not become someone they knew.

Anyway.

Outside, something changes. The gray-yellow sky pulses — not like lightning, not like anything I can compare it to. A brightening from everywhere at once, as if the atmosphere itself is being illuminated from within. The light comes through the window and it's white but not sunlight-white, not fluorescent-white. It's the white of a blank page. The white of a thing before it becomes something.

Pressure. In my skull, behind my eyes, in the spaces between my teeth. Not pain. Pressure. The feeling of being — I don't know how to say this. The feeling of being *read.* Like something is scanning me at a resolution below what I can perceive, going through me the way you go through a file cabinet, every drawer, every folder.

The bottles on the coffee table rattle. The carafe in the coffee maker cracks along its existing fault line and coffee spills across the counter. The light is brighter. The pressure is worse.

I—

The apartment is gone. No, that's not right. The apartment is still there but it's *thin*, like a photograph held up to a bright window, like I can see through the walls and the walls can see through me and we are both becoming transparent and—

Marcus's key on the counter. That's what I see. Not a memory — I'm *there*, standing in his kitchen, and the key is sitting on the granite countertop next to his wallet and his phone, and he's by the door with his jacket on and his eyes are red and he says *I can't keep doing this, Nate* and I say nothing because I have nothing to say because I did this, I did exactly this—

My father's casket. Oak. It looked cheap. I thought that at the funeral — *it looks cheap* — and hated myself for thinking it because what kind of person critiques the casket, what kind of person stands at his father's funeral and thinks about the furniture—

Thanksgiving. My mother's kitchen. The casserole on the table. My voice, too loud, too cruel, saying things I'd stored up for years like ammunition, and her face — not angry, not even hurt, just *tired*, the face of someone who has been waiting for this and is almost relieved that it's finally happening—

The woman in the coffee shop. Her smile. My turned back.

White.

Not white. The absence of everything that isn't white. No walls, no floor, no body, no—

I have no hands. I have no eyes. I am not seeing this, I am not experiencing this, I am

nothing

nothing

nothing

I'm cold.

That's the first thing. Cold, the specific kind of cold that comes from sleeping with the window open in a room that doesn't have good insulation, the kind of cold that lives in the tip of your nose and the edges of your ears. I pull the blanket up. The blanket is — it's soft. It's softer than any blanket I've owned in years. And it's plaid. Red and blue plaid.

I open my eyes.

White ceiling. Not the water-stained, slightly yellowed ceiling of my apartment. White. Clean. A ceiling fan with a pull chain, turning slowly. A light fixture shaped like a frosted dome that I—

I know that light fixture.

I sit up. The motion is wrong. My body moves too fast, like I've overestimated the effort required, like I'm lighter than I should be. My center of gravity is different. Higher. I'm—

The Dark Knight poster. On the wall. Directly across from the bed. Heath Ledger's Joker, head tilted, smeared makeup, the tagline *Why So Serious?* in dripping red letters. I got that poster at Suncoast at the mall the week after the movie came out. I was sixteen. My father drove me. He waited in the car and listened to AM radio and didn't complain when I took forty minutes because I also went to GameStop.

The desk. Under the window. A TI-84 graphing calculator sitting on top of an AP History textbook. A spiral notebook with a blue cover. A cup of pens. My father's old desk lamp, the one with the green glass shade, the one that he gave me when I started high school and said "every scholar needs a good light" and I rolled my eyes because I was fourteen and everything my father said was embarrassing.

I am in my childhood bedroom. In my parents' house. In Cedar Falls, Iowa.

For thirty seconds — I count them later in my memory, one Mississippi, two Mississippi — I believe I am dying. That this is the brain's last feature presentation, the farewell tour, the greatest hits of Nathan James Callahan. The sky went white and I died and this is the part where your neurons fire in some final desperate sequence and you see the places you were happy, or at least the places you were young, which is the closest I ever got.

But the cold is real. The blanket is real. When I grip it, the fabric bunches under my fingers and the sensation doesn't waver or smear the way dream-things do. The clock on the nightstand says 7:42 AM. It's a digital clock, red numbers, a Memorex alarm clock that I got for Christmas in seventh grade. It says the date: SEP 15, 2008.

September 2008. I was seventeen. The world hadn't ended yet, either version.

I get out of bed. My feet hit the carpet — actual carpet, not the dead beige of my apartment but the dark blue carpet my mother picked out when I was ten — and I stand. I am too short. I am looking at the room from the wrong height, two or three inches lower than I should be, and my body feels — I don't have the right word. *Elastic.* Like a rubber band that hasn't been stretched yet. No ache in my lower back. No stiffness in my knees. No hangover. For the first time in years, possibly the first time since this very room, I don't feel like I've been assembled from spare parts.

The closet door has a full-length mirror. I've been not-looking at it the way you don't-look at a car accident, keeping it in my peripheral vision, not ready. But I'm never going to be ready.

I turn.

Seventeen. Sandy hair that hasn't started thinning at the temples. Hazel eyes — my father's eyes — without the dark circles, without the permanent half-squint of someone who looks at screens and bottles with equal frequency. My face is — God, my face. It's smooth. Not in a vain way, not in a *look how handsome I was* way. In a *nothing has happened to this face yet* way. The scar through my left eyebrow isn't there. The bar fight that put it there is five years away. The nose that got broken and set slightly crooked in college is straight. Even the expression is different — I'm making a face I don't recognize, and I realize it's because I'm looking at someone who is scared, genuinely scared, without the layer of ironic distance I've been spackling over every emotion since I was twenty-two.

I touch my face. I put my fingers on my jaw and feel the skin and the bone underneath and I am shaking. My hands are shaking. These hands. These stupid, smooth, seventeen-year-old hands. They haven't left Marcus's texts unanswered. They haven't poured the drink that made me say those things at Thanksgiving. They haven't signed the check for half the rent I owed my last roommate before I ghosted. These are hands that haven't ruined anything yet.

I stand there. In the mirror, a seventeen-year-old boy with his father's eyes stares back at me. His chest is moving fast. He's breathing like he's been running, and his fingers are on his jaw, and behind him is a room full of objects from a life he hasn't lived yet, and he doesn't understand what has happened to him.

I don't understand what has happened to me.

The air smells like coffee. Not the burnt, slightly metallic coffee from my cracked carafe, but real coffee, percolated, the kind that fills a whole house. And under it, something else — bacon, maybe. Toast. The smell of a kitchen where someone is cooking breakfast because someone else is upstairs and will be coming down soon. The smell of being expected.

From downstairs: AM radio. I can hear it through the floor, tinny and distant, a voice I can't make out, probably NPR, probably the morning news. September 2008 — the financial crisis is weeks away. Obama and McCain are on every channel. The world is worried about things that seem, from where I'm standing, so small that I could hold them in my palm and close my fist.

Then a voice. From the bottom of the stairs. A voice I have heard in dreams and in the particular silence of 3 AM when you are drunk enough to stop lying to yourself.

"Breakfast is getting cold, bud."

Frank Callahan. My father. Who died in March of 2022 in a hospital bed in Waterloo, Iowa, with my mother holding his left hand and his sister holding his right, while I sat in the chair by the window and tried to hold it together and couldn't. Who was fifty-eight years old and had pancreatic cancer and went from diagnosis to death in four months. Who coached my Little League team even though he didn't know anything about baseball and we lost every game. Who drove me to the mall and waited in the car. Who gave me a desk lamp and called me a scholar.

Who is downstairs. Making breakfast. Calling me home.

My legs give. I sit on the edge of the bed. The plaid comforter bunches under me. The clock says 7:43. The poster watches me with smeared eyes.

I press my hands over my mouth, and I hold very still, and I do not make a sound. I sit there in my dead father's house, in my seventeen-year-old body, in a year that ended eighteen years ago, and I do not make a sound.

"Nate?" he calls again. A little louder. A little impatient. The voice of a man with somewhere to be, a man who is alive, a man who has no idea. "Come on, kid. Eggs are getting rubbery."

I stand up. I wipe my face with the back of my hand — these hands, these impossible hands — and I open the bedroom door, and the hallway smells like coffee and bacon and the particular wood-polish scent of my mother's house, and I go downstairs.

Chapter Two: The Man in the Boy

The coffee maker gurgles like it's drowning.

It's a Mr. Coffee, the twelve-cup model with the cracked carafe handle that Diane has been meaning to replace since I was in middle school. She never will. It'll sit on this counter through two more presidential administrations, one kitchen renovation that stops at the cabinets, and a brief experiment with a Keurig that Frank will declare "a scam" after six days. The Mr. Coffee will outlast the marriage's worst year, Sophie's braces, and the family dog they haven't adopted yet. It will not outlast Frank.

But right now it's just a coffee maker, doing what coffee makers do, and the sound of it is so ordinary that I have to grip the doorframe to stay upright.

The kitchen is a crime scene of normalcy. Frank sits at the table with the Waterloo-Cedar Falls Courier spread in front of him, reading glasses low on his nose, spooning Cheerios with the mechanical rhythm of a man who has eaten the same breakfast for thirty years. He's wearing a gray Carhartt t-shirt, the one with the small hole near the hem that Diane keeps threatening to throw away. His hair is reddish-brown and thinning at the crown in a way I know embarrasses him, though he'd eat glass before admitting it. He's fifty-four years old and he's alive and I can smell Barbasol and sawdust from six feet away and the floor is tilting.

"There he is," Frank says without looking up. "Thought you were going to sleep through Tuesday."

Diane is at the counter, spreading peanut butter on white bread with the focus of a surgeon. She's in a green cardigan with pockets that I know contain at least two pens, a grocery list, and a coupon for something she'll forget to buy. Her reading glasses are on top of her head. She'll spend twenty minutes looking for them after she drops Sophie off.

"Do you want toast, honey? There's toast."

I open my mouth. Nothing comes out.

It's September 2008. The kitchen radio — the actual radio, the one that sits on the windowsill above the sink — is tuned to AM 1650, and some guy is talking about the Hawkeyes' secondary. Diane's lunch-packing station occupies the entire left counter: bread, turkey, those little bags of baby carrots she buys in bulk because she read an article about school nutrition. Sophie's lunchbox is purple. It has a scratch on the front from when she dropped it on the first day of seventh grade and cried in the car, then denied crying, then didn't speak for two hours.

I know this because I was there. I was seventeen and annoyed about being late for something I can't remember anymore, and I told her to stop being a baby, and she looked at me with an expression I would not understand for another decade.

"Nate?" Diane turns, butter knife in hand. "You okay?"

"Yeah." My voice cracks. Not from emotion — from the genuine physiological reality of being seventeen, of having a larynx that hasn't finished settling into itself. I'd forgotten about the voice cracks. I'd forgotten about a lot of things. "Yeah, sorry. Morning."

Frank turns a page. The paper rustles. It's the most important sound I've ever heard.

"Juice is in the fridge," he says. "Your mother bought that pomegranate stuff again."

"It's full of antioxidants, Frank."

"It's full of something."

I walk to the fridge. My body moves differently than I remember — there's a looseness to it, a spring-loaded quality, like I'm piloting a sports car after years in a sedan with a check-engine light. I'm taller than I expected and shorter than I should be. My hand closes around the juice carton and I stand there with the refrigerator door open, staring at the contents: Diane's yogurts, Frank's Miller Lites lined up on the bottom shelf, a Tupperware of something from Sunday, Sophie's string cheese.

The cold air hits my face and I think: *In eighteen years, every plant in the yard will die in real time. The sky will go the color of a bruise. Frank will already be fourteen years dead and I will be standing in an apartment in Waterloo, alone, watching the emergency broadcast system tell me nothing useful.*

"You're letting the cold out, bud."

I close the fridge. I pour pomegranate juice into a glass and sit at the table across from my father. He smells like Monday morning. He smells like the first half of my life.

"You look tired," Diane says. She comes up behind me, puts her hand on my shoulder. Her fingers are cold from the lunch meat. "Did you sleep okay?"

"Fine," I say. "Weird dreams."

This is technically true in the way that being hit by a freight train is technically an interruption to your commute.

Frank folds the sports section and sets it aside. He looks at me over his reading glasses, and there it is — that expression I spent years misreading as disinterest. It's not disinterest. It's the face of a man who was raised to believe that concern is best expressed through proximity, not conversation. Frank Callahan has never in his life asked "How are you feeling?" He has, roughly ten thousand times, simply been in the room.

"Big week?" he asks.

"What?"

"School. Big week coming up?"

I have absolutely no idea what's happening at Cedar Falls High School this week. I have a vague, waterlogged memory of September 2008 being early in the school year — first or second week, probably. AP History with Mr. Danaher. Precalculus. The rest is fog.

"Just the usual," I say.

Frank nods. This is a complete conversation by Frank Callahan standards.

Sophie appears in the doorway like she was conjured. She's all elbows and knees and a ponytail pulled so tight it looks structural. Her braces catch the overhead light when she yawns. She's wearing an oversized hoodie — mine, I realize, one I thought I'd lost — and she has the string in her mouth already, chewing with the dedication of someone training for a competition.

"Morning, sweetheart," Diane says. "Your lunch is almost ready. Do you want carrots or cucumber?"

"Carrots." Sophie slides into her chair and pours Cheerios without looking at the bowl. Half of them make it in. She doesn't acknowledge this. "Can I have five dollars?"

"For what?"

"Existing."

Frank snorts. It's almost a laugh. For Frank, it is a laugh.

I watch my thirteen-year-old sister eat cereal, and what I feel is so far beyond anything I have vocabulary for that I just sit there, holding my juice, breathing. She's thirteen. She hasn't decided yet that I'm a disappointment. She hasn't written me off as the brother who left and didn't call. Right now I'm just her annoying older brother who steals the bathroom and never helps with anything, and that's — 

I stand up. "Bathroom," I say, and I'm out of the kitchen before anyone can respond.

I make it to the downstairs half-bath, the one with the seashell soap dish Diane bought at a craft fair in 2004, and I close the door and sit on the lid of the toilet and put my face in my hands and I do not cry. I want to. Every cell in my body wants to. But if I start, I'm not sure the sound that comes out will be something I can explain, so I just sit there and breathe and press my palms against my eyes until I see colors.

Two minutes. Maybe three. Then I flush the toilet for cover, run the water, and go back to the kitchen.

"Wash your hands?" Diane asks.

"Yes, Mom."

She nods, satisfied. Sophie is looking at me. She has an expression I can't read, or maybe I can read it and I just don't want to. She goes back to her Cheerios.

Frank drives me to school. This was the routine, I remember now — he'd drop me on his way to the job site, and I'd complain about the country station he played too loud, and we'd sit in comfortable silence for the eight-minute drive, and I had no idea any of it mattered.

The truck is a 2004 Ford F-150, white, with a toolbox bolted to the bed and a pine air freshener that stopped working in 2006 but still hangs from the mirror like a talisman. The bench seat has a permanent indent on the driver's side. There's a coffee thermos in the cupholder, stainless steel, dented.

Frank drives with one hand on the wheel at twelve o'clock. He hums along with something on the radio. I watch the streets of Cedar Falls scroll past — the Hy-Vee, the Casey's, the houses with their September lawns still green, the trees that haven't started turning yet — and I catalog every detail like a man memorizing a painting before the museum burns.

"Supposed to rain later this week," Frank says.

"Yeah?"

"Thursday maybe. Might push the Millard job."

I have no idea what the Millard job is. "That sucks," I say, which is apparently the right response, because Frank nods and takes a drink of his coffee.

He pulls up to the school entrance. Cedar Falls High. The building looks exactly the same and also like a place I invented. There are kids everywhere — on the steps, on the lawn, leaning against cars in the lot — and they are so young. They are preposterously, offensively young. They're wearing low-rise jeans and Hollister t-shirts and carrying backpacks that look too big for their bodies, and they're laughing about things that are important and stupid and important because they're stupid, and I want to stand on the hood of the truck and scream at them to pay attention because they will never be this free again.

I don't do that.

"Have a good one, bud," Frank says.

"Thanks, Dad."

Something in my voice must land differently than I intend, because Frank looks at me — really looks, the way he does maybe four times a year — and there's a beat of something between us. Then he nods.

"Go on. You'll be late."

I go.

AP History with Mr. Danaher is third period, and it's the first time in eighteen years I've had to sit in a desk with an attached writing surface designed for someone with the wingspan of a heron.

Danaher is talking about the 2008 presidential election. He's drawing a map on the whiteboard — red states, blue states, the whole electoral math. He's a good teacher, I remember that now. Mid-forties, beard, the kind of guy who wears ties with historical figures on them and genuinely thinks it's charming. He's asking the class to predict outcomes. Who's going to win? What states are in play?

This is, for me, like being asked to predict whether the sun will come up.

I keep my mouth shut for the first twenty minutes. I sit in the back row and I watch my classmates — seventeen-year-olds who will vote for the first time next month, who think this election is the most consequential thing that's ever happened, who have no idea that they're right but not for the reasons they think — and I practice being invisible.

It doesn't work.

"Nate. You're awfully quiet back there." Danaher's looking at me with that teacher expression, the one that means *I know you know something.* "What's your read? Virginia — red or blue this cycle?"

Virginia. I know the answer. I know the exact margin. I know the county-level breakdown. I know that northern Virginia's demographic shift is going to be the story of the night, that Mark Warner is going to win the Senate race by thirty points, that this is the election that turns Virginia from a swing state into something else entirely.

"Blue," I say. "Northern Virginia suburbs. Demographic shift. Loudon and Fairfax counties are going to run up the score."

The room is quiet.

"That's..." Danaher tilts his head. "That's a very specific analysis."

"I read a lot."

"What are you reading? Because I want to assign it." He's half-smiling, genuinely intrigued. "Most of your classmates think Virginia is safe Republican territory."

*Shut up,* says the voice in my head. *Shut up shut up shut up.*

"I think the polling data is there if you look at off-year trends," I say, because apparently I am constitutionally incapable of shutting up. "The 2006 midterms already showed the movement. Webb beat Allen. You just have to extrapolate."

Danaher stares at me. Two rows up, a girl named Rachel Kovacs turns around and mouths *what the hell?*

"Well," Danaher says. "I think you might have a future in campaign strategy, Nate."

I spend the rest of the period staring at my notebook and writing *STOP TALKING* in the margin, over and over, like a kid in detention from a horror movie.

---

Lunch is in the cafeteria, which smells exactly the way school cafeterias have always smelled and will always smell — a proprietary blend of industrial cleaner, pizza grease, and the particular funk of three hundred teenagers in a room with inadequate ventilation.

Marcus is already at our table. Marcus Wade — six-three, dark brown skin, gap-toothed grin, wearing Nike basketball shoes that are too clean for someone who actually plays basketball in them. His watch is a silver Casio, and it is, as always, three minutes fast, a fact he has never acknowledged despite years of evidence.

"Yo." He slides over to make room. "Heard you went full pundit in Danaher's class."

Word travels fast in a building this small. "I just answered a question."

"Rachel said you sounded like you were running for office."

"Rachel exaggerates."

"Rachel said you predicted Virginia going blue and cited specific counties."

"I was making conversation."

Marcus looks at me. He has this way of looking at people — direct, open, completely without guile — that made him the best friend I ever had and the easiest person I ever took for granted. In the original timeline, I borrowed three thousand dollars from Marcus Wade in 2014 to cover rent and never paid it back. I told myself I'd get to it. I told myself he understood. I told myself a lot of things. He stopped calling. I let him.

"You good?" he asks. "You seem kind of... off."

"I'm fine. Weird morning."

"Yeah, I feel that." He opens his lunch — turkey sandwich, bag of Doritos, apple he won't eat. "My mom's on this thing where she makes me bring fruit. Like I'm going to eat an apple when there's a vending machine right there."

Jake Petersen and Cody Voss show up with their trays. Jake's talking about a girl. Cody's talking about Call of Duty. They're both talking at the same time, which is a communication strategy that only works when you're seventeen and everything you're saying is essentially the same temperature of important. I eat my sandwich and I watch them, and what I see — what I can't stop seeing — is children.

Not in a condescending way. In a heartbroken way. These are kids who think the worst thing that can happen is a bad grade or a breakup, and they're right, that is the worst thing, and they should protect that ignorance with everything they have because the world is going to take it from them one piece at a time until they're sitting in an apartment in Waterloo at thirty-five, watching the sky change color and thinking, *I had everything and I spent it all on being angry.*

Marcus is telling a story about his engineering teacher, Mr. Brekke, who apparently spent an entire class period arguing with a student about whether a bridge could be built out of Popsicle sticks. Marcus is animated, funny, doing voices. The table is laughing. I'm laughing too, because Marcus is genuinely funny and because laughter is a good place to hide.

"You should do engineering club," I say. "You'd be good at it."

Marcus shrugs. "Maybe. My dad wants me to do something practical. Like business."

In the original timeline, Marcus went to Iowa State for mechanical engineering, graduated with honors, and works at John Deere now. Or worked. Will work. The tenses of my life no longer function.

"Engineering is practical," I say. "Extremely practical. Do the engineering thing."

He looks at me funny. "Okay. Thanks, career counselor."

I eat my apple. I eat Marcus's apple too, because he's not going to, and because I'm seventeen and my metabolism is an open furnace and I'm hungry in a way I haven't been in a decade.

---

Sophie is in the living room when I get home, spread across the couch with a geometry textbook open on her stomach and an expression that suggests the textbook has personally wronged her. She's chewing a hoodie string. Her ponytail has migrated to the side of her head in a way that would be concerning on an adult but reads as aerodynamic on a thirteen-year-old.

I should go upstairs. I should go to my room and close the door and try to figure out the rules of whatever this is — time travel, psychotic break, afterlife, simulation. I should not interact with my sister more than necessary until I understand what's happening.

"That's an isosceles triangle," I say, looking over her shoulder. "The two equal sides mean the base angles are equal. So if you know one base angle is fifty-two degrees, the other one is too, and the vertex is whatever's left over from one-eighty."

Sophie looks up at me. Her expression cycles through several phases: confusion, suspicion, deeper confusion.

"I didn't ask you."

"I know."

"Since when do you know geometry?"

"I've always known geometry."

"You got a C in geometry."

This is true. Seventeen-year-old Nate Callahan got a C in geometry because seventeen-year-old Nate Callahan did not do homework and studied exclusively during the passing period before tests. Thirty-five-year-old Nate Callahan has used geometry exactly four times in his adult life and remembers it only because competence compounds in weird ways over two decades.

"The C was a lifestyle choice," I say. "The vertex angle is seventy-six degrees."

Sophie stares at me for a long time. She has Diane's eyes — dark, quick, missing nothing. Then she writes down seventy-six degrees and says, without looking up, "Okay, weirdo."

I go upstairs. I close my door. I sit on the bed and I look at the Dark Knight poster and I think about how Heath Ledger has been dead for eight months and how in eighteen years the world will end and how my sister just accepted geometry help from me without the universe collapsing, and I press my hands flat against my thighs and I breathe.

---

The panic attack comes on Thursday.

I've held it together for three days. Three days of smiling at the right times and saying the right things and not screaming in the hallway when I see a poster for the Homecoming dance, because I know the DJ will play "Low" by Flo Rida at least six times and Amanda Perkins will twist her ankle during the Cupid Shuffle and the whole thing will be exactly as wonderful and idiotic as being seventeen requires.

Three days of knowing that Lehman Brothers is going to collapse in nine days. That the stock market is about to crater. That Frank has a 401(k) that will lose a third of its value by Christmas and he'll sit at the kitchen table staring at the statement without saying anything, and Diane will find him there at midnight and neither of them will talk about it.

Three days of knowing that the sky will turn yellow-gray in 2026 and every plant in the yard will die and something called the Kessari will arrive and the emergency broadcasts will cut out one by one like candles in a wind.

It hits in the bathroom between fifth and sixth period. I'm washing my hands and I look up at the mirror and I see a seventeen-year-old kid looking back at me and the kid has my eyes but they're the wrong age, there's something behind them that doesn't fit, and the room starts to breathe.

Not spin. Breathe. The walls expand and contract, the fluorescent light buzzes at a frequency I can feel in my teeth, and my chest locks up like someone has reached inside and grabbed my lungs and squeezed.

I make it to a stall. I close the door. I sit on the floor, which is tile, which is cold, which is disgusting — I'm sitting on a high school bathroom floor and I'm thirty-five years old and I can't breathe. My hands are shaking. Not trembling, shaking, the kind of shaking that starts in your fingers and moves up your arms and into your shoulders until your whole body is vibrating at a frequency that doesn't have a name.

I put my head between my knees. I try to count. I try to breathe in for four and out for four and the air won't cooperate, it comes in shallow and fast and wet-sounding, and I can hear someone's shoes squeaking in the hallway outside and a locker slamming and laughter and the normal beautiful stupid sounds of a world that doesn't know it's ending.

Frank dies at sixty-eight. Heart attack. I find out two days late because we haven't spoken in four years because I said something at Thanksgiving that I can never take back. "Maybe you should've actually been there instead of just standing in the room." I said that. I said that to a man whose entire love language is standing in the room, and I knew exactly what I was doing, and I did it anyway.

The tile is cold against my palms. My breathing sounds like an engine failing.

I know who wins every election. I know which stocks to buy. I know that the iPhone is about to change everything and that social media is going to eat the country alive and that a pandemic is coming in 2020 that will kill over a million Americans. I know that Marcus will be fine. I know that Sophie will be fine. I know that Diane will sell the house after Frank dies and move to a condo in Waverly and learn to garden and never entirely recover.

I know everything and it is not a superpower. It is a weight. It is a sentence. It is sitting on a bathroom floor in a body that doesn't match, hyperventilating, knowing that the world is going to end and that the ending starts slow, so slow, with a fight at Thanksgiving and a phone call you don't make and a letter you don't answer, and then all at once with a gray-yellow sky and the smell of dying grass.

The bathroom door opens. Footsteps. Someone uses the urinal, washes their hands, leaves. I am invisible. I am a thirty-five-year-old man sitting on a high school bathroom floor with his head between his knees, and nobody knows, and the loneliness of that is so total that it's almost funny.

I breathe. Slowly. The count works on the ninth or tenth try. In for four. Out for four. The tile is cold. The light buzzes. My hands stop shaking.

I get up. I wash my face. I go to sixth period. Nobody noticed I was gone.

Friday evening. The porch.

Frank is in his chair — the Adirondack, the green one, paint peeling at the armrests. He's got a Miller Lite and the tail end of the sunset and he's not doing anything. Just sitting. Frank Callahan sits on this porch at least four evenings a week from May to October, and he doesn't read or listen to music or talk on the phone. He just sits. For years I thought this was boring. For years I thought a lot of stupid things.

I come out and sit in the other chair, the white one that wobbles because one leg is shorter. I don't say anything. I don't have anything to say. Or I have everything to say — eighteen years of everything — and none of it is speakable, so the result is the same.

Frank glances at me. He takes a drink of his beer. He looks back at the yard.

We sit.

The September air is warm enough that you don't need a jacket but cool enough that you notice it on your arms. The neighbor's sprinkler is going, that slow *tch-tch-tch-tch* sweep, and somewhere down the block a kid is bouncing a basketball, irregular, the sound of someone shooting alone. The light is doing that thing it does in Iowa in September, going gold and then amber and then a color that doesn't have a name, the color of the last hour of a day when nothing happened.

Frank doesn't ask me what's wrong. He doesn't ask me how my week was, or what I'm thinking about, or whether I want to talk. Frank Callahan does not do that. What Frank Callahan does is stay. He stays in the room. He stays on the porch. He stays in the truck for the eight-minute drive and he stays at the breakfast table and he stays married and he stays employed and he stays present in the only way he knows how, which is physically, consistently, without narration.

I used to think that wasn't enough.

Five minutes. The gold light moves across the yard. A car passes. The basketball sound stops and starts again.

Seven minutes. Frank finishes his beer, sets the bottle on the armrest. He doesn't get up to get another one.

Ten minutes. The light is amber now, almost gone. The sprinkler stops. The street goes quiet in that temporary way streets do, a held breath between one set of sounds and the next.

Frank stands up. He puts his hand on my shoulder — just for a second, just the weight of it, rough and warm — and then he picks up his bottle and goes inside. The screen door closes behind him with that spring-loaded slap that sounds like every summer night I've ever lived.

I sit on the porch. I don't move. The amber goes to gray and the streetlight on the corner comes on, the sodium-vapor kind that turns everything orange, and I think: *He stayed. He didn't know why I needed him to and he stayed.*

I think: *I have eighteen years. I have eighteen years to be worth the room he takes up in the world, and I will not waste this. I will not say the thing at Thanksgiving. I will not let the silence grow until it's structural. I will not find out two days late.*

The screen door opens again. I expect Frank, but it's Sophie. She comes out in her hoodie and her socks — no shoes, Diane would kill her — and she stands in the doorway with her arms crossed, looking at me with that expression she has. The one that's already too old for her face.

"Why are you being weird?" she asks.

I look at her. My thirteen-year-old sister, all elbows and braces and borrowed hoodies, who sees everything and says nothing until she says exactly the right thing.

I don't have an answer.

She didn't expect one.

She goes back inside, and I sit on the porch, and the streetlight buzzes, and the night comes in.

Chapter Three: The Friend He Didn't Deserve

The ball hits the rim and bounces off at an angle that physics shouldn't allow, and Marcus snags it out of the air with one hand like he's pulling a book off a shelf.

"That's four," he says.

"I'm aware."

"Just making sure. You seem confused out here."

The court behind Cedar Falls High is cracked asphalt with faded lines and a chain-link net that sounds like a zipper when the ball drops through clean. It's the second week of October, and the air has that particular Iowa bite — not cold yet, not really, but reminding you it could be. My lungs feel tight in a way that has nothing to do with fitness.

Here's what I didn't anticipate about being seventeen again: the body. Not in the abstract sense of youth or flexibility or whatever people romanticize. I mean the specific, practical reality that my legs are three inches shorter than they were six weeks ago, my center of gravity has shifted, and my hands — these smooth, uncalloused, weirdly soft hands — keep misjudging distances by a quarter inch. My brain says *go left, plant, elevate* and my body says *sure thing, boss* and then does something that looks like a marionette with tangled strings.

Marcus drives past me on the right side because I overcorrected left, lays it in off the backboard with that effortless wrist-flick he's had since we were fourteen, and doesn't even bother counting this one out loud.

"Five."

Okay, he bothers.

"My shoes are wrong," I say.

Marcus looks down at my feet. Old Nikes, the pair Diane bought at the start of the school year. "Your shoes."

"The traction's off."

"The traction." He tucks the ball under his arm. The silver Casio catches what's left of the afternoon light. "Nate, you're playing like you've never had knees before. What's going on?"

And that's Marcus. He doesn't dress it up, doesn't pad it with qualifiers. You're playing like you've never had knees before. A perfect diagnostic.

"I think I pulled something in gym on Wednesday," I say, because I've learned over the past three weeks that I need a library of small lies, preloaded and sorted by category. Physical discrepancies get filed under minor injury. Knowledge slips get filed under "read it somewhere." Emotional leakage gets filed under bad sleep.

Marcus bounces me the ball. "So quit standing at the three-point line trying to be Reggie Miller and take it inside."

I take it inside. He blocks me so thoroughly that the ball ends up on the grass twenty feet away, and a freshman walking past flinches.

"That's not inside," Marcus says. "That's you dribbling into my chest."

"I was establishing position."

"You were establishing that you're five-eleven."

He retrieves the ball from the grass. When he comes back, he's got that look — the one where his mouth is doing something complicated, fighting between genuine concern and the pleasure of roasting me. It's a look I forgot about. You don't remember the specific expressions. You think you do, but what you actually remember is a composite, a sketch. And then you see the real thing and it hits you that memory is just a bad photocopy of a bad photocopy, and the original was so much better.

We play to eleven. He wins eleven to six, which is generous, because at least two of my baskets were him letting up. I know because I watched his feet — he had the position to contest and chose not to, and Marcus Wade does not choose not to contest unless he's managing your feelings.

We sit on the low brick wall by the parking lot, passing a bottle of water back and forth. His Nike basketball shoes are scuffed to gray at the toes. There's a callus on his right index finger from the way he holds his pencil — too tight, always too tight, pressing the graphite like it owes him money.

"So I've been thinking about the engineering thing," he says.

"Yeah?"

"My mom thinks I should do electrical. Better money, she says. And my uncle does electrical in Waterloo, so I'd have the hookup."

"But?"

He pulls at the neck of his t-shirt, letting air in. "But I keep thinking about structural. Like, bridges specifically."

"Bridges."

"I know how it sounds."

"It sounds like you want to build bridges, Marcus. That's not exactly a controversial ambition."

He looks out across the parking lot, where a woman is loading groceries into a minivan at the Hy-Vee across the street. "It's more like — okay, you know the I-35W bridge? The one that collapsed in Minneapolis last year?"

"Yeah." Thirteen people dead. I remember it differently than he does — I remember reading about it at thirty, in a Wikipedia rabbit hole at two in the morning, clicking from bridge collapses to structural engineering failures to the Hyatt Regency walkway collapse to a forum post about how gusset plates work. I remember knowing it was exactly the kind of thing Marcus would have cared about. And then closing the laptop and not thinking about Marcus for another six months.

"Thirteen people," Marcus says. "And they figured out afterward it was this one design flaw — the gusset plates were too thin. Like, *someone did the math wrong*. Or someone did the math right and someone else said it costs too much. Anyway. I keep thinking about that. How a bridge is this promise, right? You drive onto it, and the promise is that the other side exists and you're going to get there. And when the bridge is good, nobody even thinks about it. It's invisible. It just connects things."

He says it plainly, without pretension, the way Marcus says everything. They connect things. Three words, and they land in me like a stone in still water.

I know what Marcus becomes. I know it because between the ages of twenty-five and thirty-two I looked him up approximately once a year, always from a new throwaway account, always late at night, always sober enough to be ashamed and not sober enough to stop. I watched his career through the tiny window of a LinkedIn profile: B.S. in Mechanical Engineering from Iowa State, then John Deere in Waterloo. Not bridges. Not structural. Mechanical. Gears and drivetrains and hydraulic systems for combines. Good work. Real work. But not the thing he's sitting here telling me about with that look on his face, the one where his eyes go slightly unfocused because he's seeing something that doesn't exist yet.

Did he change his mind, or did someone change it for him? Did the money argument win? Did his uncle's hookup in electrical pull him sideways, and then mechanical was the compromise, and by the time he graduated he'd forgotten he ever said the word *bridges* on a brick wall in October?

I don't know. I don't know because by the time Marcus was making those decisions, I'd already started the long process of becoming someone he couldn't trust.

"You should do structural," I say.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. You'd be good at it. You think about systems — how pieces hold each other up. That's the whole job."

Marcus looks at me. The gap between his front teeth makes every smile look a little conspiratorial, like you're both in on something. "Since when do you have opinions about engineering?"

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain bullshit, is what you contain."

I laugh, and it surprises me, because it's real. Not performed, not a deflection. Just a laugh, startled out of me by my best friend being exactly himself.

The point is — and here's where it gets complicated — I know what I owe him. Not the twelve hundred dollars, though yes, that too. What I owe him is the basic dignity of not being a project. Not being the friend I'm fixing. Marcus Wade is not my redemption arc. He's a seventeen-year-old kid who likes bridges and beats me at basketball and calls me on my bullshit, and the thing I owe him is to sit here on this wall and be an actual person.

I'm getting better at that. Some days.

---

The problem is that guilt makes you generous, and generosity without honesty is just manipulation wearing a nicer shirt.

It starts small. Tuesday, I grab an extra slice of pizza at lunch and slide it onto Marcus's tray. Wednesday, I cover his Gatorade at the vending machine. Thursday, I offer him Frank's truck for the weekend because he mentioned needing to pick up a desk from his aunt's place in Waverly.

"I haven't even asked yet," he says, when I bring up the truck.

"I know. I'm offering."

"Your dad's truck. The F-150."

"He won't mind." This is probably true. Frank is the kind of man who'd lend you his truck, his tools, and his Saturday without being asked, as long as you brought it back with the same amount of gas. But Marcus knows I've never once offered before he asked. In the original timeline, Marcus asked to borrow the truck and I said yes and then complained about it afterward to Jake Petersen, because that's who I was — someone who did the right thing and then poisoned it with resentment.

Marcus leans back in his cafeteria chair, balancing on two legs the way that drives every teacher insane. He studies me with the same focus he gives word problems.

"Did you join a cult?"

"What?"

"Like a kindness cult. A generosity seminar. Some kind of Tony Robbins thing."

"I don't know who Tony Robbins is."

"Liar. Everyone knows who Tony Robbins is. He's the giant teeth guy." Marcus sets the chair down with a thunk. "Seriously, man. You bought me lunch three days in a row. You offered me your dad's truck before I even opened my mouth. Last week you helped Cody with his lab report, and Cody literally called your taste in music 'what depression sounds like.'"

"Cody's not wrong about the music."

"Nate." He waits until I look at him. Marcus has this way of going still when something matters — everything loose and easy about him just firms up for a second, and you realize the looseness is a choice, not a default. "You're being a tryhard."

The word lands. Not because it's cruel — Marcus doesn't do cruel — but because it's precise. A tryhard. Someone performing effort instead of just existing. The basketball equivalent of the guy who yells "nice shot!" after every single basket until you want to strangle him.

"Maybe I'm just being a better friend," I say.

"Or maybe you feel bad about something and you're overcorrecting." He picks up his Gatorade — the one I bought — and takes a drink. "I don't need you to buy me stuff. I need you to be normal."

*Normal.* The word is a door I can't walk through, because normal for seventeen-year-old Nate Callahan was careless and a little selfish and took Marcus for granted in all the small ways that add up. Normal was borrowing Marcus's AP History notes and not returning them. Normal was making plans and canceling when something better came along. Normal was the slow, invisible accounting that put me twelve hundred dollars in debt to my best friend and let me tell myself it didn't matter because Marcus never brought it up.

I don't want to be that normal. But Marcus is right that what I'm doing instead is its own kind of lie.

"Okay," I say. "No more free lunch."

"I didn't say that. The pizza was good." He grins, and the gap tooth makes it look like a dare. "I'm just saying, whatever you're going through? You can just tell me. You don't have to, like, purchase my loyalty."

I almost tell him. Right there in the cafeteria with Jake Petersen arguing about Xbox games two seats down and Cody dropping a fry into his own backpack without noticing. I almost say: *I'm from the future. I know how this friendship ends. I know it's my fault. And I'm trying so hard to be different that I've turned into someone even more fake.*

Instead I say, "I think I'm just stressed about college apps."

Marcus nods, accepting this, because it's the kind of normal-sized lie that fits in the space between us. And I hate myself for it — not dramatically, not in the cinematic way, but in the tired way, the way you hate yourself at the end of a long day when you had a hundred chances to be honest and took none of them.

Anyway.

---

The group project is for Mr. Danaher's AP History class, which means it's going to be thorough, exacting, and graded on a rubric that Danaher probably workshopped in his sleep. Four students per group. The topic is the Election of 1860, and I get paired with Marcus, a girl named Erin Tsao who takes notes in three colors of pen, and Derek Holt.

Derek Holt.

I know Derek the way you know a weather pattern — not from direct experience but from tracking its effects. In the original timeline, we weren't close in high school. He was a year ahead, technically, but took some junior-level APs because he'd transferred from a private school in Des Moines. I knew him later. I knew people like him later. I eventually became people like him later.

He's seventeen, sandy blond hair that falls in a way that suggests either perfect genetics or a twenty-dollar pomade, and he has blue eyes that pay attention to everything while appearing to pay attention to nothing. His father is some kind of consultant — the vague kind, the kind where you can never quite pin down what they consult about — and Derek wears a Rolex that he claims is a graduation gift from his grandfather. It is not a graduation gift. He is not yet graduated. His father gave it to him because Derek Holt's father believes in projecting success as a prerequisite for achieving it.

None of this is sinister on its own. He's a good-looking kid with a nice watch who's smarter than most of the people in the room. The problem is that I recognize him.

"So the way I see it," Derek says, five minutes into our first group meeting in the library, "we can divide this four ways, but that's inefficient. Erin, you're the best researcher. Marcus, you're good at the visual stuff — the poster, the layout. Nate, you're the writer. And I'll handle the presentation."

Erin looks up from her three-color notes. "What does 'handle the presentation' mean?"

"I'll do the talking. The actual in-class delivery. I'm good at it, and it's worth forty percent of the grade."

He says this without arrogance. That's the trick. He says it the way you'd say *I'll drive, I know the route* — as a practical observation, not a boast. And he's right: he is good at it. Derek Holt can stand in front of a classroom and make eye contact with every person individually and modulate his voice so the teacher leans forward instead of checking the clock.

But what he's actually done, in thirty seconds of friendly delegation, is assign himself the most visible job while distributing the most labor-intensive work among the three of us. He'll spend two hours rehearsing. Erin will spend eight hours in research. Marcus will spend five on layout. I'll spend six writing. And when Mr. Danaher says "excellent work," Derek will nod graciously, as if absorbing praise on behalf of the team, when what he's really absorbing is the credit.

I watch Marcus watching Derek, and I can see Marcus clocking it too — the slight narrowing, the way his fingers pause on the edge of the table. Marcus sees it. He just doesn't think it's worth fighting about, because Marcus picks his battles and this isn't one.

"Works for me," Marcus says.

"I can research and help with the writing," Erin says, because Erin Tsao doesn't let a rubric go unmaximized.

"Perfect." Derek smiles. It's a good smile. Warm, inclusive, the kind that makes you feel like the plan was collaborative even though it was dictated. "Let's meet Thursday to sync up."

After the meeting, walking down the hall, Marcus says, "He's smooth."

"Yeah."

"Like a politician."

"That's not a compliment."

"Wasn't meant to be." Marcus shifts his backpack higher on his shoulder. "But he's not wrong that he's the best presenter. So whatever."

This is the thing about Derek Holt. He's not wrong. He's almost never wrong in the factual, strategic sense. His plans are efficient. His instincts about people are accurate. His ability to read a room and position himself optimally is, honestly, a kind of genius. What he lacks is the part of the brain that asks whether optimization is the same as decency.

I know this because I lacked it too. For years. My version was messier — less polished, more chaotic — but the operating system was the same. Identify what you want. Map the most efficient path. Treat people as variables in the equation. And if someone gets a raw deal, well, that's not malice. That's just math.

Derek Holt is what my intelligence looked like before conscience moved in and started rearranging the furniture. And watching him feels like reading your own diary from an age you'd rather forget.

Election night.

Marcus's house is a single-story ranch on Tremont Street, pale yellow siding, a basketball hoop in the driveway with a net that's been replaced so many times the rim has grooves. His mother, Mrs. Wade — Gloria — has set up the living room like it's the Super Bowl. Two bags of Doritos, a veggie tray that nobody touches, a two-liter of Coke, and a plate of brownies cut into uneven squares because Darnell, Marcus's little brother, "helped."

The TV is a big old Sony, not a flat screen — flat screens exist in 2008 but they haven't reached the Wade living room yet. It sits in an entertainment center made of pressboard and determination, and right now it's tuned to CNN, where Wolf Blitzer is doing that thing he does where every sentence sounds like the world might be ending but also might be fine.

"Sit down, Nate," Gloria says. She's got Marcus's face, or he's got hers — the same wide-set eyes, the same mouth that defaults to almost-smiling. She's wearing an Obama t-shirt that she ordered online and that arrived two sizes too big, so she's got it tucked and knotted at her hip like it's 1995. "Have you eaten?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Have you eaten enough?"

This is not a question with a correct answer, so I say, "The brownies look amazing."

"Darnell made them."

Darnell, who is eleven and already taller than some eighth graders, appears from the kitchen with chocolate on his chin. "I stirred," he clarifies.

"Stirring is the most important part," I say.

"It's really not," Marcus says from the couch, where he's already set up with his laptop open to FiveThirtyEight, which in 2008 is still the blog of a guy named Nate Silver who everybody thinks is either a genius or a crackpot.

"Nate Silver says ninety-eight percent chance Obama wins," Marcus says.

"Nate Silver is correct," I say, before I can stop myself.

Marcus looks at me. "You've been saying that for weeks."

"Because it's been true for weeks."

"Nobody's that sure about an election."

"Nate Silver is."

"The other Nate." Marcus tilts his head. "You've been weird about this. Like, weirdly confident. You told me Virginia would go blue. You told me *Indiana* might go blue."

"Indiana is a toss-up."

"Indiana hasn't gone blue since LBJ."

"I'm aware."

"So how are you so sure?"

I shrug, and the shrug is a little too careful, a little too practiced. "Demographics. Early voting numbers. The Loudon and Fairfax county registrations are through the roof. It's not a mystery if you look at the data."

Marcus doesn't push it. He files it away. I can see him doing it — the way his eyes stay on me for one extra beat, the way he nods slowly, the way his fingers tap twice on the arm of the couch. Marcus Wade has a filing system for things that don't add up, and I've just been entered into it.

The early returns come in, and the room gets louder. Marcus's father, Calvin, comes home from his shift at Tyson's still in his work boots, and Gloria makes him take them off at the door, and he stands in socked feet watching Pennsylvania go blue and says, "Well, now."

That's it. "Well, now." Two words that contain the whole history of a man who grew up in a country that told him certain things were not possible, watching one of those things become possible on a Tuesday night in November.

Gloria cries. She doesn't make a show of it. She just sits on the arm of the couch and tears run down her face, and when CNN calls it, when they project Barack Obama the forty-fourth president of the United States, she puts her hand over her mouth and breathes and Darnell asks if this means they get a day off school and Calvin says, "No, boy, it means you work harder."

Marcus watches his parents. I watch Marcus. His face does something I don't have a word for — it goes open in a way that seventeen-year-old boys almost never allow, unguarded, and I can see the future engineer in him, the systems thinker, trying to build a structure that holds this feeling so he can keep it.

And I sit on the Wades' living room carpet with a brownie going stale in my hand and I think: *I was here for this. In the original timeline, I was here for this exact night, in this exact room, and I didn't appreciate it. I was thinking about myself. I was probably composing something clever to say at school the next day, some joke about McCain's concession speech, some way to be the smartest person in the room about something that wasn't about being smart.*

On the TV, the crowd in Grant Park is enormous, oceanic, and the sound of it comes through the speakers tinny and compressed but still enough to make the hair on my arms stand up. Gloria opens her hand and Calvin takes it, and they stand there in their living room on Tremont Street in Cedar Falls, Iowa, holding hands, watching.

"I told you," I say to Marcus, quiet enough that only he hears.

"You told me," he admits.

I should stop talking. I should let it be.

"Indiana, too," I say, because I can't help it, because this is the disease — the need to be right, to be seen being right, to turn every moment into evidence of my own intelligence. Even here. Even now. Even watching Gloria Wade cry.

Marcus gives me a look. Not angry. Worse — curious. "Yeah," he says. "You called that."

---

I drive home in Frank's truck, which he lent me without being asked because Frank Callahan lends things the way trees make oxygen — automatically, without tracking the debt. The heater rattles. The dead pine air freshener has graduated from faintly aromatic to purely decorative. The radio is on, and someone is analyzing exit polls, and I turn it off because I can't listen to anyone else be certain about anything tonight.

The streets of Cedar Falls are quiet the way small Iowa towns are quiet on weekday nights — porch lights on, living rooms flickering blue with television, the occasional dog trotting across a yard. It's thirty-eight degrees and dropping, and my breath fogs when I stop at the light on Main and University.

I think about Gloria Wade's hand over her mouth.

I think about Calvin saying *work harder.*

I think about Marcus's face, unguarded, open, trusting the moment to hold him.

I think about how I sat in that room as a guest, a welcomed and fed and browned guest, and my first instinct was to make sure everyone knew I'd predicted it. I was right. I was right. Look at me being right.

This is what I am. Was. Am. The tenses collapse when you're thirty-five pretending to be seventeen. What I *am* is someone who takes the warmest, most human moments in other people's lives and finds a way to make them about me. What I *was* is someone who did that for eighteen more years after this night and lost everything that mattered in the process.

I pull into the driveway on Sycamore — no. Not Sycamore. That's the apartment. That's the future. This is Maple Drive, the house with the uneven front steps that Frank keeps meaning to fix. I sit in the truck for a minute with the engine idling.

Authenticity. I keep circling the word like it's a drain. Be authentic. Be real. Be honest. But here's what I've figured out in the three weeks since I woke up in a bedroom with a Dark Knight poster: authenticity isn't something you perform. You can't try to be genuine. The trying is the opposite of the thing. It's like trying to fall asleep — the effort pushes the goal further away.

So what do you do? If trying to be good is just another kind of performance, and not trying got you a FedEx Office job and a disconnected phone number and an apartment full of nothing?

I don't know. I turn off the truck and go inside, and Frank is asleep in his Adirondack chair on the porch with a blanket over his legs that Diane must have put there. I don't wake him. I just stand for a second, looking at the shape of him in the dark — the reading glasses still on, the reddish-brown hair catching the porch light — and then I go upstairs and lie in my childhood bed and stare at the ceiling and don't sleep for a long time.

---

Friday. After school. Marcus and I walk toward Tremont. The air smells like burning leaves — someone on Elm Street has a pile going, which is technically against city ordinance but practically universal.

We've been talking about nothing. Jake Petersen's new haircut, which looks like he lost a bet. Cody Voss's theory that energy drinks don't actually give you energy, they just borrow it from tomorrow. Whether Mr. Danaher grades harder before or after lunch. The comfortable, pointless conversation of two people who don't need a reason to talk.

Marcus stops at the corner of Tremont and Orchard. This is where we split — he goes left, I go right. We've stood at this corner a thousand times. Will stand here a thousand more, if I don't ruin it.

"So," Marcus says. He adjusts his backpack. The Casio watch — three minutes fast, always, because Marcus believes being early is a form of respect — catches the late afternoon sun. "You called that election thing dead on."

I shrug. "Lucky guess."

"Nah." He shakes his head, slow. "It wasn't a guess. You knew. Virginia, Indiana, the whole map. You knew."

"I just read a lot of polls."

Marcus looks at me. The gap-toothed grin is gone. In its place is something quieter, more focused — the look he gets when he's working a problem that doesn't resolve cleanly. "Okay," he says.

That's it. *Okay.* He doesn't push. He doesn't accuse. He just takes the thing that doesn't add up and puts it in the file, and the file gets a little thicker, and Marcus Wade walks away down Tremont Street with his hands in his pockets and his backpack slung over one shoulder.

He doesn't push it.

But he remembers.

Chapter Four: The Gravity of Old Habits

The thing about gravity is that it doesn't care what you know. You can understand orbital mechanics, terminal velocity, the precise math of a body in free fall, and none of it changes the fact that when you step off the edge, you go down. Every time. No exceptions for the well-informed.

I learn this again on December nineteenth, 2008, in Tyler Briggs's basement.

---

The party is Marcus's idea, which is the problem, because saying no to Marcus requires a reason and I don't have one that works. Not one I can say out loud. *I can't go because I'm secretly thirty-five and I know that a beer at a high school party is the first domino in a seventeen-year sequence that ends with me drinking alone in a Waterloo apartment at two in the morning on a Tuesday.* That's not a thing you can say.

So I say sure.

Tyler Briggs's house sits on a cul-de-sac off West First Street, one of those split-levels from the seventies with wood paneling and carpet the color of overcooked peas. His parents are in Des Moines for a wedding. The basement smells like old laundry detergent and the particular staleness of a room that doesn't get enough air. Someone has taped a strand of Christmas lights along the drop ceiling, which gives everything a dim, reddish tint, like the whole room is blushing.

The music is too loud. It's always too loud — some playlist running through a dock speaker that distorts on the bass hits, cycling between Fall Out Boy and Lil Wayne and whatever else the algorithm of 2008 has decided we need. There are maybe forty people down here, which is twenty too many for the square footage. You can feel the floor flex when enough of them shift weight at the same time.

I know exactly three things about Tyler Briggs: he plays JV soccer, his older brother bought two cases of Keystone Light for tonight, and he will fail out of Iowa State in two years. That last one I keep to myself.

Marcus is already across the room, talking to a girl from our calc class whose name I should remember but don't. He's got a red cup but he's just holding it — Marcus has always been better at performing the social contract without actually signing it. He'll nurse one beer for three hours and no one will notice. I used to think that was boring. Now I understand it's a skill I never developed.

I'm standing near the stairs with my hands in my jacket pockets, doing the thing where you lean against a wall and try to look like you're choosing not to participate rather than failing to. There's a cooler three feet to my left. The Keystone cans are sitting in a gray slush of half-melted ice. I can hear them shifting against each other every time someone reaches in.

I'm not going to drink. I know this. I have decided this. I am a thirty-five-year-old man who has seen exactly where this road goes, and I am not going to drink a Keystone Light in Tyler Briggs's basement because I am better than this, smarter than this, more experienced than this.

"Callahan. Catch."

A can arcs toward me. I catch it. Reflex. Some kid I half-recognize — Dustin, Dalton, something with a D — grins at me and turns away, already forgetting I exist. The can is cold and wet in my hand. Water beads run down the aluminum and pool between my fingers.

I should set it down. I should set it on the edge of that folding table next to the bowl of pretzels that everyone's been touching with their unwashed hands, and I should walk away, and I should find Marcus, and I should say *let's go,* and this is the moment, this is literally the exact moment, I recognize it the way you recognize a street you crashed on — the light, the angle, the placement of every object — and I crack the tab.

It doesn't feel like a decision. That's what I need you to understand. It feels like a continuation. The can was in my hand and then it was open and then it was at my mouth and the first sip is exactly what I remember: thin, metallic, barely beer, more like carbonated regret. Keystone Light tastes like nothing pretending to be something, which, if I'm being honest, was the appeal. You don't drink it for the flavor. You drink it because it's there and because the alternative is standing in Tyler Briggs's basement with your hands in your pockets feeling the weight of seventeen years of future mistakes pressing down on your chest.

The second sip is easier. They always are.

By the third beer I'm talking to people. That's the trick of it, the thing that made alcohol feel necessary for so long — it sands down the edges of me. Sober Nate is too much. He's too aware, too precise, too careful about what he says and how he says it. Drunk Nate is just a kid at a party. Drunk Nate doesn't carry the specific knowledge that the girl laughing by the speaker will lose her mom in 2014, or that the kid doing a handstand against the wall will serve two tours in Afghanistan. Drunk Nate is free in the only way that counts, which is that he doesn't have to pretend.

I'm laughing at something someone said. I don't remember what. The basement is too hot now — all those bodies, the single vent that Tyler's dad probably hasn't cleaned since Clinton was in office — and I've taken off my jacket and I'm sitting on the arm of a couch that sags in the middle and I feel good. I feel young. I feel like this body and this moment are the only things that are real and everything else — the invasion, the displacement, the future I'm supposed to be preventing — is just a bad dream I had once.

The fourth beer. Someone's telling a story about their older cousin who got a DUI on a riding mower. The room thinks this is the funniest thing they've ever heard. I think it's pretty funny too.

I don't remember the exact math of how many. I know it was more than four. I know I wasn't blackout because I remember everything, which is almost worse — I remember being the guy I swore I wouldn't be, and I remember choosing it, and I remember that it felt like relief.

At some point I'm outside. The cold hits like a door slamming. December in Iowa, ten degrees without wind chill, and I'm standing on Tyler's back patio in a T-shirt, watching my breath crystallize under the porch light, and the stars are out — hard, white, unforgiving — and somewhere up there, right now, the Kessari are doing whatever the Kessari do in the years before they arrive, and I'm drunk in a basement because a kid tossed me a can and I caught it.

Gravity.

---

I wake up on Marcus's couch.

It's the plaid one from his living room, the one with the cigarette burn on the left armrest from when his uncle visited two Thanksgivings ago. There's a glass of water on the coffee table next to my head and a folded blanket over my legs — the heavy crocheted one his grandmother made, brown and orange, the color scheme of the seventies. My shoes are off. They're lined up neatly by the front door, which means Marcus took them off because I would have kicked them in different directions.

The clock on the wall says 8:14. Light through the curtains is gray and flat. My mouth tastes like a gas station carpet.

Marcus comes out of the kitchen holding a bowl of cereal. He's still in his clothes from last night — the grey hoodie, the basketball shorts he wears even in December. His Nike high-tops are on, which means he's been up for a while. The Casio on his wrist says 8:17, three minutes fast, as always.

He sits in the armchair across from me and eats his cereal. He doesn't say anything.

This is the thing about Marcus that I didn't deserve the first time around and deserve even less now: he drove me here. He loaded me into his mom's Civic and drove me six blocks and walked me to his couch and took off my shoes and filled a glass of water and found the blanket and went to bed. He didn't call my parents. He didn't leave me at Tyler's. He didn't lecture me in the car.

He just brought water and a blanket.

I sit up. The room lists to starboard. I drink the water in four long swallows and set the glass down and say, "Thanks."

Marcus chews. Swallows. Looks at me with an expression that isn't anger or disappointment — it's curiosity. The same look he gets when a calc problem doesn't resolve the way he expected.

"You good?" he says.

"Yeah."

"Cool."

He goes back to his cereal. After a minute, he says, "My mom left at seven for her shift. She didn't see you. In case you were wondering."

"I was."

"Figured."

He doesn't ask what happened. He doesn't ask why I drank when I told him I wasn't going to, or why I was the loudest person in the room by midnight, or why I sat on the curb outside and stared at the sky for ten minutes like I was waiting for something to fall out of it. He just eats his Honey Nut Cheerios and watches SportsCenter with the volume on low, and the kindness of it sits in my chest like something I swallowed wrong.

I want him to yell at me. I want him to say *what the hell, Nate* or *you were a mess* or *don't put me in that position again.* Judgment I can metabolize. I have systems for it — deflect, joke, redirect, move on. Judgment is a wall you can push against.

This is just open air.

"I'm sorry," I say.

Marcus looks at me. Shrugs. "You don't owe me anything, man. But you were talking weird again."

My stomach drops. "Weird how?"

"Told Jake Henderson that his dad's business was going to go under in three years and he should tell him to diversify into something, I don't know, the music was loud. Something about solar." He pauses. "Jake's dad owns a hardware store."

"Right."

"Solar, Nate."

"I was drunk."

"Yeah." He sets the cereal bowl on the side table. "You were. But you say weird stuff sober too. You know that, right? That election thing. The way you talk in class sometimes, like you already know the answer and you're deciding whether to share it." He leans forward, elbows on his knees. "I'm not trying to be a dick. I just — I noticed."

"I know you noticed."

"So what's the deal?"

I have thirty-five years of the truth sitting in my throat and nowhere to put it. So I do what I do. I say, "I just read a lot."

Marcus holds my gaze for three seconds, then nods slowly, the way you nod when you know someone's lying and you've decided to let them. "Okay," he says. "Drink your water."

---

Here's what I know about the original timeline:

Junior year. Christmas break. A party at Tyler Briggs's house — the same house, the same basement, the same Keystone Light. I drank. Then I drank at the New Year's party. Then I drank on a random Tuesday in January because Marcus's mom had vodka in the freezer and Marcus was at a robotics meeting. Then it was something I did. Then it was something I was.

It took seventeen years to build the cage and I'd just started bending the first bar.

I don't stop drinking because I'm strong. I stop because I'm terrified. There's a difference. Strength is choosing the salad because you want to be healthy. Terror is seeing the X-ray of what's growing inside you and putting down the cigarette with shaking hands. I have the X-ray. I've lived inside it. I know exactly what this becomes: the apartment, the Busch Light towers on the counter, the alarm I stopped setting because the job I stopped going to didn't need me there on time.

The morning after the party, I walk home from Marcus's house in the December cold. The hangover feels like justice. I take a shower so hot it turns my skin pink and I stand under the water until the tank runs cold and then I sit on the edge of my bed in a towel and I make a decision that isn't really a decision. It's more like setting a bone. You don't choose for it to hurt. You just hold still while it happens.

I will not drink again. Not because I'm above it. Because I'm not.

---

School reconvenes after New Year's and I throw myself into something that isn't drinking.

It starts in the Cedar Falls Public Library, which smells the way every library smells — carpet cleaner, old paper, the faint ozone of ancient desktop computers. The reference section is in the back, past the magazine racks with their copies of *Time* and *Newsweek* showing Obama's face under headlines about hope and change. The librarian is a woman named Gail who wears reading glasses on a beaded chain and has never once asked me what I'm looking for, which I appreciate.

What I'm looking for: I don't know. Not exactly.

I can't stop the Kessari. I'm a seventeen-year-old in Cedar Falls, Iowa, with a 3.4 GPA and forty-three dollars in a savings account. I don't have technology, or authority, or access to anyone who would believe me if I told them what was coming. What I have is time. Twenty-six years of it. And the specific, useless knowledge that when it all falls apart, what kills people isn't the initial strike — it's the aftermath. The supply chains that collapse. The hospitals that lose power. The evacuation routes that turn into parking lots. The systems that were never built to handle a world where the rules change overnight.

I pull books off the shelf: *Emergency Management: Principles and Practice.* Dated, clinical, useful. *The Unthinking of the American Mind* — wrong shelf, I push it back. *Disaster Planning and Control.* FEMA publications. A textbook on organizational resilience that's so dry it could start a fire, but I read it anyway, taking notes on index cards the way I used to in the other timeline, before I stopped taking notes on anything.

I start looking at college catalogs. UNI has an emergency management program. Iowa State has engineering. The University of Iowa has public health. I don't know which one is right because I don't know what the right thing looks like — I just know that sitting in Cedar Falls waiting for the sky to open is not a plan.

Some afternoons I stay at the library until Gail turns off the lights in the reference section at eight-thirty. I sit at the long table by the window with the orange desk lamp and I read about crisis communication and incident command structures and the way systems fail — not all at once, but in cascades, each failure creating the conditions for the next one, dominoes that don't fall in a line but in expanding circles. It reminds me of drinking. One thing makes the next thing possible until the whole structure is on the ground and you're standing in the wreckage saying *how did this happen* even though you were there for every step.

Marcus finds me in the library one Wednesday and sits down across from me without asking. He picks up one of the FEMA pamphlets and reads the title aloud: "*Are You Ready? An In-Depth Guide to Citizen Preparedness.*" He sets it down. "You going survivalist on me, Callahan?"

"Research project."

"For what class?"

"Independent study."

"We don't have independent study."

"Then it's a hobby."

He picks up the pamphlet again, flips through it. "This is some dark reading material, Nate."

"I'm a dark guy."

He looks at me. The curiosity is back — that patient, precise attention that makes Marcus a good engineer, or will make him one, someday. "You know you can talk to me, right? About whatever's going on?"

"I know."

"But you're not going to."

"Not today."

He nods. Stands up. Pushes his chair in. "Okay. But I'm timing you on this. Three months. If you're still being weird in three months, I'm staging an intervention."

"With who?"

"I'll figure that out. I have time."

He walks away. His Nike high-tops squeak on the library floor. Gail gives him a look.

---

It's a Thursday night in late January. Derek Holt and I are in the school library — the school one, not public, smaller, fluorescent-lit, smelling of dry-erase markers — working on the second phase of our econ project. We're building a mock investment portfolio and Derek is treating it like a blood oath. He has spreadsheets. Printed spreadsheets. Color-coded.

We've been at it for two hours and the building is nearly empty — just us, a janitor somewhere down the hall making rhythmic thumps with a mop bucket, and the hum of the overhead lights that never fully goes away.

Derek is leaning back in his chair, clicking a mechanical pencil in a pattern that probably maps to some internal calculation. His polo is wrinkled, which is unusual. Derek Holt does not wrinkle. He is the kind of seventeen-year-old who irons his jeans — or at least I assumed he was. His father's Rolex catches the light every time his wrist moves, but tonight I notice the band is loose. Too big for him. A man's watch on a boy's wrist.

"You ever think about what you'd do," he says, "if you had to start from nothing?"

I look up from the laptop. "What do you mean?"

"Nothing. Zero. No savings, no house, no — " He stops clicking the pencil. Stares at the spreadsheet. "Hypothetical."

I wait.

The janitor's bucket hits a door frame somewhere. The fluorescent lights hum their single note.

"My dad lost the house," Derek says. He says it the way you say *it's raining* — flatly, aimed at no one. "Back in October. Subprime whatever. You've seen the news." He laughs, a short, sharp sound that has no humor in it. "He had three properties. Leveraged one against the other. It worked for a while. Then it didn't."

He's not looking at me. He's looking at the spreadsheet, at the neat colored rows, at the fake portfolio we built for a grade.

"My mom works at Hy-Vee now. Deli counter. She does the morning shift and then she cleans offices at night. She comes home at eleven and her hands smell like industrial soap and lunch meat and she sits at the kitchen table and she does the bills and she doesn't cry, which is worse, honestly — I'd rather she cried — she just sits there doing math that doesn't work."

I don't say anything. There's nothing to say.

"The watch was my grandfather's," he says, and touches the Rolex. "Dad gave it to me for my sixteenth birthday. Mom wanted to pawn it. Dad said no. They fought about it for two hours." He turns his wrist. The watch face glints. "Sixty-seven hundred dollars, retail. That's four months of mortgage payments. And I'm wearing it to school like a — "

He stops.

"Money is the only thing that's real," he says. "People say it's not. People say it's about, I don't know, passion, or purpose, or whatever they put on motivational posters. But when you don't have it, you find out fast what's real. It's the thing that keeps the lights on. It's the thing that keeps your mom from working two jobs. Everything else is what you talk about when you've never had to worry."

I know this speech. Not these words, but the architecture of it — the logic of a kid who watched his family's security dissolve and decided, at seventeen, that he would never let it happen again. That money was a wall you could build. That enough of it made you safe. I know where this logic goes. I've seen Derek at thirty-five in the other timeline, or a version of him — I never kept track, honestly, I was too busy failing — but I know the type. The ambition that starts as survival and curdles into something colder.

But right now he's not cold. He's a seventeen-year-old kid with a too-big watch and a mom who smells like lunch meat, and he's telling me this because it's ten o'clock on a Thursday and I'm the one who's here.

"That sucks," I say. Because it does. And because anything more would be performance.

Derek looks at me. His blue eyes are red-rimmed in the fluorescent light. "Yeah," he says. "It does."

We sit there for a minute. Then he straightens, clicks the pencil twice, and says, "Anyway. The mock portfolio. I think we should weight heavier toward index funds — the returns are boring but the Sharpe ratio makes the argument for us."

And we go back to the spreadsheet. But something has shifted, the way a room changes when someone opens a window. Not warmer. Just different air.

---

Sophie turns fourteen on February seventh.

Diane makes a cake — yellow, from a box, with chocolate frosting from a can, because that's what Sophie has asked for every year since she was nine. Frank puts fourteen candles on it and lights them with the long-handled lighter from the grill and we sing happy birthday in the kitchen while Sophie sits at the table looking like she wants to dissolve into the floor, which is how thirteen-year-olds — fourteen-year-olds, now — receive affection. She blows out the candles in one breath and Frank says "atta girl" and Diane says "make a wish, sweetheart" and Sophie says "I already did, Mom, that's the whole point of the breath."

I give her the book.

It's *Stiff: The Curious Lives of Human Cadavers* by Mary Roach. I found it at the used bookstore on Main Street for six dollars. The cover is slightly bent. I wrapped it in newspaper because I couldn't find wrapping paper, which, looking at it now, gives it a vaguely serial-killer aesthetic that I did not intend.

Sophie peels the newspaper off. Reads the title. Looks at me.

"You said you wanted to know what happens to bodies," I say. "In October. You were watching that forensics show and you said you wished they'd explain the actual science instead of just showing the dead people."

She stares at the book. Then at me. Her braces catch the kitchen light.

"You listened?" she says.

"I'm trying to."

She holds the book against her chest, which is the Sophie equivalent of a standing ovation. Then she narrows her eyes — suspicion snapping back like a rubber band. "This is weird. You're being weird again."

"Probably."

"Boys don't listen. Like, as a species."

"I'm working on it."

She opens the book to a random page, reads three lines, and says, "Oh, this is *gross,*" in a voice of pure, undisguised delight. She takes it upstairs. Diane mouths *thank you* at me from the sink. Frank, cutting a second slice of cake he doesn't need, gives me a nod. Just a nod. The kind that means more than he'll ever put into words.

---

February drags on. Iowa in February is a test of character that the state administers without grading — you just survive it or you don't. The sky is a low ceiling of grey felt. The temperature is a thing that happens to you. I go to school, I go to the library, I come home. I don't drink. I think about drinking — not constantly, but in the way you think about a noise you heard downstairs. You know it's probably nothing. You check anyway.

One evening I'm at the kitchen table with a stack of college catalogs spread out in front of me. UNI, Iowa State, University of Iowa. I've dog-eared pages. Emergency management. Civil engineering. Public health. I'm drawing lines between them in my head, trying to build a degree out of pieces that don't naturally fit together — the institutional knowledge to understand how systems work, the technical knowledge to build better ones, the communication skills to make people listen when you tell them the sky is falling. Literally.

I hear Frank before I see him. The creak of the back door, the thump of work boots on the mat, the particular way he clears his throat when he's been outside in the cold — a deep, rattling sound, like an engine turning over. He comes into the kitchen smelling like pipe solder and the cold air that clings to canvas jackets. He opens the fridge, stands in front of it for a full ten seconds like it's going to offer him something it didn't offer him yesterday, takes out a Miller Lite, and closes the door.

He sees the catalogs. I see him see them. His eyes move across the spread — the dog-eared pages, the highlighted programs, the notes I've written in the margins — and something crosses his face that he kills before it fully forms. Not surprise. Something closer to recognition. Like he's been waiting to see this and now that it's here, he doesn't know what to do with it.

"Homework?" he says.

"Research."

He nods. Takes his beer to the living room. The recliner creaks. The TV comes on. AM radio statistics give way to evening news.

I gather the catalogs and go upstairs.

The next morning, I come down for breakfast. Diane is at the counter with her Mr. Coffee. Sophie is eating toast and reading *Stiff* at the table, which is a sentence I never thought I'd have to construct. Frank has already left for work — his cereal bowl is in the sink, rinsed but not washed, the Cheerios residue forming a pale ring around the drain.

On the kitchen table, in the spot where I sat last night, there's a pamphlet. University of Northern Iowa. The cover shows the Campanile in autumn, leaves blowing across the lawn, students walking with backpacks. Inside, someone has circled the emergency management program in blue ballpoint pen. The same pen Frank keeps in his shirt pocket at work. The same blue ink that's on every check he's ever written.

The corners of the pamphlet are bent. Creased deep, like someone gripped it too hard.

I pick it up. I look at it for a long time.

Diane is watching me from the counter. She takes a sip of coffee and says nothing. Sophie turns a page.

I put the pamphlet in my backpack. I zip the pocket closed.

Frank gets home at five-thirty that evening. He takes off his boots, opens his Miller Lite, sits in the recliner. I'm on the couch pretending to read. The TV is on. Neither of us is watching it.

He doesn't mention the pamphlet. I don't mention the pamphlet. We sit there in the living room with the TV talking to itself and the furnace cycling on and off and the whole weight of what he said without saying it filling the room like heat from a vent.

This is how Frank Callahan tells you he believes in you. He finds the thing. He circles it in blue ink. He puts it where you'll see it. And then he sits in his recliner and drinks his beer and waits, without asking, without pushing, without needing you to acknowledge it, because the acknowledgment isn't the point. The point is the pamphlet on the table. The point is that he saw you trying and he answered the only way he knows how.

I don't say thank you. He doesn't need me to.

But I carry it with me — the pamphlet, and the bent corners, and the blue circle around a future I haven't earned yet — and it's heavier than it should be, and lighter, and enough.

---

Chapter Five: The Cassandra Problem

The thing about Cassandra is that everyone gets the myth wrong. They think the tragedy is that nobody believed her. But that's not it. The tragedy is that she couldn't stop telling the truth. The gods didn't just curse her with disbelief — they cursed her with compulsion. She had to keep talking. Had to keep watching people's faces close like doors.

Anyway.

March in Iowa is winter pretending to interview for spring. The snow pulls back from the lawns in ugly patches, revealing all the things people lost in November — a tennis ball, a plastic bag, someone's mitten stiffened into a claw. The sky is the color of old dishwater. Everything smells like wet earth and patience.

I am seventeen years old and I know that General Motors will file for bankruptcy in seventy-two days.

This is not the kind of thing you can tell people. I'm learning this. Slowly, painfully, the way you learn anything real — by getting it wrong first.

---

Marcus drives a 1997 Honda Civic with a tape deck and one working speaker on the passenger side, which means every song sounds like it's being whispered directly into my right ear by a ghost with questionable taste in music. He keeps a St. Christopher medal on the rearview mirror that his grandmother gave him, and when he takes turns too fast it taps against the windshield like a tiny metallic heartbeat.

We're heading north on University Avenue because Marcus wants to look at the new pedestrian bridge they're building over Dry Run Creek. He wants to build bridges. I've said this before. He really does. He pulls over on the shoulder and leans across me to stare through the passenger window at the half-finished span, rebar jutting from concrete like bones from a compound fracture, and his whole face changes. Goes soft and serious simultaneously. Most people look like that at sunsets or babies. Marcus looks like that at load-bearing structures.

"They're using post-tensioned concrete," he says, like he's announcing the birth of his firstborn.

"Looks expensive."

"Everything good is expensive. That's literally what good means."

He pulls back onto the road. The St. Christopher medal swings. I watch the unfinished bridge shrink in the side mirror — a thing in the middle of becoming itself.

"Hey," I say. "GM is going to go bankrupt."

I don't plan to say it. That's the thing. The compulsion. Cassandra opening her mouth at exactly the wrong moment because the gods won't let her keep it shut.

Marcus doesn't even look over. "GM as in General Motors."

"Yeah."

"The car company."

"The very one."

"Nate, GM is not going to go bankrupt. They're General Motors. They made the Corvette. They're like — they're America."

"They're going to file Chapter 11 before summer."

He does look at me now. Quick, then back to the road. His silver Casio catches a band of sunlight through the windshield. "Why do you think that?"

And here's where I've gotten better at this. Smarter. More practiced in the machinery of deception. "Look at the burn rate on their cash reserves," I say. "The credit markets are frozen. They've been losing market share for twenty years. The bailout money Congress gave them isn't enough to restructure. The math doesn't work."

All of which is true. All of which I could have read in any newspaper if I were the kind of seventeen-year-old who read the financial section of newspapers, which I have carefully, over these past months, established myself to be. The lie works because it's made of true things. The best ones always are.

Marcus is quiet for two blocks. Then: "You said Virginia would go blue."

"I did."

"And Indiana."

"Right."

"And they did."

"They did."

Another block. He drums his thumbs on the steering wheel. "If you're right about this, that's three for three. You know that."

"It's not — it's just paying attention, Marcus."

"Yeah," he says. He doesn't sound convinced. He doesn't sound unconvinced either. He sounds like someone filing something away for later. "Sure."

The St. Christopher medal swings.

I stare at the road and think about the first time I watched GM collapse. I was thirty-one. Mara and I were in our apartment in Des Moines, and she was grading papers at the kitchen table, and the news came on the little TV we kept on the counter, and I said something like *about time*, and she said *people are going to lose their jobs, Nate*, and she was right. She was usually right about the human cost of things. That was one of her gifts. Looking at a headline and seeing the people underneath it.

I don't know where Mara is right now. Eighth grade, maybe. Painting her nails with her friends and being thirteen and not knowing that in seven years she'll sit next to a strange boy in an Introduction to Crisis Communication class and say, "You look like you've seen a ghost," and he'll say, "You have no idea."

The point is: I told Marcus about GM because I couldn't not tell him. The same way I couldn't not tell Mr. Danaher about Virginia. The same way I'll keep leaking the future in small, careful doses, telling myself each time that this is the last one, that I'll stop, that the next prediction will stay locked in the box where I keep the things I'm not allowed to say.

The box is getting full. The lid doesn't close right anymore.

---

Mr. Paulsen teaches AP Government in Room 214, which always smells like dry-erase markers and the particular staleness of a room whose windows haven't been opened since the Clinton administration. He's forty-something, receding hairline, wears the same rotation of four polo shirts in colors that suggest he bought them as a pack. He's not a bad teacher. He's just a man who found a groove in 1998 and has been riding it since.

We're talking about pandemic preparedness because it's on the syllabus, sandwiched between FEMA org charts and something about the Stafford Act. Paulsen puts a slide up about the 1918 flu and reads the bullet points verbatim, which is a teaching strategy in the same way that microwaving a steak is cooking.

"The point is," he says — and yes, I notice that he says it too, everyone says it, it's the universal human signal for *I'm about to skip the complicated part* — "we haven't seen anything like that in modern times. Modern medicine, modern sanitation, modern communication networks. A pandemic on that scale is largely a historical concern."

And I should let it go. I should nod and write something in my notebook and let the moment pass like a cloud.

"A major pandemic is overdue," I say.

Paulsen looks up from his slides. "Overdue how?"

"Statistically. Epidemiologically. We've had SARS, we've had avian flu scares, we've had small outbreaks that got contained by luck as much as by infrastructure. The conditions are all there — global travel networks, factory farming, gaps in surveillance. It's not if, it's when."

The room is quiet. Twenty-two juniors and seniors who were hoping to coast through the last period of the day are suddenly looking at me.

"That's a fairly alarmist position," Paulsen says, but he says it carefully, the way teachers do when a student has said something they can't immediately dismiss.

"It's the WHO's position. It's in their annual risk assessments."

"Well." He adjusts his polo shirt collar. "Let's hope they're wrong."

Three weeks later, the first reports of H1N1 come out of Mexico. Within a month, it's in the United States. Within two months, the WHO declares a pandemic.

Paulsen sends me an email through the school's system. I open it in the library, sitting at one of the particleboard desks near the reference section, Gail reshelving returns behind me with her careful, deliberate rhythm.

*Nate — How did you know? — Mr. Paulsen*

I type back: *Lucky guess.*

I close the browser. I sit there for a while. Gail pushes her cart past and says, "You look like you swallowed something sideways, hon," and I say, "Just tired," and she says, "Sleep more, read less," and then adds, "Actually, don't. Keep reading."

The lie tastes worse each time. Not worse like something going stale — worse like something getting sharper. The first lies were smooth, easy to swallow, little white capsules of self-preservation. Now they have edges. They catch in my throat. Lucky guess. Paying attention. I just read a lot. Each one is a tiny betrayal of the people who are starting to trust me, starting to look at me with something other than casual indifference, starting to think that maybe Nate Callahan is someone worth listening to.

They're right. I am worth listening to. That's the whole problem.

---

Sophie finds me on the back porch in April. I'm sitting on the steps watching the neighbor's dog — a barrel-chested Lab named Dutch — dig a hole in their yard with a commitment that borders on religious conviction. The air is finally warm enough to sit outside without a coat, barely, if you're wearing a flannel and willing to argue about it.

She sits down next to me. Not close. She leaves exactly enough space between us to establish that this is not a casual thing, that she has come here with purpose. She's wearing a hoodie with the sleeves pushed up and the strings pulled to different lengths, and her ponytail is slightly off-center in a way that would bother most people but doesn't bother Sophie because Sophie has never in her life cared about symmetry.

She doesn't say anything for a while. Dutch digs. Dirt flies. A robin lands on the fence and leaves.

"You're not you anymore," she says.

I look at her. She's watching Dutch, not me. Her braces catch the light when she presses her lips together.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean what I said. You're not you. You used to be — I don't know. Louder? More annoying? You used to fight with Dad about curfew and leave your dishes in the sink and play that same Eminem song forty times until Mom threatened to hide your speakers. Now you're — " She waves a hand vaguely at all of me. "This."

"People grow up, Soph."

"Not like that. Not overnight."

"It wasn't overnight. It was —"

"It was since September. Since school started. You came back from summer and you were different. Everyone says so."

"Everyone?"

"Me and Mom. Dad won't say it but he looks at you sometimes like he's trying to figure out where you put the real Nate."

Dutch has stopped digging. He's lying in his hole now, panting, satisfied with his work. Sophie pulls at a thread on her sleeve.

"People change," I say. "It's normal."

"People don't change," she says. "They just get better at hiding."

She says it plainly. Not mean, not kind. Just factual, the way she says everything, because Sophie at fourteen has already figured out that observation is a form of power and she wields it without apology.

I don't say anything. What do you say to that? She's right. She's so exactly right that the truth of it sits between us like a physical object, heavy and undeniable, a stone on the step.

"I'm still me," I say, and even I can hear how thin it sounds.

She finally looks at me. Straight on, no flinching. She has Frank's eyes — that particular shade of gray-blue that looks like Lake Michigan in October — and right now they're doing something Frank's never do. They're asking.

"Okay," she says.

She doesn't believe me. She gets up and goes inside and the screen door claps shut behind her and I sit there on the porch while Dutch climbs out of his hole and shakes himself off and trots away like the whole exercise meant nothing, like destruction and departure are the same gesture.

People don't change. They just get better at hiding.

I'm going to hear that for a long time.

---

The college essay writes itself. That's the problem with it.

I sit at the desk in my room — the same desk where seventeen-year-old Nate carved his initials into the underside of the drawer in eighth grade, I checked, they're still there, NGP, the P for Patrick which is my middle name and which I have hated since birth — and I write about community resilience.

I write about Cedar Falls during the 2008 flood. How the river rose and the sandbags went up and people who'd never spoken to each other stood shoulder to shoulder in the mud passing fifty-pound bags down a human chain. How the National Guard came and the Red Cross came but it was the neighbors who came first. How resilience isn't a policy or a plan but a practice, something communities do with their hands before they do it with their budgets.

It's good. It's really good. I know it's good because I've written grant proposals and program evaluations and a thesis chapter about exactly this, in a life that hasn't happened yet, and all that experience flows into this essay like water into a channel that was already cut.

I write about wanting to study emergency management because I believe preparation is a form of compassion. Because the next disaster is always coming, and the question isn't whether we'll face it but whether we'll face it together.

And here's the thing. It's genuine. Every word of it is something I actually believe, actually feel, actually care about with the full weight of everything I know and everything I've seen. It is the most honest document I have ever produced.

It is also strategic. Every paragraph is calibrated. The hook, the personal narrative, the pivot to professional aspiration, the landing. I know what admissions committees want because I've sat on the other side of that table. I know how to make sincerity look like sincerity, which is different from sincerity being sincerity, except when it's both, except I can't tell anymore.

The point is: I get in. Early acceptance. Full ride.

Frank doesn't say anything when I tell him. He's in his Adirondack chair with a can of Busch Light, the last Saturday in May, late afternoon light coming in at that low angle that makes everything look like a memory even while it's happening. He nods once. Takes a drink. Looks at the yard.

"Good," he says.

And then, so quietly I almost miss it: "That's real good, bud."

---

June. Graduation.

The gym smells like floor wax and the collective anxiety of four hundred parents trying to record video on devices they don't fully understand. The bleachers are packed. Someone has strung blue and white streamers from the basketball hoops, and they twist in the draft from the open doors like the whole room is breathing.

We're in alphabetical order, which puts me between Emily Caldwell, who is crying already, and Pete Carpenter, who is asleep. The gowns are cheap and shiny and make everyone look like they're wrapped in blue garbage bags. I am wearing a cap that doesn't fit and a tassel that keeps swinging into my left eye and a pair of dress shoes that Frank lent me because we're the same size now, which is a fact that seems to have startled both of us.

Derek Holt gives the commencement speech because of course he does. He stands at the podium in his too-big gown with his grandfather's Rolex peeking from his sleeve and he talks about ambition, about reaching for things, about the future being a blank page waiting for their story. It's polished. It's practiced. He hits every beat — the humor, the sincerity, the call to action. The parents love it. The teachers love it. Derek's mother, in the third row, holds her phone up with both hands and her face is doing something complicated and fierce.

He doesn't mention his father losing the house. He doesn't mention his mother's two jobs. He talks about blank pages and reaching for things and he means it, he really does, and the distance between what he says and what he knows is something only he and I can see.

Money is the only thing that's real, he told me. Standing there in his blue gown, talking about dreams, he looks like someone who has learned to perform belief. I recognize the skill. I practice it daily.

Marcus is three rows behind me. I can hear him because Marcus is not a person who experiences things quietly. He whoops when they call his name. He walks across the stage like the stage was built for him, all six feet three inches, his gap-toothed grin visible from the back row, his Nike high-tops just barely showing under the gown's hem. His grandmother is in the bleachers with a camera that probably cost more than the Civic and she is taking photos with the focused intensity of a war correspondent.

They call my name. Nathan Patrick Callahan. I walk across the stage and shake the principal's hand and take the diploma and I think: I've done this before. Not metaphorically. Not spiritually. I have literally done this before, in 2009, the first time, when I was actually seventeen, when the future was actually unknown, when the diploma meant something because I didn't already know that the world it promised was going to end.

I look out at the bleachers. Diane is crying. Not delicately — fully, with Kleenex, her wire glasses pushed up on top of her head where they always are, her green cardigan buttoned wrong. She cries the way she does everything: completely, without apology.

Frank is sitting next to her. He's not crying. Frank doesn't cry. Frank is sitting very straight with his hands on his knees and his jaw set and he looks like a man who is containing something large and unnameable, a man whose son just walked across a stage, and the look on his face is so private and so raw that I have to look away.

Sophie is eating cake. She found it somewhere — the reception isn't supposed to start for another forty minutes — and she's sitting at the end of the bleachers with a paper plate on her knees and a plastic fork and she's eating cake and watching. Just watching. Taking it all in with those gray-blue eyes that don't miss anything, filing it away in whatever vast and meticulous archive she keeps inside her head.

She catches me looking. She raises her fork. It's not a wave. It's an acknowledgment. *I see you. I don't understand you. But I see you.*

I nod back.

---

After the ceremony, outside, the parking lot is chaos. Caps in the air, photos, hugs, someone's car alarm going off, a dad trying to fit a giant teddy bear into the back of a Corolla. The sun is low and warm and the light has that golden late-afternoon quality that makes you think the universe is sentimental, which it isn't, but it's nice to pretend.

Frank finds me near the gym doors. He's in his good shirt — the blue Oxford he wears to weddings and funerals and apparently graduations — and he's standing with his hands at his sides and he looks like he's about to say something important and has no idea how to start.

He puts his hand out.

I look at it. I look at him.

I take it.

His grip is firm and dry and calloused in exactly the places you'd expect from thirty years of fitting pipes, and he shakes once, twice, and then he lets go and puts his hand in his pocket and nods, and that's it. That's the whole thing. A handshake. Man to man. The most my father has ever said with his body.

I think I might die from it.

"Proud of you, bud," he says, and then Diane swoops in and the moment is over, dissolved into her hug and her crying and her cardigan and the smell of the Jean Naté body splash she has worn for as long as I've been alive either time.

---

Marcus finds me later, when the crowd is thinning and the custodians are starting to look meaningfully at the trash cans. He's still in his gown, unzipped, cap pushed back on his head at an angle that shouldn't work but does.

"Callahan," he says.

"Washington."

"We did it."

"We did."

He looks around the parking lot. At the stragglers, the families, the last few graduates posing for photos against the school sign. His Casio catches the light — still three minutes fast, still always three minutes fast, as if Marcus exists slightly ahead of the rest of us.

"GM filed yesterday," he says.

I know. Of course I know. June 1, 2009. Chapter 11. The largest industrial bankruptcy in American history. Thirty-one billion dollars in assets, forty thousand jobs, an entire city's identity hollowed out like a jack-o'-lantern.

"I saw," I say.

He's quiet for a moment. Then he turns to me and his face is doing something I haven't seen before — not suspicion exactly, not fear, but a kind of careful, weighted attention. The way you look at something you're not sure is safe.

"That's three for three," he says.

"Marcus —"

"Virginia. Indiana. GM. Three for three."

"I just —"

"Pay attention. Yeah. You said." He studies me. The gap in his teeth shows when he presses his lips together. "You pay a lot of attention, Nate."

"Is that a bad thing?"

"I don't know yet."

And then, because Marcus is Marcus and because the weight of a moment is something he carries lightly, like everything else, he grins. He pulls his cap off his head and looks at it — cheap blue polyester, gold tassel, Cedar Falls High School Class of 2009.

"Everything starts now, right?" he says, and he throws the cap into the air.

It spins against the sky, blue on blue, catching the last of the light, and for a second it's just a shape suspended in nothing — not rising, not falling, just hanging there in the space between what was and what will be.

"Yeah," I say. "Everything starts now."

He means the future. College, careers, the whole bright sprawling promise of adulthood that every commencement speech tries to name and none of them do. He means beginnings. He means possibility. He means the blank page that Derek talked about, waiting to be written.

I mean: in fifteen months, Lehman Brothers will be a memory. In four years, Edward Snowden will walk into a hotel room in Hong Kong. In eleven years, the world will shut its doors. In seventeen years, the Kessari will come, and the sky will look like something out of a painting no one would ever want to paint, and Cedar Falls will burn, and Marcus — Marcus who wants to build bridges, Marcus who is standing here with his face turned up to the sun and his whole life ahead of him, Marcus who brought me water and a blanket when I was drunk and pathetic on his couch — Marcus will be somewhere I can't follow, doing something I can't prevent, becoming a person I won't get to meet.

Everything starts now.

The cap comes down. Marcus catches it one-handed and laughs and puts it back on crooked and says, "Come on, my grandma wants pictures," and I follow him across the parking lot into the golden wreckage of the afternoon.

Behind us, a custodian picks up a stray program from the ground. The gym doors close. Someone's car starts. The ordinary machinery of ending does its work.

Everything starts now.

I mean something completely different.

Chapter Six: The Bookshelf and the Debt

The first board comes up with a groan that sounds almost human.

Frank works the pry bar under the decking plank and leans his weight into it, and the nails that have held this board down since 1994 give up one at a time — pop, pop, pop — rusted fasteners pulling free of joists that have gone soft with fifteen years of Iowa weather. The board splinters at one end. Frank tosses it onto the pile in the yard without comment.

"Next one," he says.

I get the flat bar under the adjacent plank and start working it loose. The sun is already brutal at nine in the morning, the kind of wet Midwestern heat that sits on your chest like a second shirt. My T-shirt is soaked through at the back and under the arms. Frank is wearing a long-sleeve work shirt buttoned to the wrists because that's what he wears, regardless of season or temperature or common sense.

We've been at this for three days. The back deck is twelve by sixteen, pressure-treated pine over a simple joist frame, and the surface boards have cupped and split and gone gray-green with age. Frank decided in May that it needed replacing. He priced out contractors, did the math, and told Diane he'd do it himself. What he meant was we'd do it together, but he didn't say that part out loud. He just told me to be up by seven on Monday.

So here I am. Up by seven on Monday, and Tuesday, and now Wednesday.

The demolition is the easy part. Ripping out the old deck is just leverage and stubbornness, and Frank has plenty of both. The hard part comes later — checking the ledger board, sistering any joists that have gone punky, laying new decking straight and true. Frank has a library book on deck construction that he's read twice. He has a torpedo level and a chalk line and a speed square that belonged to his father. He does not have experience building decks, but he has the conviction that a man should be able to build a deck, and for Frank Callahan that's close enough.

I already know how to build a deck. I worked a summer for Vogel Construction in a timeline that doesn't exist anymore — framing houses in Waterloo, learning to read a tape measure by getting yelled at every time I cut a board wrong. I can toe-nail a joist and set a post and run a string line in my sleep.

I don't tell Frank any of this.

He shows me how to hold the pry bar at the right angle to get maximum leverage without splitting the wood. I nod. He shows me how to pull nails with the claw hammer, rolling the head against the board to protect the surface. I nod and do it exactly the way he shows me. He tells me to measure twice and cut once, and I say "Got it" like I've never heard this before, like it's wisdom being passed from father to son instead of something every tenth-grader in shop class has on a poster above the band saw.

The restraint costs me nothing. That's what I've learned. Letting someone teach you something you already know — it's not dishonesty. It's just making room.

Frank doesn't talk much while we work. He gives instructions. He points. He makes sounds of approval or disapproval that are nearly identical in tone but distinguishable after eighteen years of exposure. We drink water from a plastic jug that Diane refills and sets on the porch steps. We eat lunch at noon — sandwiches, chips, whatever she's made — sitting on overturned five-gallon buckets in the shade of the garage. We go back to work. We stop at five when the heat has baked itself into the wood and every surface is too hot to touch without gloves.

It's the most time I've spent with my father in either lifetime.

---

By Thursday we've got the old decking stripped and stacked. The joist frame is exposed like ribs, and two of the joists have gone soft near the ledger board where water pooled and sat. Frank prods them with a screwdriver, frowning.

"These need to come out."

"Can we sister them? Run new lumber alongside and bolt through?"

He looks at me. It's a quick look, assessing. I've said something that suggests I know more than a kid who's never built anything.

"Where'd you learn that?" he asks.

"Library." Which is true in the broadest sense. I learned it in a book once, even if I learned it better with a nail gun and a hangover.

Frank considers this. Then he nods, once. "That'd work. We'll need carriage bolts. Half-inch, at least."

We drive to the hardware store in his truck, a 2004 F-150 with a cracked dash and a passenger seat that doesn't adjust anymore. The truck smells like pipe dope and old coffee. Frank drives with one hand on the wheel and the other resting on the window frame, and he doesn't turn on the radio.

At the hardware store he takes his time. He reads the labels on every box of bolts. He picks up lumber and sights down the edge to check for bow and twist, setting aside the ones that don't pass. He asks the kid in the orange apron a question about joist hangers and listens to the answer with the patience of a man who has all day, even though it's ninety-two degrees and we've been working since seven.

In the truck on the way home, stuck at the light on University Avenue, he says: "My old man could build anything."

I wait.

"Patrick Francis Callahan. Pipe fitter, Local 125. Hands like — I don't know. He could do things with his hands that didn't make sense. Fix a furnace, build a cabinet, wire a house. Never read a book about any of it. Just knew."

The light changes. Frank drives.

"He built the kitchen cabinets in the house on Tremont. The house I grew up in. Built them out of oak he got from a guy at the union hall who was tearing down a barn. They were beautiful. My mother said they were the nicest thing in the house, and they were."

I've heard Patrick Callahan's name before. Twice, maybe three times in eighteen years. Frank doesn't talk about his father the way some men talk about their fathers — with either reverence or resentment. He mentions him the way you'd mention a weather event. Something that happened. Something you lived through.

"He drank," Frank says. Not a question, not a confession. A fact, stated in the same tone he'd use to say the man was left-handed.

"Every night. Beer after work, then whiskey after dinner, then more whiskey after my mother went to bed. Functional. Went to work every day, never missed a shift, never got written up. Came home and built things and drank until he fell asleep in his chair."

He pulls into the driveway. Turns off the truck. Doesn't move to get out.

"Died at sixty-one. Liver. They opened him up and the surgeon told my mother there was nothing to do. Cirrhosis so advanced that his liver looked like a piece of — something. I don't remember what she said the surgeon said. Something ugly."

He's looking through the windshield at the garage door, which needs painting.

"I was twenty-three. Your mother and I had just started dating. I went to the funeral and I didn't cry and nobody expected me to because Callahan men don't do that. We just — " He stops. Flexes his hand on the steering wheel. "We just show up. That's what we do. We show up and we work and we don't talk about the rest."

I don't say anything. I know better. There's no response to this that doesn't make it smaller.

Frank opens his door. "Let's get this lumber out of the bed before it gets any hotter."

We carry the boards to the backyard in silence. We measure and cut the sister joists, drill the bolt holes, tighten everything down until the frame is solid. We work until five. We drink water. We don't talk about Patrick Callahan or liver failure or the things men pass to their sons without meaning to.

---

On Saturday I'm in the garage looking for the box of deck screws Frank bought at the hardware store. He said he put them on the shelf by the paint, which describes approximately forty percent of the garage's surface area because Frank's organizational system is based on the principle that he'll remember where he put something, which he never does.

I move a five-gallon bucket of exterior latex. Behind it, two cans of wood stain that have been here so long the labels have yellowed. Behind those, a string of Christmas lights wound around a cardboard tube. Behind that, shoved against the wall and half-hidden by a drop cloth, is a bookshelf.

Pine. Four shelves. About three feet wide, maybe four feet tall. The joints are — not perfect. The shelves sit in dados that were cut a little too deep on the left side, so the whole thing leans slightly, a degree or two off plumb. The surfaces have been sanded smooth by hand; I can see the scratch pattern where the sandpaper went against the grain in a few spots. There's no finish on the wood. It's bare pine, gone golden with age, dusty but not damaged.

I pull it out from the wall. It's lighter than it looks. One of the shelf supports on the back has come loose where a nail worked its way out. Easy fix. Ten minutes with a hammer and some wood glue.

I stand there holding this bookshelf in the garage with sawdust on my arms and sweat running down my neck and I know exactly what this is.

Frank built this when I was twelve. I know because I remember the summer he spent evenings in the garage — Diane told me he was working on something, and I didn't care because I was twelve and didn't care about anything that wasn't a book or a video game. He never gave it to me. I don't know why. Maybe he looked at it and saw the imperfections — the lean, the rough spots, the missing finish — and decided it wasn't good enough. Maybe he meant to give it to me and the moment never came. Maybe he put it in the garage meaning to sand it one more time, apply a coat of polyurethane, make it right, and then a week turned into a month turned into six years.

In the original timeline, this bookshelf stayed in this garage until — I don't know when. I never found it. I never looked.

I carry it inside.

Up the stairs. Through the hall. Into my room. I set it against the wall next to my desk, where the afternoon light hits it. The lean is barely visible when it's loaded. I take the books off my desk — the astrophysics texts I checked out from the library, the emergency management handbook, the dog-eared copy of *Cosmos* I've had since freshman year — and put them on the shelves. I add a few paperbacks from the box in my closet. A Stephen King. A Michael Crichton. The copy of *Hatchet* I must have read fifteen times in middle school.

The bookshelf holds them fine. The lean doesn't matter. The rough spots don't matter. It is a solid, functional piece of furniture built by a man who noticed his son was reading everything and decided to do something about it.

I go back to the garage and find the deck screws where Frank actually left them, on the workbench under a rag.

That evening, after dinner, Frank walks down the hallway toward the bathroom and passes my open door. I'm at my desk reading. He stops.

I don't look up right away. I let him see it — the bookshelf against the wall, the books on the shelves, the thing he built fitting into the room like it was always meant to be there.

"Looks good in there," he says.

Three words. He keeps walking. I hear the bathroom door close.

I wait until I can hear the TV downstairs, the muffled play-by-play of a Twins game. Then I put my face into the pillow and press hard and stay there for a while.

---

Mondays and Wednesdays I go to the library.

The Cedar Falls Public Library is air-conditioned, which in late July makes it the most civilized building in town. Gail Andersen works the reference desk. She's in her fifties, reads two novels a week, and has the particular gift of helping you find what you need without asking why you need it.

I've been coming here since January. First it was emergency management — FEMA publications, disaster response frameworks, the kind of reading that makes sense for a kid about to study the subject in college. Gail helped me navigate the interlibrary loan system to get textbooks that UNI didn't stock. She never asked why an eighteen-year-old was reading *Principles of Emergency Management and Homeland Security* for fun.

Now I'm reading astrophysics.

Not casually. I'm working through published papers with the slow, grinding effort of someone who has the curiosity but not the math. I took AP Physics and got a four on the exam, which means I understand force and motion and energy but I am functionally illiterate when it comes to the kind of mathematics that astrophysicists use the way plumbers use wrenches.

It doesn't matter. I'm not trying to become an astrophysicist. I'm trying to understand enough to be taken seriously by one.

Dr. Judith Chen. I found her name in February, searching the UNI faculty directory. Associate professor of physics. Her published work is mostly in radio astronomy — signal processing, noise reduction, the technical problem of distinguishing a meaningful signal from the background static of the universe. She's published in *The Astrophysical Journal* and *Icarus* and a handful of smaller publications. She co-authored a paper on anomalous signal detection that I've now read four times.

I don't understand all of it. I understand the abstract and the introduction and the discussion section. The methodology is dense with equations I can't follow. But the core argument is clear: Chen believes that current SETI protocols are too narrow, too focused on a specific type of signal, and that a genuine extraterrestrial transmission might look like noise under conventional detection frameworks.

This matters because what's coming in 2031 won't look like what anyone expects.

I have a notebook — spiral-bound, college-ruled, bought at Walmart for eighty-nine cents. In this notebook I am building a script. Not a screenplay, not a sales pitch. A script for a conversation. How do you walk into a professor's office and say something extraordinary without sounding like a lunatic? How do you establish credibility when you're eighteen and your evidence is impossible?

You don't lead with the impossible part. That's what I've figured out. You lead with the questions. You ask about her work. You demonstrate that you've read it, that you understand it, that you take it seriously. You build a relationship based on legitimate academic interest. And then, slowly, over months or years, you introduce the ideas that matter.

It's manipulation. I know that. I'm engineering a relationship with a specific outcome in mind. The fact that the outcome is saving the planet doesn't make the method clean.

I write in the notebook: *Approach window — September/October, first semester. Attend office hours. Ask about the anomalous signal paper. DO NOT mention predictions. DO NOT mention the countdown. Be a curious student, nothing more.*

I underline "nothing more" twice.

Gail comes by the table where I'm working, a stack of returned books in her arms. She glances at the papers spread out in front of me — printouts from JSTOR, a textbook chapter on radio frequency interference, my notebook.

"Branching out?" she says.

"Just interested."

"You know UNI has a physics department. Real professors. They'd probably love a student who reads papers for fun."

"That's the plan."

She smiles and moves on. Gail is good at this — offering exactly one sentence more than necessary, then letting it go.

---

The deck gets finished on a Tuesday.

It takes us two and a half weeks total. The new decking is pressure-treated southern yellow pine, screwed down with coated deck screws every sixteen inches. Frank insisted on a consistent screw pattern — two screws per joist, lined up across the width of the deck so they form clean parallel rows. It took twice as long as random placement. It looks right.

We stand in the backyard and look at it. It's not beautiful the way a contractor's deck would be beautiful. The cuts at the border aren't perfectly tight. One of the stair stringers is a quarter-inch off. But it's square and level and solid, and when Frank steps onto it and bounces on the balls of his feet, testing, it doesn't move.

"Not bad," he says.

Diane brings out lemonade. She stands on the new deck and puts her hand on the railing and says it's wonderful, and Frank says it just needed doing, and Diane catches my eye and smiles in a way that means she knows what this was.

Sophie comes out and looks at it. "It's a deck," she says, with the withering accuracy of a fourteen-year-old, and goes back inside.

---

The evening is warm enough to sit out. Diane and I are on the front porch. Frank is inside watching something. Sophie is in her room. The neighborhood is doing what neighborhoods do on summer evenings — sprinklers, the distant complaint of a lawn mower, kids on bikes at the end of the street.

Diane has a glass of iced tea. I have water. She's in the porch swing, moving it with one foot, and I'm on the steps.

"You're quiet tonight," she says.

"Just tired. The deck."

"You did good work on that. Your father — he won't say it, but he's proud."

I know he is. I know it in a way I didn't know it the first time through, because the first time through I wasn't paying attention. I was looking for grand gestures, for explicit declarations, for a father who would say the words. I missed the bookshelf. I missed all of it.

"Nate."

I look back at her. She's stopped swinging.

"I don't know what happened to you," she says. Carefully, like she's been thinking about how to say this. "But you're different. This last year, since — I don't know when exactly. Since last fall, maybe. You're different."

The light is going. Her face is half in shadow, half in the amber glow from the window behind her.

"Better," she says. "You're better. And I don't just mean your grades or the drinking or any of that. You're — kinder. You notice things. You helped your father build a deck and you didn't complain once, and the Nate from two years ago would have fought me for a week before he even picked up a hammer."

She takes a sip of her tea.

"I'm proud of you. I wanted to say that."

My mouth opens. I can feel it — the whole story, sitting right there, ready to spill. The countdown. The future. The version of me that wasted this, wasted her, wasted Frank, wasted everything. The weight of knowing what I know and carrying it alone.

I close my mouth.

If I told her, she'd believe me. That's the thing. Diane Callahan has a capacity for faith that borders on reckless. She'd listen to the whole story and she'd look at me and she'd decide that her son was telling the truth, because her son doesn't lie to her anymore, and then she'd carry this weight too. It would sit behind her eyes every time she looked at me. Every time she looked at the sky.

I won't do that to her.

"Thanks, Mom."

She reaches over and squeezes my arm. Then she picks up her iced tea and goes back to swinging, one foot pushing off, the rhythm steady and slow, and we sit there together as the streetlights come on.

---

Later. My room. The window is open and the air is thick and still, the kind of July air that doesn't move, that just lies on top of you. Cicadas outside, electric and relentless.

I'm lying on my bed looking at the bookshelf. Frank's bookshelf, with my books on it. *Cosmos* next to *The Stand* next to *Introduction to Emergency Management* next to a three-ring binder of printed astrophysics papers. The shelf leans just slightly to the left. In the dark I can barely see it, but I know it's there — the weight of books pulling it into its imperfection, settling it into place.

In the original version, I spent this summer getting drunk with Derek Holt. We drove to Waterloo and bought cheap beer with his older brother's ID and sat in parking lots and talked about nothing. I didn't build a deck. I didn't read astrophysics. I didn't sit on the porch with my mother and hear her say she was proud of me, because there was nothing to be proud of.

In that version, the shelf stayed in the garage. Year after year after year, behind the paint cans and the Christmas lights, while the pine went gray and the nails worked loose and whatever Frank was trying to say with it went unsaid and unreceived and eventually, I assume, rotted.

That's the thing about the people who love you quietly. You have to be paying attention. You have to know the language. And by the time most people learn it, the speakers are gone.

I reach over and turn off the lamp. The room goes dark. The cicadas keep going. Through the floor I can hear the murmur of the television and the low, settling sounds of a house with people in it — Frank locking the front door, Diane running water in the kitchen, Sophie's music bleeding through her bedroom wall.

Tomorrow I'll go to the library. I'll keep building the case, the careful architecture of questions and knowledge that will let me walk into Judith Chen's office in September and be taken seriously. I'll keep preparing for a conversation that has to go right because I don't know how many chances I get and I've already wasted more than I deserved.

Tomorrow I start building something else.

But tonight, the shelf holds.

---

Chapter Seven: The Scientist and the Prophet

My roommate's name is Todd and he's from Dubuque and he has a poster of a woman in a bikini draped across the hood of a Camaro and he thinks I'm pre-law.

"You've got that vibe," he tells me, three days into fall semester. He's arranging his desk with the care of a man who will never use it. "Like, intense. Organized. You've got a whole system."

He's pointing at my bookshelf. Pine, hand-sanded, slightly leaning to the left. My father built it with me over the summer and it followed me here in the back of his truck along with two garbage bags of clothes and a box of books that made him raise an eyebrow. *Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics*. *Fundamentals of Emergency Management*. *The Selfish Gene*. A pocket guide to edible plants of the upper Midwest that I'd found at a garage sale and couldn't explain even to myself.

"Pre-law guys always have a system," Todd says.

"I'm emergency management," I say.

"Right." He nods like I've confirmed his theory. "So, like, FEMA?"

"Something like that."

"Cool, cool." Todd turns back to his Camaro poster, adjusting the angle with a level of precision I wish he'd apply to his hygiene. He will spend the semester playing *Call of Duty* until three in the morning while I lie in the dark with my eyes open doing math I can't show anyone. We will coexist the way a monk coexists with a golden retriever—mutual bafflement, occasional shared meals, no genuine conflict.

The point is, I'm at college now. I'm eighteen. And every single person around me is eighteen in the way that eighteen is supposed to be—terrified and electric and convinced that the world is a door about to open. They're picking majors like lottery numbers. They're falling in love in the dining hall over trays of institutional pasta. They're becoming themselves for the first time.

I became myself already. Twelve years ago, or twenty minutes ago, depending on how you count.

---

The University of Northern Iowa is not where you go when you want to study astrophysics. It's where you go when you want to study education, or business, or how to coach high school wrestling. The physics department occupies the third floor of a building that smells like chalk dust and resignation, and most of the faculty have made peace with teaching survey courses to students who need the credits.

But there's a lecture series. Thursdays at four, in a basement seminar room in McCollum Science Hall. "Advanced Topics in Observational Astrophysics." No credit offered. No attendance taken. I find it in the back of the course catalog like a message in a bottle.

October 8th, 2009. The topic is "Anomalous Signal Detection in the Microwave Background." The room holds forty. Twelve people show up, and three of them are clearly lost. One guy has a campus map unfolded across his lap.

I take a seat in the back row and wait.

She comes in four minutes late carrying a travel mug, a laptop trailing its charger cord like a tail, and a stack of papers held together with—I'm not making this up—a large binder clip that is also, somehow, holding her hair in a bun. She's five-one. She's wearing a corduroy blazer that has seen better decades over a Ramones t-shirt, and her Converse sneakers have a stripe of duct tape across the left toe. Her glasses are thick-framed and smudged in a way that suggests she sees the world through a permanent soft filter and has chosen not to care.

Dr. Judith Chen sets her things down on the podium, takes a sip from her travel mug, and squints at us like she's counting.

"Twelve," she says. "That's actually up from last month. Either I'm getting better or the vending machine in the lobby finally broke and you're all trapped." She pauses. "It broke, didn't it."

Nobody answers. She doesn't seem to need us to.

"Right. Anomalous signals." She opens her laptop. A slide appears on the projection screen—dense, beautiful, meaningless to anyone who hasn't spent the summer reading everything this woman has ever published. Which I have. "Let's talk about how the universe whispers and why we keep mistaking it for noise."

For the next fifty minutes I don't move. I barely breathe. I have read her papers—the published ones, the conference proceedings, the one co-authored working paper that got posted to an MIT server and then quietly taken down. I've read them the way a detective reads case files: looking for what's there, but also for the gaps. The places where she stops just short of saying what she's actually thinking.

And here she is. Not the careful, hedged version of herself that appears in journals. The real one. She talks with her hands. She draws diagrams in the air that somehow make more sense than the ones on the screen. She starts sentences with "Technically" and then abandons technical language entirely in favor of analogies that shouldn't work but do. "Think of the cosmic microwave background like a photograph of the universe when it was three hundred eighty thousand years old," she says. "Except the photograph is also a song. And someone might have left a voicemail on top of it."

Two of the lost students leave. The guy with the campus map stays. I think he's fallen asleep.

When she gets to spectral analysis of anomalous signals in the microwave background, she pulls up a frequency chart and I see it—the gap. The range she doesn't mention. The one I know she's been investigating privately because the math in her published papers doesn't make sense unless she's also looking at something in the 90 to 115 gigahertz window that she hasn't told anyone about.

She wraps up. The remaining audience shuffles toward the door. I sit in the back row with my notebook open to a page of questions I've been refining for three months, ever since Gail at the Cedar Falls Public Library helped me request her early papers through interlibrary loan.

The script in my notebook says: *Start with the published work. Ask about methodology. Let her talk. Don't be weird. Don't be weird. Don't be weird.*

I walk down to the podium where she's unplugging her laptop.

"Dr. Chen?"

She glances up. The look she gives me is the one every professor gives an approaching student at the end of a lecture—half patience, half preemptive exhaustion.

"If you're looking for Intro to Astronomy, that's Tuesdays and Thursdays in—"

"I'm not. I wanted to ask about the spectral analysis framework you presented. Specifically about the filtering protocols for the 90 to 115 gigahertz range."

She stops unplugging her laptop. Her hands go still, which I will learn is the equivalent of another person's jaw dropping. Judith Chen's body is always in motion—hands, eyes, the slight rocking from one sneaker to the other—except when something has her full attention. Then she goes absolutely still, like a hawk that's just spotted movement in the grass.

"What about it," she says. Not a question. A door opening one inch.

"Your filtering protocols in the published methodology would strip a legitimate anomalous signal in that range. You'd lose it in the noise reduction. Unless you're running a parallel analysis with different parameters." I swallow. My script is already on fire. "Which you'd only do if you had a reason to think something was there."

The stillness holds for three seconds. Four. She pushes her glasses up her nose with one finger, smudging them further.

"Where did you read about that?"

"I didn't read about it. I thought about it."

She stares at me. I am eighteen years old in a UNI hoodie and jeans and I am telling a physicist with a PhD from Caltech that I reverse-engineered her private research from the gaps in her public work, and she has every reason to dismiss me as a crank or a plagiarist or just a very strange freshman.

"What's your name?" she says.

"Nate Callahan."

"What's your major, Callahan?"

"Emergency management."

She blinks. Something that might be amusement crosses her face, but she kills it before it arrives.

"Come to my office hours," she says. "Wednesdays. Two to four. Bring that notebook."

---

Her office is on the third floor of McCollum, at the end of a hallway that narrows like it's trying to discourage visitors. The door has a Far Side cartoon taped to it—the one with the kid pushing on the door of the Midvale School for the Gifted—and a handwritten index card that says CHEN / OFFICE HOURS WED 2-4 / ALSO BY APPT / ALSO BY AMBUSH IF YOUR QUESTION IS GOOD ENOUGH.

The office itself is small and catastrophic. Papers on every surface. Three whiteboards, all full. A desk somewhere under the geology of accumulated work. And on the one clear chair—the one obviously reserved for visitors—an enormously fat orange tabby cat who looks at me when I walk in with the expression of a bouncer who's already decided I'm not getting past the rope.

"That's Boltzmann," Chen says from behind a monitor. "He doesn't move. Don't take it personally. He doesn't move for anyone. I'm eighty percent sure he's alive. Sit wherever you can find purchase."

I move Boltzmann gently to one side. He permits this the way a continent permits tectonic drift—slowly, with deep structural resistance. I sit. He resettles himself across my lap with a sigh that seems to come from his entire body.

"He likes you," Chen says, not looking up. "He usually bites."

"Should I be flattered?"

"You should be wary. He bites the people he likes too, just later." She finally looks at me over her glasses. "So. Callahan. Tell me about the 90 to 115 range."

This is a test. I understand that. What I don't understand yet—but will learn over the following months—is that everything with Chen is a test. Not in the adversarial sense. In the diagnostic sense. She's always measuring, always calibrating. She wants to know what you know, but more importantly, she wants to know the shape of what you don't know, because the shape of someone's ignorance tells you more than the shape of their knowledge.

I tell her what I've worked out. The filtering protocols. The math that doesn't balance unless there's an unacknowledged variable. The frequency range where, if you're looking, there might be something worth finding.

She listens without interrupting, which I'll learn is rare. When I finish, she picks up a stick of celery from a Tupperware container on her desk—peanut butter, already applied—and takes a bite.

"Some of that's right," she says. "Some of it's wrong in interesting ways. And one piece of it is something I haven't told anyone outside of two people at MIT and a woman at JPL who owes me a favor." She chews. "Where did you say you were from?"

"Cedar Falls."

"And you're studying emergency management."

"Yes."

"And you figured this out at—"

"The public library. Mostly."

She sets down the celery. Picks up a mandarin orange. Begins peeling it with surgical precision, the rind coming off in one continuous spiral.

"Technically," she says, "what you've just described is either very sophisticated guesswork or something I need to take seriously. I haven't decided which. Come back next Wednesday."

---

I come back next Wednesday. And the Wednesday after that. And every Wednesday for the rest of the semester and into the spring.

Here's what I learn about Judith Chen:

She eats the same lunch every day. Peanut butter on celery, black coffee from a thermos she refills from a pot that I'm pretty sure has been brewing since the Clinton administration, and one mandarin orange, peeled in a single strip. If the strip breaks, she is quietly irritated for the rest of the afternoon.

She keeps a spreadsheet on her laptop, visible once when she tilted the screen to show me a data set, that is titled SUNSET_RANKINGS.xlsx. It has columns for date, location, cloud coverage, color spectrum, and an overall score on a ten-point scale. There are over six hundred entries.

She does not do small talk. The first time I try—"How was your weekend?"—she looks at me like I've asked her to explain the concept of weekends from first principles. "Callahan. I have forty-five minutes before a department meeting that will leach the will to live from my bones. Do you want to talk about your weekend or do you want to talk about signal attenuation?"

She calls every student by last name. I never hear her use a first name. I'm not sure she knows first names exist.

She speaks in paragraphs. Dense, self-correcting, full of parenthetical detours that somehow arrive back at the original point with more precision than the direct route would have offered. She starts sentences with "Actually" and "No, but—" and "The thing people miss is—" She once described a gravitational lens as "like if your grandmother's reading glasses worked on spacetime" and then got annoyed when I laughed because she wasn't joking.

She doesn't laugh often. When she does, it's a sudden surprised bark, like she's been ambushed by the humor and hasn't had time to prepare a more dignified response.

She was denied a major grant at thirty-nine. I learn this not because she tells me but because I find the rejection letter pinned to her corkboard under a Far Side cartoon. The letter is polite in the way that professional devastation always is. She caught me looking at it once and said, "That's my North Star, Callahan. Every time I think about quitting, I read that letter and it makes me so angry I do another year out of spite."

She is, in the way that matters most, alone. Not lonely—I don't think she has the capacity for loneliness, or maybe she just doesn't have the time. But alone in the way that people are when the thing they care about most is something no one else can see yet. She's spent twenty years listening to a frequency that the rest of the scientific community considers background noise, and she is right, and no one knows it, and she keeps showing up anyway.

I recognize the condition. I just can't tell her why.

---

The weeks compress. October becomes December becomes February. Cedar Falls freezes solid and stays that way, the campus becoming a wind tunnel of black ice and students in North Face jackets moving between buildings with the grim determination of arctic explorers. I go home for Thanksgiving and Christmas and sit at the table with my family and practice being the person they think I am.

Frank asks about my classes. I tell him about the ones that don't matter. Diane asks if I'm eating well. I lie with the conviction of a man who has been eating dining hall chicken patties for three months. Sophie watches me from across the table with those cataloging eyes and says nothing, which is worse.

Marcus calls from Ames on a Tuesday night in January.

"You're doing something," he says. No preamble. Marcus has never needed a preamble.

"I'm studying."

"Yeah, I know you're studying. You're also doing something else. You've got that tone."

"What tone."

"The one where you're about to do something stupid and you've already decided it's smart."

He's not wrong. He's almost never wrong about me, which is one of the things that makes Marcus both essential and terrifying.

"I'm just taking classes, man. Making friends. Having the college experience."

"You don't have friends. You have a roommate who thinks you're pre-law and a physics professor you visit every week even though you're an emergency management major. Don't look surprised—your mom told my mom."

"That's not—she's helping me with—"

"You're not going to finish that sentence, are you."

"Probably not."

Silence. Then: "Three for three, Nate."

The predictions. He's still counting. Virginia and Indiana going blue. GM filing for bankruptcy. I'd almost forgotten he was keeping score, which was stupid, because Marcus never stops keeping score.

"That doesn't mean anything," I say.

"Maybe not. But if you hit a fourth one, I'm driving to Cedar Falls and you're going to sit in a room with me until you explain how a kid from Iowa who got C's in geography can predict national events."

"I got a B minus in geography."

"Goodnight, Nate."

He hangs up. I sit on my dorm bed with the phone in my hand and Boltzmann's fur on my jeans—the cat has started shedding on me with such consistency that I suspect it's intentional—and I think about what it means to be known by someone who doesn't know anything.

---

March. The semester tilting toward spring. The ice retreating from campus in stages, like an occupying army that's lost its supply lines. I've been going to Chen's office for five months. She's tested me. Probed the edges of what I know. Asked questions designed to trip up someone who's memorized answers without understanding them, and I've passed because I do understand—not because I'm a genius but because I've had the benefit of knowing which questions to ask.

But there's a limit to how long I can do this. Every week I sit in her office, every week Boltzmann sprawls across my lap like a fur-covered sandbag, every week she and I talk about anomalous signals and spectral analysis and the cosmic microwave background, and every week I get a little closer to the edge of what I can explain without explaining everything. The knowledge I have doesn't come from libraries. It comes from a future that hasn't happened yet, where a woman named Judith Chen detected something that changed everything, and I can't tell her that, so I've been feeding her conclusions disguised as questions, and she's too smart not to notice the shape of what I'm doing.

She notices on a Wednesday in March. We're looking at a data set on her second whiteboard—the one she's started calling "ours"—and I make a comment about signal periodicity that I shouldn't be able to make. Not from the data in front of us. Not from anything published. Only from knowing what the signal will eventually look like when it's found.

She puts down her dry-erase marker. She turns to face me. Boltzmann, sensing a change in atmospheric pressure, opens one eye.

"Callahan."

"Yeah."

"I've been very patient."

"I know."

"I've been patient because you're smart and because you ask good questions and because, frankly, I don't get a lot of people in this office who can follow me past the second whiteboard." She crosses her arms. The corduroy blazer bunches at her shoulders. Today's shirt is The Clash. "But you don't ask questions like a student. You ask questions like someone who already knows the answers and is trying to lead me to them without me noticing."

"I—"

"I noticed."

I look at my hands. Boltzmann has gone back to sleep.

"So here's what I need from you," she says. "Either you tell me where this is actually coming from, or these office hours end. I don't have the career capital to waste time on someone who might be running a very sophisticated prank."

This is the moment. I've been preparing for it since September. Since before September—since the library, since Gail helped me track down Chen's early papers, since I sat at a carrel with a spiral notebook and mapped out exactly how this conversation would go.

It's not going the way I mapped it. It never does.

"It's not a prank," I say.

"Then what is it?"

I take a breath. In my notebook, the script says: *Provide a specific, verifiable prediction. Make it narrow enough that luck can't explain it. Make it far enough in the future that you have time to prepare for the consequences. Don't tell her more than she needs. Don't tell her less than she deserves.*

"Within eighteen months," I say. "An anomalous signal will be detected in the microwave background. It won't match any known source. It won't be instrument error. It will be in a frequency range that almost no one is monitoring because the consensus says there's nothing there."

Chen doesn't move. Her hands are still.

"I can give you the coordinates," I say.

"You can give me the coordinates."

"Right ascension and declination. The exact patch of sky."

The office is very quiet. The building's heating system clicks and sighs. Somewhere down the hall, a door closes.

"Callahan," she says carefully. "Do you understand what you're telling me?"

"Yes."

"You're telling me that you know—with some degree of certainty—where and when an unprecedented astronomical signal will appear."

"Yes."

"And you're an eighteen-year-old emergency management major from Cedar Falls, Iowa."

"Also yes."

She takes off her glasses. Rubs her eyes with thumb and forefinger. Puts the glasses back on. They're smudgier than before.

"I think," she says slowly, "that you're either experiencing a psychotic break, in which case I have a responsibility to connect you with student health services, or you're telling me something important, in which case I have a responsibility to listen. I'm not able to determine which."

"I know."

"That's not reassuring."

"I know that too."

She goes to her desk. Opens a drawer. Takes out a fresh yellow legal pad—the only pristine object in the entire office—and uncaps a pen.

"Give me the coordinates."

I give them to her. She writes them down in small, precise handwriting. She writes the date range. She writes the frequency range. She caps the pen and looks at what she's written.

"If this signal appears," she says, "where you say it will, when you say it will, I'm going to need a real explanation. Not the version you've been giving me. The real one."

"If it appears," I say, "you'll get one."

She studies me for a long moment. I don't know what she sees. An eighteen-year-old kid. A con artist. A lunatic. A person who is telling the truth about something impossible. Some days I don't know which of those I am either.

"Wednesday," she says. "Two o'clock. Bring the notebook."

"Okay."

"And Callahan?"

"Yeah?"

"If you're wasting my time, I will make your academic career a footnote in the history of regret."

"Understood."

"Good. Boltzmann, get off his lap. He's leaving."

Boltzmann does not move. I extract myself from under him like a man escaping a soft, purring landslide, and I walk out of her office into the hallway that smells like chalk dust and old radiators, and my hands are shaking and my mouth is dry and I've just bet everything on a woman I've known for five months.

Not everything. Not yet. But the first real piece of it. The first time I've given someone a thread they could pull. If she pulls it the wrong way—reports me, tells the department, decides I'm unstable—the whole strategy falls apart. Two years of careful, invisible preparation, gone.

But the strategy doesn't work without her. That's the thing I realized at the library over the summer, reading her papers for the fourth and fifth time. The signal is coming whether I tell anyone or not. The question is whether anyone is listening in the right place when it arrives. And the only person brilliant enough and stubborn enough and ignored enough to be looking where no one else is looking—the only person already tuned to the right frequency—is a five-foot-one physicist in duct-taped sneakers who ranks sunsets on a spreadsheet.

You don't save the world with a plan. You save it with people. And you can't get the people without the risk.

Anyway.

---

Back in my dorm. Todd is at a party. The room is quiet except for the radiator, which makes a sound like a small animal trapped in the wall. My books are on the shelf my father built—the slight leftward lean is worse now, or I'm imagining it, or I've been staring at it too long.

I open my journal. Not the spiral notebook with the script—the other one. The one I don't show anyone. The one where I try to say what I actually mean instead of what's useful.

I write the date. I write Chen's name, and the coordinates I gave her, and the way her hands went still.

Then I write this:

*Here's the problem. The man who can see what's coming has to use people. Has to manipulate timelines, position assets, exploit foreknowledge. Has to be calculating and strategic and willing to treat human beings as pieces on a board, including the ones he loves, because the stakes are literally everything.*

*And the man who deserves to survive what's coming—the man who would be worth saving—wouldn't do any of that. Would be honest. Would be present. Would be the son Frank and Diane actually raised instead of the one who's performing the role while running a secret operation out of a physics professor's office.*

*The man who can save the world and the man who deserves to be saved cannot be the same person.*

*I have to be both.*

I stare at the page. The radiator clicks. Outside, someone crosses the quad, laughing at something I can't hear.

*Anyway. The point is—*

The pen hovers. I can feel the sentence sitting there, just past the edge of what I know how to say. The thing that connects the two halves. The answer to a question I've been asking since I woke up seventeen years old in a bed that wasn't mine with a future that isn't mine and a past that no one else remembers.

I don't write it. I don't know it yet.

I close the journal. I put it on the shelf between *Atmospheric Chemistry* and the pocket guide to edible plants. I turn off the desk lamp. I lie down in the dark and listen to the building settle around me—the radiator, the pipes, the footsteps of eighteen-year-olds living lives that make sense—and I wait for the thing I can't name to become the thing I can.

Outside, spring is coming. You can't see it yet but it's there, the way a signal is there before anyone detects it—present in the data, hidden by noise, waiting for someone stubborn enough to look in the right place at the right time and believe what they find.

Eighteen months.

The countdown is running.
