Chapter Three: The Friend He Didn't Deserve

The ball hits the rim and bounces off at an angle that physics shouldn't allow, and Marcus snags it out of the air with one hand like he's pulling a book off a shelf.

"That's four," he says.

"I'm aware."

"Just making sure. You seem confused out here."

The court behind Cedar Falls High is cracked asphalt with faded lines and a chain-link net that sounds like a zipper when the ball drops through clean. It's the second week of October, and the air has that particular Iowa bite — not cold yet, not really, but reminding you it could be. My lungs feel tight in a way that has nothing to do with fitness.

Here's what I didn't anticipate about being seventeen again: the body. Not in the abstract sense of youth or flexibility or whatever people romanticize. I mean the specific, practical reality that my legs are three inches shorter than they were six weeks ago, my center of gravity has shifted, and my hands — these smooth, uncalloused, weirdly soft hands — keep misjudging distances by a quarter inch. My brain says *go left, plant, elevate* and my body says *sure thing, boss* and then does something that looks like a marionette with tangled strings.

Marcus drives past me on the right side because I overcorrected left, lays it in off the backboard with that effortless wrist-flick he's had since we were fourteen, and doesn't even bother counting this one out loud.

"Five."

Okay, he bothers.

"My shoes are wrong," I say.

Marcus looks down at my feet. Old Nikes, the pair Diane bought at the start of the school year. "Your shoes."

"The traction's off."

"The traction." He tucks the ball under his arm. The silver Casio catches what's left of the afternoon light. "Nate, you're playing like you've never had knees before. What's going on?"

And that's Marcus. He doesn't dress it up, doesn't pad it with qualifiers. You're playing like you've never had knees before. A perfect diagnostic.

"I think I pulled something in gym on Wednesday," I say, because I've learned over the past three weeks that I need a library of small lies, preloaded and sorted by category. Physical discrepancies get filed under minor injury. Knowledge slips get filed under "read it somewhere." Emotional leakage gets filed under bad sleep.

Marcus bounces me the ball. "So quit standing at the three-point line trying to be Reggie Miller and take it inside."

I take it inside. He blocks me so thoroughly that the ball ends up on the grass twenty feet away, and a freshman walking past flinches.

"That's not inside," Marcus says. "That's you dribbling into my chest."

"I was establishing position."

"You were establishing that you're five-eleven."

He retrieves the ball from the grass. When he comes back, he's got that look — the one where his mouth is doing something complicated, fighting between genuine concern and the pleasure of roasting me. It's a look I forgot about. You don't remember the specific expressions. You think you do, but what you actually remember is a composite, a sketch. And then you see the real thing and it hits you that memory is just a bad photocopy of a bad photocopy, and the original was so much better.

We play to eleven. He wins eleven to six, which is generous, because at least two of my baskets were him letting up. I know because I watched his feet — he had the position to contest and chose not to, and Marcus Wade does not choose not to contest unless he's managing your feelings.

We sit on the low brick wall by the parking lot, passing a bottle of water back and forth. His Nike basketball shoes are scuffed to gray at the toes. There's a callus on his right index finger from the way he holds his pencil — too tight, always too tight, pressing the graphite like it owes him money.

"So I've been thinking about the engineering thing," he says.

"Yeah?"

"My mom thinks I should do electrical. Better money, she says. And my uncle does electrical in Waterloo, so I'd have the hookup."

"But?"

He pulls at the neck of his t-shirt, letting air in. "But I keep thinking about structural. Like, bridges specifically."

"Bridges."

"I know how it sounds."

"It sounds like you want to build bridges, Marcus. That's not exactly a controversial ambition."

He looks out across the parking lot, where a woman is loading groceries into a minivan at the Hy-Vee across the street. "It's more like — okay, you know the I-35W bridge? The one that collapsed in Minneapolis last year?"

"Yeah." Thirteen people dead. I remember it differently than he does — I remember reading about it at thirty, in a Wikipedia rabbit hole at two in the morning, clicking from bridge collapses to structural engineering failures to the Hyatt Regency walkway collapse to a forum post about how gusset plates work. I remember knowing it was exactly the kind of thing Marcus would have cared about. And then closing the laptop and not thinking about Marcus for another six months.

"Thirteen people," Marcus says. "And they figured out afterward it was this one design flaw — the gusset plates were too thin. Like, *someone did the math wrong*. Or someone did the math right and someone else said it costs too much. Anyway. I keep thinking about that. How a bridge is this promise, right? You drive onto it, and the promise is that the other side exists and you're going to get there. And when the bridge is good, nobody even thinks about it. It's invisible. It just connects things."

He says it plainly, without pretension, the way Marcus says everything. They connect things. Three words, and they land in me like a stone in still water.

I know what Marcus becomes. I know it because between the ages of twenty-five and thirty-two I looked him up approximately once a year, always from a new throwaway account, always late at night, always sober enough to be ashamed and not sober enough to stop. I watched his career through the tiny window of a LinkedIn profile: B.S. in Mechanical Engineering from Iowa State, then John Deere in Waterloo. Not bridges. Not structural. Mechanical. Gears and drivetrains and hydraulic systems for combines. Good work. Real work. But not the thing he's sitting here telling me about with that look on his face, the one where his eyes go slightly unfocused because he's seeing something that doesn't exist yet.

Did he change his mind, or did someone change it for him? Did the money argument win? Did his uncle's hookup in electrical pull him sideways, and then mechanical was the compromise, and by the time he graduated he'd forgotten he ever said the word *bridges* on a brick wall in October?

I don't know. I don't know because by the time Marcus was making those decisions, I'd already started the long process of becoming someone he couldn't trust.

"You should do structural," I say.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. You'd be good at it. You think about systems — how pieces hold each other up. That's the whole job."

Marcus looks at me. The gap between his front teeth makes every smile look a little conspiratorial, like you're both in on something. "Since when do you have opinions about engineering?"

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain bullshit, is what you contain."

I laugh, and it surprises me, because it's real. Not performed, not a deflection. Just a laugh, startled out of me by my best friend being exactly himself.

The point is — and here's where it gets complicated — I know what I owe him. Not the twelve hundred dollars, though yes, that too. What I owe him is the basic dignity of not being a project. Not being the friend I'm fixing. Marcus Wade is not my redemption arc. He's a seventeen-year-old kid who likes bridges and beats me at basketball and calls me on my bullshit, and the thing I owe him is to sit here on this wall and be an actual person.

I'm getting better at that. Some days.

---

The problem is that guilt makes you generous, and generosity without honesty is just manipulation wearing a nicer shirt.

It starts small. Tuesday, I grab an extra slice of pizza at lunch and slide it onto Marcus's tray. Wednesday, I cover his Gatorade at the vending machine. Thursday, I offer him Frank's truck for the weekend because he mentioned needing to pick up a desk from his aunt's place in Waverly.

"I haven't even asked yet," he says, when I bring up the truck.

"I know. I'm offering."

"Your dad's truck. The F-150."

"He won't mind." This is probably true. Frank is the kind of man who'd lend you his truck, his tools, and his Saturday without being asked, as long as you brought it back with the same amount of gas. But Marcus knows I've never once offered before he asked. In the original timeline, Marcus asked to borrow the truck and I said yes and then complained about it afterward to Jake Petersen, because that's who I was — someone who did the right thing and then poisoned it with resentment.

Marcus leans back in his cafeteria chair, balancing on two legs the way that drives every teacher insane. He studies me with the same focus he gives word problems.

"Did you join a cult?"

"What?"

"Like a kindness cult. A generosity seminar. Some kind of Tony Robbins thing."

"I don't know who Tony Robbins is."

"Liar. Everyone knows who Tony Robbins is. He's the giant teeth guy." Marcus sets the chair down with a thunk. "Seriously, man. You bought me lunch three days in a row. You offered me your dad's truck before I even opened my mouth. Last week you helped Cody with his lab report, and Cody literally called your taste in music 'what depression sounds like.'"

"Cody's not wrong about the music."

"Nate." He waits until I look at him. Marcus has this way of going still when something matters — everything loose and easy about him just firms up for a second, and you realize the looseness is a choice, not a default. "You're being a tryhard."

The word lands. Not because it's cruel — Marcus doesn't do cruel — but because it's precise. A tryhard. Someone performing effort instead of just existing. The basketball equivalent of the guy who yells "nice shot!" after every single basket until you want to strangle him.

"Maybe I'm just being a better friend," I say.

"Or maybe you feel bad about something and you're overcorrecting." He picks up his Gatorade — the one I bought — and takes a drink. "I don't need you to buy me stuff. I need you to be normal."

*Normal.* The word is a door I can't walk through, because normal for seventeen-year-old Nate Callahan was careless and a little selfish and took Marcus for granted in all the small ways that add up. Normal was borrowing Marcus's AP History notes and not returning them. Normal was making plans and canceling when something better came along. Normal was the slow, invisible accounting that put me twelve hundred dollars in debt to my best friend and let me tell myself it didn't matter because Marcus never brought it up.

I don't want to be that normal. But Marcus is right that what I'm doing instead is its own kind of lie.

"Okay," I say. "No more free lunch."

"I didn't say that. The pizza was good." He grins, and the gap tooth makes it look like a dare. "I'm just saying, whatever you're going through? You can just tell me. You don't have to, like, purchase my loyalty."

I almost tell him. Right there in the cafeteria with Jake Petersen arguing about Xbox games two seats down and Cody dropping a fry into his own backpack without noticing. I almost say: *I'm from the future. I know how this friendship ends. I know it's my fault. And I'm trying so hard to be different that I've turned into someone even more fake.*

Instead I say, "I think I'm just stressed about college apps."

Marcus nods, accepting this, because it's the kind of normal-sized lie that fits in the space between us. And I hate myself for it — not dramatically, not in the cinematic way, but in the tired way, the way you hate yourself at the end of a long day when you had a hundred chances to be honest and took none of them.

Anyway.

---

The group project is for Mr. Danaher's AP History class, which means it's going to be thorough, exacting, and graded on a rubric that Danaher probably workshopped in his sleep. Four students per group. The topic is the Election of 1860, and I get paired with Marcus, a girl named Erin Tsao who takes notes in three colors of pen, and Derek Holt.

Derek Holt.

I know Derek the way you know a weather pattern — not from direct experience but from tracking its effects. In the original timeline, we weren't close in high school. He was a year ahead, technically, but took some junior-level APs because he'd transferred from a private school in Des Moines. I knew him later. I knew people like him later. I eventually became people like him later.

He's seventeen, sandy blond hair that falls in a way that suggests either perfect genetics or a twenty-dollar pomade, and he has blue eyes that pay attention to everything while appearing to pay attention to nothing. His father is some kind of consultant — the vague kind, the kind where you can never quite pin down what they consult about — and Derek wears a Rolex that he claims is a graduation gift from his grandfather. It is not a graduation gift. He is not yet graduated. His father gave it to him because Derek Holt's father believes in projecting success as a prerequisite for achieving it.

None of this is sinister on its own. He's a good-looking kid with a nice watch who's smarter than most of the people in the room. The problem is that I recognize him.

"So the way I see it," Derek says, five minutes into our first group meeting in the library, "we can divide this four ways, but that's inefficient. Erin, you're the best researcher. Marcus, you're good at the visual stuff — the poster, the layout. Nate, you're the writer. And I'll handle the presentation."

Erin looks up from her three-color notes. "What does 'handle the presentation' mean?"

"I'll do the talking. The actual in-class delivery. I'm good at it, and it's worth forty percent of the grade."

He says this without arrogance. That's the trick. He says it the way you'd say *I'll drive, I know the route* — as a practical observation, not a boast. And he's right: he is good at it. Derek Holt can stand in front of a classroom and make eye contact with every person individually and modulate his voice so the teacher leans forward instead of checking the clock.

But what he's actually done, in thirty seconds of friendly delegation, is assign himself the most visible job while distributing the most labor-intensive work among the three of us. He'll spend two hours rehearsing. Erin will spend eight hours in research. Marcus will spend five on layout. I'll spend six writing. And when Mr. Danaher says "excellent work," Derek will nod graciously, as if absorbing praise on behalf of the team, when what he's really absorbing is the credit.

I watch Marcus watching Derek, and I can see Marcus clocking it too — the slight narrowing, the way his fingers pause on the edge of the table. Marcus sees it. He just doesn't think it's worth fighting about, because Marcus picks his battles and this isn't one.

"Works for me," Marcus says.

"I can research and help with the writing," Erin says, because Erin Tsao doesn't let a rubric go unmaximized.

"Perfect." Derek smiles. It's a good smile. Warm, inclusive, the kind that makes you feel like the plan was collaborative even though it was dictated. "Let's meet Thursday to sync up."

After the meeting, walking down the hall, Marcus says, "He's smooth."

"Yeah."

"Like a politician."

"That's not a compliment."

"Wasn't meant to be." Marcus shifts his backpack higher on his shoulder. "But he's not wrong that he's the best presenter. So whatever."

This is the thing about Derek Holt. He's not wrong. He's almost never wrong in the factual, strategic sense. His plans are efficient. His instincts about people are accurate. His ability to read a room and position himself optimally is, honestly, a kind of genius. What he lacks is the part of the brain that asks whether optimization is the same as decency.

I know this because I lacked it too. For years. My version was messier — less polished, more chaotic — but the operating system was the same. Identify what you want. Map the most efficient path. Treat people as variables in the equation. And if someone gets a raw deal, well, that's not malice. That's just math.

Derek Holt is what my intelligence looked like before conscience moved in and started rearranging the furniture. And watching him feels like reading your own diary from an age you'd rather forget.

---

Election night.

Marcus's house is a single-story ranch on Tremont Street, pale yellow siding, a basketball hoop in the driveway with a net that's been replaced so many times the rim has grooves. His mother, Mrs. Wade — Gloria — has set up the living room like it's the Super Bowl. Two bags of Doritos, a veggie tray that nobody touches, a two-liter of Coke, and a plate of brownies cut into uneven squares because Darnell, Marcus's little brother, "helped."

The TV is a big old Sony, not a flat screen — flat screens exist in 2008 but they haven't reached the Wade living room yet. It sits in an entertainment center made of pressboard and determination, and right now it's tuned to CNN, where Wolf Blitzer is doing that thing he does where every sentence sounds like the world might be ending but also might be fine.

"Sit down, Nate," Gloria says. She's got Marcus's face, or he's got hers — the same wide-set eyes, the same mouth that defaults to almost-smiling. She's wearing an Obama t-shirt that she ordered online and that arrived two sizes too big, so she's got it tucked and knotted at her hip like it's 1995. "Have you eaten?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Have you eaten enough?"

This is not a question with a correct answer, so I say, "The brownies look amazing."

"Darnell made them."

Darnell, who is eleven and already taller than some eighth graders, appears from the kitchen with chocolate on his chin. "I stirred," he clarifies.

"Stirring is the most important part," I say.

"It's really not," Marcus says from the couch, where he's already set up with his laptop open to FiveThirtyEight, which in 2008 is still the blog of a guy named Nate Silver who everybody thinks is either a genius or a crackpot.

"Nate Silver says ninety-eight percent chance Obama wins," Marcus says.

"Nate Silver is correct," I say, before I can stop myself.

Marcus looks at me. "You've been saying that for weeks."

"Because it's been true for weeks."

"Nobody's that sure about an election."

"Nate Silver is."

"The other Nate." Marcus tilts his head. "You've been weird about this. Like, weirdly confident. You told me Virginia would go blue. You told me *Indiana* might go blue."

"Indiana is a toss-up."

"Indiana hasn't gone blue since LBJ."

"I'm aware."

"So how are you so sure?"

I shrug, and the shrug is a little too careful, a little too practiced. "Demographics. Early voting numbers. The Loudon and Fairfax county registrations are through the roof. It's not a mystery if you look at the data."

Marcus doesn't push it. He files it away. I can see him doing it — the way his eyes stay on me for one extra beat, the way he nods slowly, the way his fingers tap twice on the arm of the couch. Marcus Wade has a filing system for things that don't add up, and I've just been entered into it.

The early returns come in, and the room gets louder. Marcus's father, Calvin, comes home from his shift at Tyson's still in his work boots, and Gloria makes him take them off at the door, and he stands in socked feet watching Pennsylvania go blue and says, "Well, now."

That's it. "Well, now." Two words that contain the whole history of a man who grew up in a country that told him certain things were not possible, watching one of those things become possible on a Tuesday night in November.

Gloria cries. She doesn't make a show of it. She just sits on the arm of the couch and tears run down her face, and when CNN calls it, when they project Barack Obama the forty-fourth president of the United States, she puts her hand over her mouth and breathes and Darnell asks if this means they get a day off school and Calvin says, "No, boy, it means you work harder."

Marcus watches his parents. I watch Marcus. His face does something I don't have a word for — it goes open in a way that seventeen-year-old boys almost never allow, unguarded, and I can see the future engineer in him, the systems thinker, trying to build a structure that holds this feeling so he can keep it.

And I sit on the Wades' living room carpet with a brownie going stale in my hand and I think: *I was here for this. In the original timeline, I was here for this exact night, in this exact room, and I didn't appreciate it. I was thinking about myself. I was probably composing something clever to say at school the next day, some joke about McCain's concession speech, some way to be the smartest person in the room about something that wasn't about being smart.*

On the TV, the crowd in Grant Park is enormous, oceanic, and the sound of it comes through the speakers tinny and compressed but still enough to make the hair on my arms stand up. Gloria opens her hand and Calvin takes it, and they stand there in their living room on Tremont Street in Cedar Falls, Iowa, holding hands, watching.

"I told you," I say to Marcus, quiet enough that only he hears.

"You told me," he admits.

I should stop talking. I should let it be.

"Indiana, too," I say, because I can't help it, because this is the disease — the need to be right, to be seen being right, to turn every moment into evidence of my own intelligence. Even here. Even now. Even watching Gloria Wade cry.

Marcus gives me a look. Not angry. Worse — curious. "Yeah," he says. "You called that."

---

I drive home in Frank's truck, which he lent me without being asked because Frank Callahan lends things the way trees make oxygen — automatically, without tracking the debt. The heater rattles. The dead pine air freshener has graduated from faintly aromatic to purely decorative. The radio is on, and someone is analyzing exit polls, and I turn it off because I can't listen to anyone else be certain about anything tonight.

The streets of Cedar Falls are quiet the way small Iowa towns are quiet on weekday nights — porch lights on, living rooms flickering blue with television, the occasional dog trotting across a yard. It's thirty-eight degrees and dropping, and my breath fogs when I stop at the light on Main and University.

I think about Gloria Wade's hand over her mouth.

I think about Calvin saying *work harder.*

I think about Marcus's face, unguarded, open, trusting the moment to hold him.

I think about how I sat in that room as a guest, a welcomed and fed and browned guest, and my first instinct was to make sure everyone knew I'd predicted it. I was right. I was right. Look at me being right.

This is what I am. Was. Am. The tenses collapse when you're thirty-five pretending to be seventeen. What I *am* is someone who takes the warmest, most human moments in other people's lives and finds a way to make them about me. What I *was* is someone who did that for eighteen more years after this night and lost everything that mattered in the process.

I pull into the driveway on Sycamore — no. Not Sycamore. That's the apartment. That's the future. This is Maple Drive, the house with the uneven front steps that Frank keeps meaning to fix. I sit in the truck for a minute with the engine idling.

Authenticity. I keep circling the word like it's a drain. Be authentic. Be real. Be honest. But here's what I've figured out in the three weeks since I woke up in a bedroom with a Dark Knight poster: authenticity isn't something you perform. You can't try to be genuine. The trying is the opposite of the thing. It's like trying to fall asleep — the effort pushes the goal further away.

So what do you do? If trying to be good is just another kind of performance, and not trying got you a FedEx Office job and a disconnected phone number and an apartment full of nothing?

I don't know. I turn off the truck and go inside, and Frank is asleep in his Adirondack chair on the porch with a blanket over his legs that Diane must have put there. I don't wake him. I just stand for a second, looking at the shape of him in the dark — the reading glasses still on, the reddish-brown hair catching the porch light — and then I go upstairs and lie in my childhood bed and stare at the ceiling and don't sleep for a long time.

---

Friday. After school. Marcus and I walk toward Tremont. The air smells like burning leaves — someone on Elm Street has a pile going, which is technically against city ordinance but practically universal.

We've been talking about nothing. Jake Petersen's new haircut, which looks like he lost a bet. Cody Voss's theory that energy drinks don't actually give you energy, they just borrow it from tomorrow. Whether Mr. Danaher grades harder before or after lunch. The comfortable, pointless conversation of two people who don't need a reason to talk.

Marcus stops at the corner of Tremont and Orchard. This is where we split — he goes left, I go right. We've stood at this corner a thousand times. Will stand here a thousand more, if I don't ruin it.

"So," Marcus says. He adjusts his backpack. The Casio watch — three minutes fast, always, because Marcus believes being early is a form of respect — catches the late afternoon sun. "You called that election thing dead on."

I shrug. "Lucky guess."

"Nah." He shakes his head, slow. "It wasn't a guess. You knew. Virginia, Indiana, the whole map. You knew."

"I just read a lot of polls."

Marcus looks at me. The gap-toothed grin is gone. In its place is something quieter, more focused — the look he gets when he's working a problem that doesn't resolve cleanly. "Okay," he says.

That's it. *Okay.* He doesn't push. He doesn't accuse. He just takes the thing that doesn't add up and puts it in the file, and the file gets a little thicker, and Marcus Wade walks away down Tremont Street with his hands in his pockets and his backpack slung over one shoulder.

He doesn't push it.

But he remembers.
