I tell Marcus on a Tuesday because Tuesdays are when he works half-days at the Deere plant, and because I have learned over eighteen years that the shape of a confession matters almost as much as its content. Not a weekend, when Priya and the baby are home and the house smells like her mother's dal and Marcus is soft with domestic comfort. Not a Friday, when the week's exhaustion makes everything feel heavier than it is. Tuesday. His garage. Two lawn chairs that have survived four Iowa winters. The mini-fridge he keeps stocked with Busch Light that I have never once seen him drink.

He's got a carburetor spread across newspaper on the workbench when I pull up. Rebuilding something for his neighbor's riding mower. His hands are black to the wrists, and he wipes them on a rag that only redistributes the grease. Six-three and folded into a camp chair like a man who learned early that the world builds everything six inches too short.

"You look terrible," he says.

"I need to talk to you."

He checks his Casio — still three minutes fast, still silver, still the same watch he bought at a Walmart in Ames our sophomore year because he said digital was more honest than analog. Marcus has always had opinions about honesty.

"Talk," he says.

So I do.

I start where I started with Elena, with Chen, with Frank. The plane. The moment of death. The return. But Marcus is not Elena, who listened with her strategist's mind already sorting information into actionable categories. He is not Chen, who heard the impossible and immediately began constructing frameworks to contain it. He is not Frank, who believed because he'd spent thirty-four years learning the specific frequency of his son's voice when his son is telling the truth.

Marcus listens with his arms crossed and his jaw set and the expression of a man watching someone weld a load-bearing joint without checking the specs.

I talk for forty minutes. The garage gets hot. A wasp builds something ambitious in the corner of the window frame. Marcus does not interrupt. He does not ask questions. He does not move, except once, to scratch the back of his neck where the sun has turned his skin the color of old brick.

When I finish, the silence lasts long enough for the wasp to make meaningful progress.

"Okay," Marcus says.

"Okay?"

"I'm going to need to see everything."

---

I hand him the evidence package. It's physical — a three-ring binder, because Marcus distrusts anything that lives exclusively on a screen. Printouts of every prediction I've logged since 2008, timestamped and notarized. Chen's atmospheric data with the anomalous sulfur dioxide readings and the spectral analysis that doesn't match any terrestrial source. Pathfinder orbital tracking for all six objects, plotted against my original projections. Photocopies of sealed envelopes opened in the presence of witnesses — the 2016 Cubs, the 2020 pandemic, the 2024 earthquake in Turkey, a dozen smaller things that nobody would think to predict unless they'd already seen them happen.

Marcus takes the binder. He does not open it in front of me.

"Three days," he says.

"Take as long as you need."

"Three days." He says it like he's estimating cure time on an epoxy joint. Not negotiable. A material property of the process. "I'll call you."

He stands up, and for a moment I think he's going to say something else — something about the eighteen years of half-truths, about the blackmail, about the night he told me he was tired of watching me turn the right thing into the wrong thing done efficiently. But Marcus has never been a man who speaks before he's finished thinking.

He walks inside. The screen door slaps shut behind him.

I sit in his garage for another ten minutes, listening to the wasp.

---

Day one, he calls Chen.

I know this because Chen texts me at 11:47 PM: *Your friend Marcus just spent two hours on the phone with me. He asked better questions than the peer reviewers.*

I write back: *What did you tell him?*

*The truth. That you are the most sane person I know, and I wish you weren't.*

*How did he take that?*

*He asked me to email him the raw spectral data. Not the paper. The data. He said he wanted to run his own analysis.* A pause. Then: *I like him.*

Day two, Marcus drives to the University of Iowa observatory. I know this because Dr. Lena Vasquez, who runs the public observation program, emails me to say a very tall man with a gap between his front teeth spent four hours in her facility cross-referencing orbital trajectories against publicly available tracking data from the Minor Planet Center, and could I please explain why he had hand-drawn plots that matched objects she'd been quietly monitoring for six months.

I call Vasquez. She is one of Chen's three connected scientists. She knows what the objects are. She tells me Marcus was meticulous, patient, and did not accept a single data point without verifying it against an independent source. She says he recalculated three of her own trajectory projections and found a rounding error in one of them.

"He corrected my math," she says, and she sounds like she means it as the highest possible compliment.

Day three, nothing. No calls. No texts. I spend the day at the NEORAN operations center with Elena, running final checks on communication redundancies, and every twenty minutes I look at my phone like a teenager.

At 6:14 PM, a text from Marcus: *Come over.*

---

The garage again. Same chairs. He's cleaned the workbench. The carburetor is gone, reassembled and presumably reinstalled in the neighbor's mower, because Marcus does not leave work unfinished. The binder is on the workbench, closed, with a yellow legal pad on top of it covered in his handwriting — small, precise, all-caps, the penmanship of a man who was taught that if your notes aren't legible to someone else, they aren't notes, they're secrets.

He's sitting in the lawn chair with a Busch Light open in his hand. First time I have ever seen him drink one.

"Sit down," he says.

I sit.

Marcus takes a long pull of the beer. Sets it on the concrete between his feet. Looks at me with an expression I can't read, which is unusual, because I have known this man for sixteen years in this timeline and twelve in the one before it, and Marcus's face has always been a large, legible document.

"Your predictions are accurate," he says. "All of them. The notarization timestamps check out. Chen's atmospheric data is clean — I ran the spectral analysis three different ways and got the same result every time. The orbital tracks for the six objects match your projections within a margin that's too tight to be coincidence or luck. Vasquez's independent observations confirm."

He picks up the beer again. Puts it down without drinking.

"Either you are telling the truth," he says, "or you have orchestrated the most elaborate and pointless hoax in human history, involving a Stanford atmospheric physicist, a university observatory, and publicly verifiable astronomical data, for the sole purpose of convincing your best friend of something impossible. And I know you. You are not that creative."

I almost smile. Almost.

"So," Marcus says. "You had eighteen years."

"Yes."

"You could have made a billion dollars."

"Yes."

"You could have been president. You could have — what? Cured something? Prevented something? You had perfect knowledge of eighteen years of human history. You could have done anything."

"Yes."

Marcus leans forward. His elbows on his knees. His hands hanging between them, still big, still calloused, still marked with the ghost of Tuesday's grease.

"And you spent them fixing your relationship with your dad and building a disaster nonprofit in Iowa."

The sentence lands in the garage like a stone into still water. I can feel the rings of it moving outward, touching the walls, the workbench, the mini-fridge, the folded chairs in the corner that we only bring out when Frank comes over for playoff games.

"Yes," I say.

"Why?"

I have had eighteen years to formulate an answer to this question. I have turned it over in my mind on long drives and sleepless nights and in the shower and while watching Frank eat pot roast and while holding Sophie's hand in the NEORAN office and while sitting next to Elena at her desk while she redesigned the supply chain for the end of the world.

The answer has never gotten more complicated than the truth.

"Because the first time around," I say, "I was rich in nothing that mattered."

Marcus breathes out through his nose. A long, controlled exhale. The sound of a man absorbing impact.

"Tell me about the first time," he says. "Tell me about me."

I knew this was coming. I have known since I sat in his garage three days ago that Marcus would not stop at verifying the future. He would need to understand the past. The other past. The one I unmade.

"You were my best friend," I say. "Same as now. But I was — I was not a good friend. I was not a good anything. I was running. I was always running, and I told myself the running was ambition, and I left behind everyone who couldn't keep up."

Marcus says nothing. His eyes are on me. Steady. Unblinking.

"I owed you twelve hundred dollars," I say. "It had been months. I kept saying I'd pay you back. And then you found out I had money set aside. Not for you. For a Vegas trip."

Marcus's jaw tightens. Just slightly. Enough.

"You left your key on the counter," I say. "The key to my apartment. The one I gave you after I got the place on Sixth Street. You put it on the counter and you walked out and you never spoke to me again."

The garage is so quiet I can hear the wasp in the window frame. I can hear the hum of the mini-fridge. I can hear, distantly, through the wall that separates the garage from the kitchen, Priya singing something to the baby. A melody I don't recognize. Something her mother taught her, maybe.

Marcus's eyes have gone bright. Not wet. Bright. The look of a man staring at a schematic of a bridge that collapsed with people on it.

"And you spent eighteen years making sure that didn't happen," he says.

"It was the least I owed you."

He picks up the beer. His hand is steady. He drinks the rest of it in one long pull and sets the empty can on the concrete with a precision that is purely Marcus — centered between his feet, label facing forward, as if even garbage has a correct orientation.

"Twelve hundred dollars," he says.

"Twelve hundred dollars."

"That's what it cost."

"That's what it cost."

He stands up. He is so tall that he has to duck the crossbeam of the garage door track, and I have watched him do this ten thousand times, and it has never once stopped being the most Marcus thing in the world — the way he dips his head, automatic, like the house is a machine and he knows exactly where the clearances are.

"Come here," he says.

He hugs me. Marcus is not a hugger. In sixteen years, I can count the times on one hand — his wedding, the day his daughter was born, the night Frank's scan came back clean, the night after the blackmail when he showed up at my door and didn't say a word, just stood there until I let him in. This is the fifth time.

He holds on for a long time. His arms around my shoulders, his chin on the top of my head because the height difference makes anything else architecturally impractical. He smells like beer and motor oil and the specific laundry detergent Priya buys in bulk from Costco.

When he lets go, he steps back and clears his throat and adjusts his watch, which does not need adjusting.

"What do you need from me?" he says.

"I need you to take your family to the Grinnell shelter on June 12th. I need you to stay there until I tell you it's safe."

"What else?"

"That's it. That's all I need."

"No," Marcus says. "What else. What do you need help with. Between now and then. What's the work."

I look at him. His jaw is set. His shoulders are squared. He looks like a man who has been told the bridge is going to fail in two weeks and has decided that two weeks is enough time to reinforce the span if nobody sleeps.

"There's a lot of work," I say.

"Good," Marcus says. "I'm off Wednesdays."

---

NEORAN goes fully operational on a Wednesday in October, because Elena believes in symmetry and Sophie believes in getting things done and I have learned to stay out of their way when they agree on a timeline.

The activation is not a single moment. It is a cascade.

The shelter network goes live first. Fourteen reinforced facilities across Iowa, Illinois, and Missouri, each stocked for six months of self-sufficiency. We tested the ventilation systems in August. The water filtration in September. The communication hardlines that don't rely on satellites in the second week of October, when Sophie stood in the basement of the Grinnell facility and spoke into a handset and Elena heard her voice, clear and unbroken, in the Des Moines operations center, routed through sixty miles of buried fiber optic cable that Chen's funding helped pay for.

"Copy," Elena said, and Sophie said, "Copy," and I stood between them, thirty-seven miles from each, in the Iowa City relay station, and listened to both their voices and thought: this is what I built. Not the cable. Not the shelters. The fact that these two women trust each other because they both, independently, decided to trust me.

The seed vaults seal in November. Four locations. Cold-hardy grain varieties, native prairie species, medicinal plants. The agricultural team Chen connected us with from Iowa State runs the final inventory. Eleven thousand seed samples. Each one a small argument against extinction.

The hydroponic facilities come online in December, running at half capacity as a proof of concept. Leafy greens and herbs. The full-spectrum grow lights cast everything in a pinkish glow that makes the workers look like they're inside a body. Sophie tours every facility personally. She is twenty-eight years old and she walks through the rows of lettuce and kale with a clipboard and a flashlight and the specific authority of a woman who has decided that competence is a form of love.

The communication redundancies go live in January. Ham radio networks. Mesh nodes. The buried fiber. Satellite uplinks for as long as satellites exist. Printed manuals for when they don't. Marcus builds the antenna array for the Iowa City hub on three consecutive Wednesdays, arriving at seven AM with his own tools and a thermos of coffee and working until the light fails. He does not talk much while he works. He reads the specs once, asks two clarifying questions, and then builds the thing exactly as designed, except for one junction where he improvises a better solution and documents the change in his all-caps handwriting on the margin of the blueprint.

I watch the network take shape and I think about what it is and what it is not.

It is not enough. Fourteen shelters will not save Iowa. The food stores will run out. The communications will degrade. The seed vaults are a hope, not a guarantee. We are building a lifeboat on the deck of a ship that is going to hit an iceberg, and the lifeboat is too small, and we know it is too small, and we are building it anyway because the alternative is to sit in the lounge and wait for the water.

It is something. It is the best we can do. And I have spent eighteen years learning that the best you can do is enough, not because it solves the problem, but because it means you did not look away.

---

Spring comes early. The winter was mild. February is forty-five degrees and sunny, which in Iowa feels like a personal favor from a universe that does not usually extend them.

Sophie runs the final community outreach push in March. Door-to-door in the small towns. We don't tell people what's coming. We can't. We tell them about the shelters. We tell them about the emergency protocols. We tell them that NEORAN exists and that if something happens — anything, a tornado, a flood, something you've never seen before — there is a place to go and a plan in place and people who will help.

Most of them sign up. Iowans are practical about disaster preparedness. They live in a state that tries to kill them with weather every year. They keep canned goods in the basement and flashlights by the door and they understand, in their bones, that readiness is not paranoia. It is respect.

Sophie handles the skeptics with a patience I did not know she had. The rancher outside Tama who thinks we're a government front. The church group in Marengo who believes preparedness is a failure of faith. The old couple in Brooklyn — Brooklyn, Iowa, population fourteen hundred — who have survived sixty years of blizzards and floods and see no reason to change their approach now.

She does not argue. She does not convince. She gives them the information and the location of the nearest shelter and her phone number and she says: "If something happens, call me. I will come get you." And she means it, and they can tell she means it, and most of them take the card.

I watch her work from the passenger seat of the NEORAN van, because Sophie has told me, in terms that do not invite negotiation, that I am not to speak during community outreach visits. "You sound like a TED talk," she said. "People don't trust TED talks. They trust the person who shows up."

She's right. She's been right about most things since the midnight phone call, since the night she sat on the porch in Cedar Rapids and I told her the truth and she cried and then stopped crying and then asked what needed to be done. The sibling who spent a decade not trusting me. The operational partner who trusts me now with a ferocity that sometimes makes it hard to breathe.

April. Chen calls. The objects have shifted again. Closer than projected. The new estimates put arrival at June 15th, plus or minus forty-eight hours. We had July. Now we have June. The timeline is a rope and someone is pulling the other end.

I tell Elena. She recalculates supply needs, adjusts shelter rotation schedules, moves the final equipment installations forward by three weeks. She does this in one afternoon, at her desk in the Des Moines center, with a cup of coffee that goes cold while she works. She does not panic. Elena does not panic. She processes. She adapts. She builds new structures inside the ruins of the old ones before the dust has settled.

I tell Marcus. He cancels his next three Wednesdays off and works the antenna installation every evening after his shift at Deere. Priya brings him dinner at the site. Containers of rice and chicken and dal, still warm, because Priya has decided that if her husband is going to work himself into the ground preparing for something he won't fully explain to her, the least she deserves is the knowledge that he's eating. Marcus has told Priya that NEORAN is a disaster preparedness nonprofit and that Nate is worried about some atmospheric data and that it's probably nothing but better safe than sorry. This is not a lie. It is a version of the truth that will keep her functional until June 12th, when Marcus will take her and the baby to the Grinnell shelter and explain the rest. He and I have agreed on this. The timing. The place. What he will say and what he will not say.

I tell Sophie. She looks at me across the office with an expression that is pure Callahan — the set jaw, the level gaze, the refusal to let the scope of a problem determine the scope of the response.

"Then we go faster," she says.

We go faster.

May. The days are long and bright. Iowa turns green with the aggressive optimism of a state that does not know what's coming, and even if I told it, would probably plant the corn anyway. The fields outside the office window are plowed and seeded and already showing the first pale threads of emergence. Someone will harvest this corn or no one will. Both possibilities exist simultaneously, and I have learned to hold them without choosing, because choosing is a luxury that belongs to people who are not counting days.

The final systems check happens on May 28th. All fourteen shelters. All communication lines. All supply caches. Sophie runs it from Iowa City. Elena monitors from Des Moines. Chen patches in from Palo Alto. Marcus is in Grinnell, testing the last antenna junction, the one he redesigned because the original spec used a connector rated for sixty-mile-per-hour winds and Marcus has opinions about the difference between rated capacity and actual performance under stress.

Everything works. Not perfectly. The Ottumwa shelter has a ventilation lag. The Quad Cities relay drops signal for eleven seconds during the handoff test. The Cedar Rapids facility's water filtration runs three percent below target flow rate.

But everything works. The imperfect, cobbled-together, underfunded, understaffed network that we built in a state most people fly over — it works. The lights come on. The air moves. The water runs. The voices carry.

It is not enough.

It is something.

---

June 1st, 2026.

I am standing at my office window. The NEORAN Iowa City hub, which used to be a farm supply warehouse, which before that was a feed store, which before that was probably a barn. The bones of the building are old and honest. Iowa builds things to last, even when it doesn't know what they'll need to last through.

The sky is clear. Not a cloud. Not a contrail. Just blue, the specific blue of early Iowa summer, deep enough to fall into if you tilt your head back far enough. I have been looking at the sky more often lately. Checking it. The way you check a wound you know is going to open.

Two weeks.

Fourteen days. Give or take forty-eight hours, which is not a margin of error I find comforting, but Chen says it's the best the data can do and I believe her because she has never told me a comfortable thing when an accurate thing was available.

My phone buzzes on the desk. I pick it up.

Frank Callahan.

*Dinner Sunday? Your mom's making pot roast.*

I read the text three times. I read it the way I read Chen's atmospheric data, the way I read Marcus's all-caps notes on the antenna blueprints, the way I read Elena's supply calculations — looking for the meaning underneath the meaning, the structure beneath the words.

But there is no hidden structure. There is no subtext. There is a seventy-one-year-old man in Cedar Rapids who survived lymphoma and a son who lied to him for eighteen years and a wife who makes pot roast on Sundays because Sunday is for family and pot roast is what she makes when everyone's coming home.

Frank knows the world ends in two weeks. He knows it the way he knows the cancer might come back and the way he knows the winters are getting worse and the way he knows his son carried an impossible weight for eighteen years — he knows it completely, and he has decided that the correct response is pot roast.

This is Frank. This has always been Frank. The man who showed up. The man who stayed. The man who, when his son told him the truth in a parking lot in the rain, did not ask for proof or time or space. He asked if Nate had eaten.

I type my response.

*I'll be there.*

I put the phone down. I look out the window at the sky that is still blue, that is still clear, that in fourteen days will not be. I think about pot roast. I think about my mother's kitchen, the one with the window that faces the backyard where Frank built the deck the summer I was twelve, the first time, and the summer I was twelve, the second time, and both times he let me hold the level and both times I held it crooked and both times he said, "Close enough, bud."

I think about Marcus in his garage, checking his Casio, running his own analysis because faith is not a methodology and love does not excuse you from verification. I think about Sophie in the NEORAN van, handing out cards with her phone number, promising strangers she will come for them. I think about Elena at her desk, building systems that will outlast the world that needed them. I think about Chen in Palo Alto, measuring the approach of something she cannot stop, with instruments precise enough to tell her exactly how much time she has left.

I think about all the versions of these people who exist in a timeline that no longer does. The Marcus who left his key on the counter. The Frank who never knew his son. The Sophie who never trusted her brother. The versions of them that I unmade when I chose, in the first hours of the second chance, to be worth what I'd been given.

Two weeks. The sky is still blue.

I'll be there.
