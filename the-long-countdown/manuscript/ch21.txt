I tell her on a Tuesday because there's no good day for it and I'm tired of waiting for one.

We're in the apartment. Elena is grading papers at the kitchen table, red pen in her teeth, laptop open to a rubric she built herself because the district's version, she says, was written by someone who has never met a tenth-grader. I'm standing at the counter pretending to make coffee. I've been pretending to make coffee for eleven minutes. The grounds are in the filter. The water is in the reservoir. I haven't pressed the button.

"You're hovering," she says without looking up.

"I need to tell you something."

The pen comes out of her teeth. She caps it, sets it down parallel to the edge of her laptop. Lines things up. Elena does this when she's bracing. I've learned her tells the way you learn weather — not because you want to but because your life depends on it.

"Okay," she says.

I sit down across from her. The table is small. Our knees almost touch.

"You know about the prediction work," I say. "The atmospheric modeling. The catastrophe timeline."

"I know what you've told me."

There it is already. What I've told her. The implication that what I've told her is a curated version of something larger. She's not wrong. She's almost never wrong about this kind of thing, which is one of the reasons I love her and one of the reasons this is going to be terrible.

"There's more," I say.

"I assumed."

"This part is going to sound — " I stop. Start over. "I'm not going to qualify it. I'm just going to say it and you can react however you react."

Elena closes her laptop. Folds her hands on top of it. Gives me the look she gives her students when they're about to explain why the essay is late — patient, expectant, already three moves ahead.

"I'm not from this timeline," I say. "I lived through the catastrophe once already. In another version. A first version. I was displaced backwards — sent back to before any of it happened. I've been living this version with full knowledge of what's coming."

Silence. A car passes on the street below. Someone honks.

"How far back," she says.

Of all the questions. Not *what* or *how* or *are you serious*. How far back.

"Thirteen years. I came back to 2004. I was twenty-one the second time around."

She's quiet for a long time. Not the dramatic quiet of shock. The working quiet of a mind doing math. I can almost see her running the numbers, cross-referencing dates, mapping my decisions against this new variable.

"The volunteer event," she says finally.

"Yes."

"You organized it."

"Yes."

"You knew I'd be there."

"No." I lean forward. This matters. "I organized it because I needed volunteers for the network. People who understood community organizing, mutual aid. You showed up because your father got deported when you were fifteen and you know what it means to lose everything. I didn't recruit you. You walked in."

"But you knew who I was."

"I knew your name. From the original. You were — you ran a community literacy program in Reno. We never met. You were a name on a list of people doing useful work."

"A list." She says it flat.

"Elena."

"No, keep going. I want to hear all of it. Not in installments."

That lands. She means it to.

"In the original timeline, the catastrophe hit in 2037. Mass atmospheric event. Cascading failures — agriculture, infrastructure, supply chains. Billions of people. I survived for a few years after. Then the displacement happened and I was back, twenty-one years old, standing in a parking lot in New Mexico, and I knew everything that was coming."

"Everything."

"The science. The timeline. Not the personal things. Those were all different this time. Frank, Diane, NEORAN, Chen — the relationships are all new. You're new. You were always new."

She stands up. Goes to the window. Stands with her back to me. Her shoulders are level, spine straight. She teaches teenagers in an underfunded school district. She doesn't collapse. She regroups.

"How much of what you've told me over the past year has been true?" she asks.

"All of it."

"How much has been complete?"

I don't answer immediately, and that's answer enough.

She turns around. "That's what I thought. You've been telling me the truth in installments, like I couldn't handle it all at once. Like I needed to be managed."

"That's not — "

"That's exactly what it is. You decided what I was ready for. You decided when I could know about the predictions, when I could see the modeling, when I could meet Chen. You built a ladder and you let me climb it one rung at a time and you told yourself it was kindness."

"It wasn't kindness. It was fear."

"Fear of what? That I'd leave?"

"That you'd stay for the wrong reasons. That you'd stay because the stakes were too high to walk away, not because you wanted to be here."

She crosses her arms. Studies me. I've seen her do this to colleagues who bring her problems disguised as compliments. She has a gift for seeing the mechanism behind the gesture.

"And Chen?" she says. "How did Chen take it?"

"Chen asked for the data."

"Of course she did." Almost a smile. Not quite. "And she believed you."

"Chen believed the atmospheric modeling first. The personal stuff she accepted because it explained the data."

"That's very Chen." Elena uncrosses her arms. Sits back down. Not across from me — in the chair beside me. Closer than the table allows for comfortably. Our knees do touch this time.

"I have questions," she says.

"I know."

"A lot of questions."

"I know."

"Were you married? In the original."

"No. I had relationships. They didn't last. I wasn't good at them."

"Are you good at them now?"

"I'm trying to be."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the honest one."

She looks at her hands. Her nails are short, unpainted. She bites them when she's thinking about budget cuts. They're bitten down now. Budget season.

"The volunteer event," she says again. "The one where we met. You really didn't know I'd show up."

"I hoped someone like you would show up. Someone who understood mutual aid, who had organizing experience, who wasn't going to need the crisis explained to them. I didn't know it would be you."

"But you'd seen my name."

"On a list. Years before. In a different version of the world."

"And when I walked in?"

"I didn't recognize you. You were a stranger. I thought you were angry about something — you had this look — "

"I was angry about something. My car overheated on Route 28 and I had to walk the last mile."

"I didn't know that."

"No," she says. "You didn't." She looks at me. "You didn't plan me."

"You were the only variable I never accounted for."

Something shifts in her face. Not softening exactly. Something more precise than that. Like a lens clicking into focus.

"I'm not leaving again," she says. "I need you to hear that. But you need to understand something. I'm staying because I choose to, not because you managed my information correctly. Not because you calibrated the dosage. I choose to be here and that means you don't get to decide what I can handle. Not anymore."

"Okay."

"I mean it, Nate."

"I know you do."

"Everything. From now on. If Chen finds something, I hear about it the same day. If the models change, I see them. If you're scared, you tell me you're scared. You don't metabolize it and hand me the summary."

"Okay," I say again, and I mean it in a way that costs something.

She reaches across and takes the hand I've been resting on the table. Her grip is strong. She has a callus on her right index finger from the red pen.

"Thirteen years," she says, and shakes her head. "Thirteen years of knowing."

"Yes."

"Were you lonely?"

The question is so simple and so exactly right that for a moment I can't speak. I look at our hands on the table. Hers brown, mine pale, fingers interlocked. The kitchen light buzzes faintly. Her laptop hums in sleep mode. A Tuesday in July, seven-thirty in the evening, and someone has finally asked the only question that matters.

"Yes," I say.

She nods. Doesn't say *I'm sorry* or *that must have been hard*. Just nods, like she's filing it in the place where she keeps the things she'll carry for you without being asked.

---

We have eleven weeks of something close to normal. Elena integrates the full truth the way she integrates everything — methodically, with follow-up questions at unexpected moments. She asks about the first timeline over dinner, in bed, on walks. Not the science. The texture. What did people eat. How did governance fail. Were there still schools.

She asks about the version of herself that existed in that timeline, the one I never met. I tell her what I know, which isn't much. Community literacy program. Reno. She would have been good at it.

"I would have been good at it," she agrees, and there's no sadness in it. Just recognition.

She meets with Chen twice. I'm not present for either conversation. When I ask what they discussed, Elena says, "Science. And you. Mostly you." She doesn't elaborate. I don't push. I told her I'd stop managing her information and I'm keeping that promise even when it makes me itch.

In September, she sits in on a NEORAN strategy session. Afterward she tells me the organizational structure is inefficient and the communication protocols need work. By October she's redesigned both. Nobody asked her to. She just did it, the way she redesigned the district's grading rubric — because the existing version wasn't good enough and someone had to fix it.

"You realize you've basically become the operations director," I tell her.

"Someone had to be."

This is the thing about Elena that I couldn't have predicted from a name on a list: she doesn't orbit. She builds. You give her a crisis and she doesn't react to it, she reorganizes around it. She treats the end of the world like a systems problem because that's what it is, and systems problems have solutions if you're willing to do the work.

I watch her run a logistics meeting in November and think: this is what I was missing. Not a partner. An architect.

---

Frank calls on a Saturday in December. Diane's voice, actually, on Frank's phone.

"Don't panic," she says, which is what people say when the appropriate response is to panic. "Frank had some chest pain this morning. We're at St. Mary's. They're running tests."

I'm in the car before she finishes the sentence. Elena hears me grab the keys and comes to the bedroom door. "What happened?"

"Frank. Chest pain. Hospital."

She's dressed and in the passenger seat in under two minutes. She doesn't ask if I want company.

The drive to St. Mary's takes twenty-two minutes. I make it in sixteen. Elena doesn't comment on my speed. She puts her hand on my knee when I run the second yellow light but she doesn't tell me to slow down.

The emergency department smells like hand sanitizer and floor wax. Fluorescent lights the color of old teeth. A television in the waiting area plays cable news on mute. The chairs are plastic, molded to fit no human body that has ever existed. A child is crying somewhere down the hall — the breathless, hiccupping cry of exhaustion, not pain.

Diane meets us in the hallway. She looks small in the way that competent people look small when the competence isn't enough. Her hair is pulled back in a clip that's losing its grip on the left side. She's wearing Frank's flannel over her own shirt, like she grabbed whatever was closest.

"They're saying it's a mild MI," she says. "Heart attack. Mild." She says *mild* like she's testing whether the word can bear weight. "There's a blockage. They want to put in a stent."

"Same as last time?"

"Different artery. They said it's manageable."

Manageable. Mild. The vocabulary of medical reassurance, engineered to prevent the useful kind of fear.

"Can I see him?" I ask.

"He's in pre-op. You can go in for a few minutes."

Frank is in a hospital gown, sitting up in bed, looking like a man who has been wrongly imprisoned. The gown is too short in the arms and he keeps pulling at the IV line like it's a personal insult. His color is off — not gray exactly, but dulled, like someone turned his contrast down.

"For Christ's sake," he says when he sees me. "They called you."

"Diane called me."

"I told her not to. It's a stent. Plumbing work. They go in through the wrist now, did you know that? In and out."

"Frank."

"Don't give me that face. That's the face people make when they think you're dying. I am not dying. I am getting a stent, which is a medical procedure roughly as complicated as an oil change, and then I am going home."

I pull up the chair beside his bed. The vinyl squeaks. "How do you feel?"

"Annoyed. The pain stopped an hour ago. I feel fine."

"You didn't feel fine this morning."

"This morning I felt like someone was sitting on my chest. This is no longer the case. I have conveyed this information to the staff, who are unmoved."

I look at him. Sixty-three years old. Thinner than he used to be. The hair gone fully white at the temples. His hands on the hospital blanket are spotted and knuckled in a way they weren't when I first sat across from him in a VA group meeting, when he was fifty-four and I was twenty-five and he was the first person who talked to me like I was real.

In the first timeline, Frank died at sixty-eight. I know this with the same precision I know atmospheric carbon trajectories and grain yield projections. Heart failure, progressive. The first event was the one that started it — a mild MI, manageable, a stent. And then another. And then the decline.

In the first timeline, I wasn't here. I was in another state, building another version of a network that didn't include a retired Marine who cooked bad chili and told the truth. Frank died without me because in the first version we hadn't met yet and wouldn't have, and he was a statistic in a medical database, one more sixty-eight-year-old man whose heart gave out.

"Hey," Frank says. "Where'd you go?"

"Nowhere. I'm here."

"You've got that look. The one where you're doing math about things you can't control."

"I'm not doing math."

"You're always doing math. Sit down and stop doing math."

"I am sitting down."

"Then sit down harder. Relax. It's a stent."

A nurse comes in to check his vitals. Frank submits to the blood pressure cuff with the tolerance of a man who has learned that arguing with medical professionals is a losing proposition. The nurse — young, efficient, tired around the eyes — tells us the procedure is scheduled for forty-five minutes from now. She calls it "straightforward," which I am learning is the medical profession's favorite word.

When she leaves, Frank looks at me. Really looks, the way he does when he's about to say the thing he's been thinking about for a while.

"I'm sixty-three," he says. "I've had a good run."

"Don't."

"I'm not being morbid. I'm being practical. Sixty-three is a real number. My father died at fifty-nine. My grandfather at sixty-one. I'm already playing with house money."

"You're getting a stent, not writing a eulogy."

"I'm saying — if something happens — "

"Nothing is happening. You said so yourself. Plumbing work."

He studies me. "You're scared."

"I'm not."

"Kid, I've known you for nine years. You're terrified. And you're terrible at hiding it."

I don't say anything. He reaches over and taps the back of my hand with two fingers. The gesture is awkward because of the IV, and small, and it breaks something open in my chest that I immediately brick back up because this is not the time.

"I'll be fine," he says.

"I know."

"Go sit with Diane. She's the one who needs company."

---

The waiting room during a cardiac procedure is a specific kind of hell. Not the dramatic kind. The beige kind. Plastic chairs and vending machines and a television nobody is watching. Time moves differently here — not slowly exactly, but thickly, like pushing through water. Every minute is a minute in which something could go wrong, and every minute in which nothing goes wrong is a small exhausting victory that leads directly into the next minute of the same.

Elena has gone to get coffee from the cafeteria. Diane and I sit side by side in chairs designed to make sitting uncomfortable. She's knitting. She brought knitting to the emergency room, which tells you everything you need to know about Diane — she is a woman who prepares for waiting the way other people prepare for storms.

The needles click. Soft yarn, dark green. She's making a scarf for someone.

"He was in the garage when it started," she says, not looking up from her work. "Sorting his fishing tackle. He sat down on the step and he said, 'Di, something's wrong.' Just like that. No drama. Just 'something's wrong.'"

"That sounds like him."

"I drove. He wanted to drive. I told him if he touched the steering wheel I'd break his hand."

"Also sounds like you."

She almost smiles. The needles keep clicking. Purl, knit, purl. A rhythm like breathing.

"He had the stent two years ago," she says. "Different artery, they said. So either the plumbing has multiple problems or the first fix didn't take. Either way."

"Either way."

She sets the knitting down in her lap. Looks at me. Her eyes are dry and clear and frightened in a way she's controlling with the same discipline Frank uses to control his.

"He's going to be fine," she says.

"He is."

"He's too stubborn to die in a hospital. He told me once he plans to die fishing. Fall off the boat and float away. He was very specific about it."

"That's a terrible plan."

"I told him that. He said the fish would appreciate the irony."

I almost laugh. Almost. What comes out is something smaller, a short breath through the nose that acknowledges the joke without committing to it.

A doctor walks through the waiting room. Not Frank's doctor. Not here for us. My pulse spikes and settles. This will happen six more times before the procedure is done.

"Thank you for coming," Diane says. "I know it's a drive."

"It's not a drive."

"It's a drive, Nate. You live forty minutes away."

"So it's a drive. I don't mind."

She picks up the knitting again. Click, click. Green yarn feeding through her fingers.

"He talks about you," she says. "More than he'd want you to know. He's very proud of you. He won't say that because he's Frank, but I'm saying it because someone should."

I press my palms against my thighs. The plastic chair is cold through my jeans. The fluorescent lights buzz their eternal tuneless hum. Someone pages a doctor over the intercom — Dr. Ramirez to cardiology, Dr. Ramirez — and the name hangs in the air and dissolves.

"He changed my life," I say. It's not enough. It's not close to enough. But the real version of it — that Frank Deluca is the only reason I survived the loneliness of knowing, that his garage and his bad chili and his blunt refusal to let me disappear into the work are the architecture that holds me up — that version is too large and too true for a hospital waiting room, so I say the small version and hope she hears the rest.

"I know," she says. "He knows too."

Elena comes back with three coffees in paper cups. She hands one to Diane, one to me, sits on my other side. The coffee is bad. Hospital cafeteria bad, burnt and thin. Elena drinks hers like it's fine because Elena is not a woman who complains about coffee during a cardiac procedure.

We wait. The television shows weather. A commercial for car insurance. Another commercial for a medication whose side effects take longer to list than its benefits. Diane knits. Elena reads something on her phone. I sit between them and try not to do math.

Forty minutes. Then an hour. Then an hour and ten.

At an hour and fifteen, Frank's cardiologist comes through the double doors. Dr. Sato, short and precise, the kind of person who conveys competence through economy of movement. She finds us in the waiting room and I'm on my feet before she's within speaking distance.

"Went well," she says. "Clean placement. Good blood flow. He's in recovery."

Diane's knitting needles stop. She exhales. It's a small sound, private, and I look away because it's not mine to witness.

"Can we see him?" Diane asks.

"Give us about twenty minutes. He's groggy but he'll want to see you."

Dr. Sato gives us the details — medications, recovery timeline, follow-up appointments. Diane asks precise questions. Elena takes notes on her phone without being asked. I stand there and let the information wash over me like tide over rock. He's fine. The word I was waiting for. Fine.

When Dr. Sato leaves, Diane sits back down. She picks up her knitting, puts it down. Picks it up again. Sets it in her bag.

"Well," she says.

"Well," I say.

She reaches over and takes my hand. Her grip is firm and warm and slightly trembling. She holds on with both hands, the way you hold something you're afraid of dropping.

"He's going to be fine," she says.

I nod. And I hold on tighter, because I know what fine means and what it costs. In the first timeline, fine was temporary. In the first timeline, fine was the preamble to a decline that took four years and ended in a room like this one, under lights like these, and the man in the bed didn't get up.

But this is not the first timeline. This is the one I built, piece by piece, year by year. This is the timeline where I was here when it happened. Where Diane didn't sit alone. Where Frank came out of the procedure and would, twenty minutes from now, open his eyes and say something irritable about the hospital gown, and Diane would cry, and Elena would hand her a tissue, and I would stand at the foot of the bed and feel the specific gravity of still having him.

Fine is not forever. I know this better than anyone alive. Fine is borrowed. Fine is a loan against a future that charges interest.

But every minute of fine is earned, and I intend to earn as many as I'm given.

---

Frank comes home two days later. He's on medication and a modified diet and strict orders to take it easy, which he interprets as permission to resume all normal activities at a slightly reduced pace. Diane and I form an unspoken alliance to enforce the actual instructions.

Elena drives me home from the hospital the night of the procedure. It's late, past eleven, and the highway is empty. She drives with one hand on the wheel and one on the gearshift and doesn't try to fill the silence with talk.

Somewhere past the Route 7 exit, she says, "That's what you've been afraid of."

"What?"

"Losing him. Not in the catastrophe. Now. Before any of it."

I look out the window. Bare trees. Dark sky. A gas station glowing on the shoulder like a small emergency.

"In the other timeline," I say, "he died at sixty-eight."

"You told me."

"This is how it started. The first event. Mild MI, stent. And then it got worse."

"But you changed things."

"I changed some things. I can't change his arteries."

She's quiet for a mile. Two miles. Then: "You can't save everyone."

"I know that."

"Do you?"

I don't answer. She takes the exit toward home. The car fills with the amber pulse of the turn signal.

"You built something real," she says. "NEORAN. The network. Chen's research. All of it. That's not nothing."

"It's not enough."

"It never will be. That's not the point."

She pulls into the parking lot. Kills the engine. Turns to me in the dark car. Her face is lit by the apartment building's security light, half in shadow, half in gold.

"The point is you're still building," she says. "You didn't stop. You didn't quit after the first timeline. You didn't quit when Chen didn't believe you. You didn't quit when Frank had the stent two years ago or when I walked out or when the models got worse. You keep building."

"Because there's no alternative."

"There's always an alternative. You could stop. You don't." She opens her door. The dome light comes on, unflattering and honest. "That's not stubbornness. That's something else. I haven't figured out the word for it yet."

She gets out. I sit in the car for another thirty seconds, in the dome light, in the parking lot, in the version of the world I built because the original one ended.

Then I get out and follow her inside, because she's right. There's always an alternative, and I keep choosing this one — the one where I show up, and the people I love are still here, and fine is the best word in the language, and every minute of it is a minute I refuse to waste.
