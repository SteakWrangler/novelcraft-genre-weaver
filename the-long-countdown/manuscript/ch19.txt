My phone rings at 12:47 AM and I know before I look that it's bad news. Good news sleeps through the night. Good news keeps business hours.

Sophie's name on the screen. I'm already sitting up, already reaching for my jeans on the floor.

"Hey." My voice comes out rough. I clear it. "Soph?"

Nothing for a second. Then breathing. The particular breathing of someone trying very hard to sound normal and failing at every level.

"Nate." Her voice is small. Not Sophie-small — Sophie doesn't do small. This is something else. This is a person stripped down past the performance. "I need — can you —"

"Where are you?"

"I don't — outside Grinnell. There's a gas station. Casey's, I think. Off I-80."

"Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Are you safe?"

"Yes. I'm — yes."

I'm already pulling a shirt over my head, already patting the nightstand for my keys. "I'm coming. Two hours, maybe less. Stay there."

"Nate, you don't have to —"

"Stay there."

She makes a sound I've never heard from her. Something between a laugh and a collapse. "Okay."

I drive. The highway at one in the morning is its own country — dark fields on both sides, the occasional freight truck blasting past with its running lights like a cathedral on wheels. I keep it at eighty. The Civic shudders a little above seventy-five, but it holds.

She didn't call Frank. She didn't call Diane. She didn't call any of the friends she's accumulated over twenty-six years of being the dependable one, the straight-A one, the one who stayed close and called on Sundays and remembered birthdays without being reminded.

She called me.

I don't know what to do with that yet, so I drive.

---

The Casey's is the only thing lit for miles. That particular gas station glow — sodium vapor and fluorescent tube, the kind of light that makes everyone look like they're recovering from something. Bugs spiral against the canopy over the pumps. A semi idles in the back lot, its driver asleep or watching something on his phone.

Sophie's rental is parked crooked in the far corner, away from the lights but not quite in the dark. I pull in next to her. Through the window I can see her sitting in the driver's seat, hands on the wheel, staring straight ahead. Engine off.

I get out. Walk around. Tap on her window.

She unlocks the door but doesn't open it. I open it.

"Come on. My car. I'll buy you coffee."

She looks up at me. Mascara smudged under her left eye. Her hair's pulled back in a ponytail that's come half undone. She's wearing a nice blouse — she was out somewhere. Dinner, maybe. Drinks after. She looks twenty years old and seventy at the same time.

"It's gas station coffee," she says.

"I know what I said."

She almost smiles. Gets out. Follows me inside. I pour two cups from the machine by the hot dog roller. The coffee is burnt and has been sitting since whenever the night shift started. I hand her one. She wraps both hands around it.

We sit in my car. I leave the engine running for the heat — May, but the Iowa nights still bite. She's in the passenger seat with her knees drawn up, the coffee balanced on her kneecap. She hasn't looked at me directly since I got here.

"I got pulled over," she says.

"Okay."

"On 146, coming back from — I was visiting Amara. From school. She moved back to Newton last year."

"Okay."

"I had wine at dinner. I thought I was fine. The officer said I was weaving." She takes a breath. "I blew point-oh-seven."

"Under the limit."

"Under the limit." She repeats it like she's been repeating it to herself for the last hour. "He let me go. Said I should pull over and wait it out. Get some coffee." She looks down at the cup. "So."

"So."

Silence. The parking lot hums with that particular late-night frequency — the coolers inside, the lights above, the interstate a quarter mile away. A car passes on the road without slowing.

"I sat in the car for forty minutes," she says. "After he left. I couldn't stop shaking. I still can't." She holds out her hand. It trembles. "See?"

I see.

"I kept thinking, what if it had been point-oh-eight. What if I'd had one more glass. What if there'd been someone in the road, or —" She stops. Swallows. "I do this thing where I run the variables. All the ways it could have gone worse. I can't stop doing it."

"I know that thing."

"I know you do."

More silence. She sips the coffee. Makes a face.

"This is terrible."

"The worst."

"Why did you say you'd buy me coffee if you knew it was going to be gas station coffee?"

"Because you were sitting in a parking lot at one in the morning and I figured the quality of the coffee was secondary to the gesture."

She laughs. It's a real laugh, shaky but real, and it opens something in her chest — I can see it happen, the way the muscles around her jaw release, the way her shoulders drop an inch. And then she's crying. Not dramatically, not with sound. Tears running down her face while she holds the terrible coffee and stares out the windshield at the bugs circling the fluorescent light.

"I didn't think you'd come," she says.

"I will always come."

"It's two hours."

"I will always come, Soph."

She cries harder. I put my arm around her. She stiffens for a second — instinct, the old architecture of our relationship, the one where she holds me at arm's length because I've earned it — and then she lets go. Leans into me. Her forehead against my shoulder, her hand still gripping the coffee cup.

I don't say anything.

This is the thing I've learned, the thing that took me thirty-one years and two timelines and every mistake I've catalogued in my prediction box: sometimes the only thing required of you is to not leave. Not to fix. Not to explain. Not to offer the lesson or the perspective or the five-step plan. Just to remain in the seat, in the parking lot, in the silence, and let the other person fall apart without catching them. Let them hit the ground. Sit on the ground next to them.

She cries for a while. I hold her. The coffee goes cold in her hand.

Around 2 AM she pulls back, wipes her face with her sleeve, and says, "I've been drinking too much."

"Okay."

"Not like — I'm not — it's not every day. But three, four times a week. More than I should. More than I want to."

"Okay."

"I keep waiting for it to become a problem so I can justify doing something about it. Like I need permission from the universe to admit I'm struggling." She looks at me. "That's insane, right?"

"It's the most sane thing anyone's said to me in years."

"I'm supposed to be the one who has it together."

"Says who?"

"Says — everyone. Mom. Dad. Me. The whole — I'm the responsible one, Nate. That's the deal. You get to be the mess and I get to be the one who cleans it up."

"That's a terrible deal."

"I know." She's not crying anymore. Her voice is flat, factual. "I've been running on that deal since I was twelve and you came home from your road trip to wherever and Mom was so relieved she forgot I'd won the state science fair that week. And I decided, fine. I'll be the one who doesn't need anything. I'll be the one who's fine."

"I'm sorry."

"I'm not telling you this to make you feel guilty."

"I know. I'm still sorry."

She looks at me for a long time. "You've changed," she says. "I keep waiting for the other shoe, but you've actually changed."

"I'm trying."

"No, you have. The old Nate would've driven here and turned it into a speech about his own recovery. You haven't mentioned yourself once."

I hadn't noticed. But she's right. I hadn't been counting the minutes or formulating advice or preparing the monologue about my own near-miss with the whiskey in the Iowa City bar. I'd just been sitting here.

"I don't want to go to rehab," she says. "I don't think I need that. I think I need to stop pretending I'm fine and tell someone I'm not fine."

"You just did."

"I just did." She exhales. Looks at the coffee cup like she's seeing it for the first time. "This really is the worst coffee I've ever had."

"Top three for me."

"What's worse than this?"

"There was a truck stop outside Dubuque in 2016 that served something I'm not fully convinced was coffee. Could have been motor oil. Could have been a cry for help."

She laughs again. Easier this time.

We sit there until 3 AM. She tells me about her program, the parts she doesn't post on social media — the burnout, the imposter syndrome, the professor who told her she lacked ambition because she wanted to do community health instead of policy research. She tells me about a relationship that ended badly in March, a guy who was nice on paper and empty in practice. She tells me she's been having panic attacks since January and hasn't told anyone.

I listen. That's all. I listen and I ask questions and I don't offer solutions. When she says something that sounds like she's testing me — waiting for the judgment, the advice, the older-brother routine — I just nod.

At a quarter past three, she yawns. "I should sleep."

"Follow me back? There's a motel off the exit near my place. Or you can take the couch."

"The couch."

"Okay."

She reaches over and squeezes my hand. Quick, firm, and then gone. "Thank you."

"Anytime. And I mean that literally. Three AM, four AM. Wednesday afternoon. I don't care."

"I know." She opens the car door. The night air comes in, cool and smelling like cut grass and diesel. "That's the weird part. I actually know."

I follow her taillights back to Cedar Rapids. She drives carefully, both hands on the wheel, five miles under the speed limit. I match her pace. Two cars on the empty highway, holding formation.

---

I spend two weeks after that thinking about what it means to tell the truth.

Not the philosophical version. The practical one. The version where you stand in front of someone you love and say the thing that will make you sound insane, because the alternative — the continued silence, the maintained lie, the protective omission — has already cost you everything it can cost.

Elena lives in Iowa City now. Same town, different apartment. She moved after the separation — couldn't stay in the house we'd shared, which I understood. I drive past her building sometimes on the way to meetings. Not on purpose, or at least not entirely on purpose. Her light is on in the evenings. She has plants on the windowsill that she didn't have when we were together.

I rehearse what I'm going to say. I rehearse it in the shower, in the car, standing in the kitchen at midnight eating cereal over the sink. I rehearse it so many times it stops sounding like words and becomes a kind of music, abstract and meaningless.

I'm going to tell her the truth.

Not the whole truth. Not "I died in 2037 and woke up in my eleven-year-old body and have been living a second life guided by predictions I wrote down in a shoebox." That truth would end the conversation before it started.

But a version. The version that's closest to the shape of the thing without being the thing itself.

On a Tuesday in early June, I drive to her apartment. I park on the street. I sit in the car for eleven minutes. I know it's eleven because I watch the dashboard clock the way you watch an approaching wave — with the full knowledge that you're going to get hit and the irrational hope that maybe this time you won't.

I get out. I walk up. I knock.

She opens the door and the first thing I notice is that she's cut her hair. Shorter, to her jaw. It makes her look sharper, more herself, like she'd been wearing the long hair as a concession to someone else's idea of her and finally stopped.

"Nate."

"Hi."

She leans against the doorframe. Arms crossed. Not hostile — assessing. Elena has always looked at me like she's reading a page that's been partially redacted, trying to fill in the blacked-out words from context.

"It's nine o'clock on a Tuesday," she says.

"I know."

"You didn't call."

"If I'd called, I would have talked myself out of it."

A pause. She steps back. Not an invitation exactly, but an absence of refusal. I step inside.

Her apartment is small and clean. Books everywhere — on shelves, on the table, stacked on the floor by the couch. A mug of tea on the kitchen counter, still steaming. She was reading. I've interrupted her reading, which in Elena's hierarchy of activities ranks somewhere between breathing and sleeping.

"Do you want tea?" she asks.

"No. Thank you."

"Sit down."

I sit on the couch. She sits in the chair across from me. The coffee table between us is covered in medical journals and a laptop with sticky notes framing the screen. She's been studying. She's always been studying.

"I rehearsed this," I say. "For about two weeks. I had a whole speech. It was — it was pretty good, actually. Structured. Built to a point. I was going to ease into it."

"And now?"

"Now I'm here and none of it works."

She waits. Elena is the best waiter I've ever known. She can hold a silence longer than anyone, not as a weapon but as an offering — here is space; fill it when you're ready.

"When you found the box," I say. "The predictions. I told you it was a creative writing project."

"I remember."

"That was a lie."

"I know."

"I mean — yes. Obviously. But I want to say it plainly. It was a lie, and I chose it because the truth sounded impossible, and I decided that protecting you from the impossible truth was more important than being honest. And I was wrong."

She doesn't move. Doesn't uncross her arms. But something shifts behind her eyes — a door opening an inch.

"The truth," I say, and here it is, the wave I've been watching. "I have knowledge of something catastrophic that's coming. I've had this knowledge since I was a kid. The predictions in that box aren't fiction — they're things I knew were going to happen. The financial crisis. The pandemic. Other things. And there's something bigger coming, and I've spent my adult life preparing for it."

I stop. The silence in the room is total. Even the refrigerator has quit humming, as if the apartment itself is holding its breath.

"I couldn't tell you," I say. "Not because I didn't trust you. Because if I told you, you'd think I was delusional or dangerous or both, and I couldn't — I couldn't lose you to the truth so I lost you to the lie instead, and it turns out both roads end up in the same place."

She's studying me. That look. The one that peels back every layer of performance and evasion and lands on whatever's actually underneath.

"I'm not asking you to believe me," I say. "I'm not asking you to come back. I just — you deserved to know why. Not the cover story. The actual why."

More silence. A car passes on the street below. Someone laughs in the apartment next door, muffled through the wall.

"That's it?" she says.

"That's it."

"That's the speech you rehearsed for two weeks?"

"The rehearsed version was better. It had transitions."

Something happens at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. The ghost of one. The place a smile goes to think about whether it wants to exist.

"You are," she says slowly, "the most honest liar I have ever met."

"I don't know what to do with that."

"I don't believe the world is ending, Nate."

"I know."

"I think you've built a story that makes sense of something in you that doesn't make sense otherwise. The intensity. The focus. The way you've lived since you were a teenager, like you're running out of time. I think the story is your way of organizing that."

"That's a reasonable interpretation."

"But." She unfolds her arms. Places her hands on her knees. "I believe you believe it. And I believe you're sorry. And those are the two things I spent three years needing to hear."

The wave hits. I don't cry — not quite — but something breaks loose in my chest, some calcified knot of holding that I've been carrying so long I forgot it was there. My eyes sting. I look at the floor.

"I'm sorry," I say. "For the lie. For all the lies. For every time you asked what was wrong and I said nothing."

"I know."

"I should have told you from the beginning. I should have let you decide whether it was too much. Instead I decided for you."

"Yes. You did."

"I'm sorry for that."

She's quiet for a while. Then she stands up, goes to the kitchen, and pours me a cup of tea I didn't ask for. Sets it in front of me. Sits back down.

"Drink your tea," she says.

I drink the tea.

"I left," she says, "because I found out the person I loved had been lying to me about something fundamental for our entire relationship. Not because of what was in the box. Because of the look on your face when I asked about it. You were so practiced. The lie came so easily. And I thought — what else? What else has come that easily?"

"Nothing. Elena, there was nothing else —"

"I know that now. I didn't know it then." She picks up her own mug. Holds it in both hands the way Sophie held the gas station coffee. "I've had three years to think about it. And what I keep coming back to is that you weren't lying to protect yourself. You were lying to protect something else — this mission, this thing you're carrying. And the tragedy of it is that you thought those were the same thing."

I don't have an answer for that. She's right. She's precisely, surgically right, and the precision of it is both a wound and a relief.

"Choosing the mission over us was wrong," I say. "I need you to know I see that now."

"I see that you see it. That's not nothing."

We talk until midnight. Not about the predictions, not about the catastrophe, not about the future. About us. About the three years apart. About what she's been doing — she finished her residency, started a practice in Iowa City, finally got a cat. About what I've been doing — NEORAN, the council work, Sophie's call. When I tell her about the gas station parking lot, about Sophie crying into my shoulder, she goes still.

"She called you," Elena says.

"She called me."

"She wouldn't have done that three years ago."

"No."

Elena looks at me. Something recalibrates. I can almost hear it — the quiet click of a revised assessment.

"I'm not coming back to pretend nothing happened," she says.

"I wouldn't want you to."

"I'm serious, Nate. If I — if we try this again, it's on harder terms. No omissions. No protective lies. No deciding what I can and can't handle."

"Okay."

"And I want to see everything. The box. The predictions. Whatever you've been working on. All of it."

"All of it."

"If I'm going to be in this, I'm going to be in it with full information. Even if the information is that the person I love has structured his life around something I don't understand."

"Okay."

"Okay."

She doesn't kiss me. Doesn't touch me. She walks me to the door and stands there with her arms crossed again, but differently this time — not a wall, a frame. Holding herself together while she lets something new in.

"Good night, Nate."

"Good night."

I drive home. I don't play music. The silence is enough.

---

She moves back in on a Saturday in October. Not all at once — a carload of books, some clothes, the cat. The cat's name is Leonard, and he regards me with the specific contempt of an animal who has correctly assessed that his owner's taste in men is questionable.

The first night, we lie in bed on opposite sides. Not touching. Not talking. The space between us is maybe eighteen inches, and it might as well be a canyon. I can hear her breathing. She can hear mine. We're two people who know each other's bodies completely, lying side by side like strangers on a train.

"This is weird," she says.

"Yeah."

"It's going to be weird for a while."

"I know."

"Okay." She turns on her side, facing away from me. "Good night."

"Good night."

I stare at the ceiling. Leonard jumps up on the bed and settles exactly in the middle of the gap between us, as if he's been hired as an eighteen-pound feline DMZ. I close my eyes. I don't sleep, and I don't think she does either, but we stay. That counts. That's enough for now.

Over the next few weeks, the distance narrows. Not in dramatic gestures — in small, accretive moments. She leaves her coffee mug in the sink and I wash it without comment. I leave my shoes by the door and she moves them to the closet without complaint. We develop a new set of habits, laid over the old ones like a palimpsest, familiar and foreign at the same time.

She asks questions. Not all at once, but steadily, like someone doing research. "When did the predictions start?" Over dinner. "How accurate have they been?" While we're grocery shopping. "Who else knows?" On a walk around the neighborhood, Leonard watching from the window with judgment in his eyes.

I answer everything. Every question, fully, without hedging. It feels like peeling off skin. It feels like relief.

On a Sunday night in November, she says, "Show me."

I get the box from the closet shelf where it's lived for twenty years. The shoebox, battered and soft at the corners, held closed with a rubber band that's been replaced four times since the original. I set it on the bed between us.

She looks at it. "This is it?"

"This is it."

"It's a shoebox."

"Nike Air Max 95. My mom bought them for me in sixth grade."

"The fate of the world is in a Nike shoebox."

"If it helps, they were very comfortable shoes."

She doesn't laugh. She reaches out and touches the lid. Runs her finger along the edge. Then she looks at me, and I see in her face something I haven't seen before — not skepticism, not pity, not the clinical assessment she uses on patients. Fear. She's afraid of what's inside, not because she thinks the predictions are real but because she knows that whatever's in this box is the thing that's shaped the man she loves, and seeing it will change her understanding of him permanently, and there's no going back from that.

"You're sure?" I say.

"Open it."

I pull off the rubber band. I lift the lid.

Inside: the folded notebook pages, yellowed now at the edges. The index cards with dates and events written in my eleven-year-old handwriting, which gives way to my teenage handwriting, which gives way to the cramped precise script I developed in my twenties. The margins of the notebook are filled with annotations, cross-references, probability assessments. It looks like the work of a brilliant person or a disturbed one, and I've never been sure which.

Elena picks up the first page. Reads it. Picks up the next. Reads it.

She reads for a long time. I sit on my side of the bed and watch her face. She's careful with the pages, turning each one like it's a specimen. Occasionally she pauses, goes back, rereads. Once she pulls out her phone and checks something — a date, I think. A confirmation.

I wait. This is what it looks like when you hand someone the core of your life and let them examine it at their own pace. It looks like waiting. It looks like the space between the question and the answer, stretched thin.

After almost an hour, she sets the pages down. She doesn't put them back in the box. She leaves them spread out on the comforter between us — the predictions, the dates, the journal entries, all of it exposed to the air for the first time in years.

"The pandemic," she says.

"Yes."

"You wrote this in — this is dated 2004. You were twelve."

"Eleven. My birthday's in December."

"You wrote about a respiratory pandemic in 2020. Originating in a Chinese wet market."

"Yes."

She stares at the page. Then at me. Then back at the page.

"I want to tell you how that's possible," I say. "I want to explain it in a way that makes sense. But there isn't a way that makes sense. All I can tell you is that it's real, and I've spent twenty years acting on it, and there's something else coming that's worse."

She picks up another page. The one about the Kessari impact. She reads it twice. Three times.

"If you're right," she says quietly, "I want to help."

"Elena —"

"If you're right, I want to help. And if you're wrong —" She puts the page down. Looks at me. "If you're wrong, I've wasted my life."

"That's my line."

"It's mine now too."

Something shifts in the room. Not the distance between us — that's still there, still measurable, still real. But the quality of it changes. Before, the gap was filled with everything I hadn't said. Now it's filled with everything I have, and she's choosing to sit with it anyway.

I don't close the box. I leave it open, the pages spread out on the bed, the rubber band coiled on the nightstand like a shed skin. Elena reaches across the gap — not far, just a few inches — and puts her hand on mine. A light pressure. An anchor.

Leonard jumps down from the bed, apparently disgusted by the sentimentality. His collar bell jingles as he stalks into the hallway.

We sit there in the lamplight, the open box between us, the pages exposed. Twenty years of predictions, twenty years of secrets, laid out on a comforter in a house in Cedar Rapids while the November wind scrapes the branches against the window. She's reading another page. I'm watching her read.

For the first time, I do not close the box.
