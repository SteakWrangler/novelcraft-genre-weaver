The sky is wrong at 6:14 in the morning.

I know because I've been watching it since three, sitting on the loading dock behind the Iowa City hub with a thermos of coffee and a sat-phone and the kind of stillness that only comes when you've waited eighteen years for a single day. Chen's atmospheric monitors flagged the particulate shift at midnight Pacific time — trace amounts, barely registering, the kind of reading you'd dismiss as sensor drift if you didn't know what you were looking for.

I know what I'm looking for.

The color starts at the eastern horizon. Not sunrise-wrong, not storm-wrong. A flattening. Like someone laid a sheet of wax paper over the sky and the light behind it just — stopped trying. Gray-yellow. The color of old teeth. The color of a bruise five days into healing, when it stops hurting but keeps reminding you.

I've seen it before. Waterloo, 2008, the first time. I was seventeen and drunk and alone in an apartment that smelled like carpet cleaner and burnt ramen, and I watched the sky go this color through a window I hadn't cleaned in three months. I remember thinking the hangover was worse than usual. I remember thinking the light was wrong but not caring, because everything was wrong, because I was seventeen and had already lived one life and thrown it away and was slouching through the second one like a man wearing someone else's clothes.

That was the first time the world ended.

This is the second time. The difference is everything.

My phone buzzes. Chen.

"Nate." His voice is flat with controlled precision, the way it gets when the data confirms the thing he hoped the data wouldn't confirm. "Particulate density just crossed the threshold. We're at point-seven micrograms per cubic meter over the Pacific monitoring stations and climbing. Rate of increase matches the model exactly."

"How long?"

"Six hours to critical atmospheric density over the continental US. Twelve to global saturation. The photosynthetic collapse cascade begins—"

"At critical density. I know." I stand up. My knees ache. Thirty-five years old and my knees ache like I'm fifty, like the body knows what the mind has carried. "Activate everything."

There's a pause. Half a second. The pause of a man who built monitoring systems for a scenario he believed in intellectually but never quite believed in his marrow.

"Activating," Chen says.

I go inside.

---

The hub is a converted warehouse on Gilbert Street that we leased two years ago through a shell nonprofit. From the outside it looks like nothing — beige siding, gravel lot, a sign that reads HEARTLAND COMMUNITY RESILIENCE PROJECT in letters designed to be forgettable. Inside, it's a nerve center. Banks of communication equipment. Maps on every wall — Iowa, the Midwest, the national NEORAN network, each node marked in blue. Supply manifests. Medical staging areas. Twelve cots along the back wall for staff who've been pulling overnight shifts since the atmospheric readings started trending three days ago.

Elena is already up. She's standing at the medical triage station, inventorying supplies with the kind of focused calm that made her the best ER nurse in the University of Iowa system before she left to run our medical operations full-time. Her hair is pulled back. She's wearing scrubs and the fleece vest I bought her in January that she said was ugly and then wore every day for two months.

She looks up when I come in.

"It's today," I say.

She doesn't ask how I know. She stopped asking how I know a long time ago — not because she understands (she doesn't, not fully, no one does) but because she trusts the pattern. Eighteen years of predictions that came true. Eighteen years of Nate saying *this will happen* and then it happens. At some point, trust becomes its own kind of understanding.

"I'll start the call tree for medical volunteers," she says.

"Thank you."

She crosses the room and puts her hand on my chest. Not a hug. Not a kiss. Just her palm over my sternum, feeling my heartbeat, the gesture she started doing two years into our relationship when she realized that my calm wasn't calm — that underneath the steady voice and the organized plans, my heart rate sits at a hundred and ten on days like this. Days when the timeline closes in.

"Breathe," she says.

I breathe.

She goes to work.

---

The phone trees fire at 6:22 AM Central. Forty-seven NEORAN coordinators in eleven states receive the activation code — a simple text, pre-arranged, unremarkable to anyone who might intercept it: *Garden report ready for review.* Within fifteen minutes, each coordinator begins their local cascade. Phone calls, texts, door knocks. In small towns across the Midwest, people who've been training for this — who attended workshops they half-believed in, who stocked supplies they thought they'd probably never need — start moving.

I watch it happen on the board. Blue lights going green. Node after node reporting activation.

Sophie's voice comes over the internal channel, steady and precise. She's twenty-nine now, and she runs NEORAN communications the way she used to run the debate team at UNI — with absolute command and a low tolerance for wasted words.

"Cedar Falls gymnasium is opening now. Volunteers on-site, supplies staged. Des Moines node activated — they're running thirty minutes behind on volunteer mobilization but compensating. Grinnell node reports full shelter readiness."

"Marcus?"

"Checked in twenty minutes ago. Priya and Amara are settled at the Grinnell shelter. He's doing structural assessments on the secondary buildings."

Marcus. My best friend since college, the structural engineer who thought I was eccentric for ten years before he decided I was something else — not crazy, not prophetic, just *prepared* in a way that defied easy explanation. He took his wife and daughter to Grinnell two days ago because I asked him to. He didn't ask why the timing mattered. He just went.

"Chen, status on atmospheric tracking?"

Chen's voice comes through the satellite link from the monitoring station in Fort Collins. "Rate of increase holding steady. We'll hit critical density over Iowa by approximately one PM Central. Photosynthetic disruption will be visible within two hours after that."

Visible. He means the plants will start dying.

I know what that looks like. I've seen it twice now — once living through it, once in the memory that won't let go. Grass browning in fast-forward. Trees shedding leaves in June like a film of autumn played at thirty-two times speed. Gardens curling in on themselves, each leaf folding like fingers making a fist. The world clenching.

"National emergency broadcast?" I ask.

"Nothing yet. NOAA is reporting unusual atmospheric readings but no public advisory. My estimate is they'll issue something by late morning. Maybe noon."

Two hours after we've already moved everyone.

That was always the plan. Not to replace the institutions — they'll do what they do, slowly, cautiously, through chains of command designed for hurricanes and not for this. The plan was to be faster. To have the shelters open before the broadcast. To have people inside before the panic starts.

"All right," I say. "Phase two. Get everyone off the roads by ten."

---

By nine AM, the sky over Iowa City is the color of dirty dishwater and the temperature has dropped four degrees. People on the street are looking up. Not alarmed yet — confused. The light is wrong in a way that doesn't match any weather pattern, any experience, any frame of reference. I watch a woman on the sidewalk outside the hub stop walking, tilt her head back, and just stand there, mouth slightly open, trying to process what her eyes are telling her.

I want to go out and tell her. I want to say *come inside, it gets worse, I know because I've done this before.* But I can't save everyone on the sidewalk. That's not the plan. The plan is systems. The plan is networks. The plan is the hundred small towns where coordinators are already moving people into shelters stocked with food and water and medical supplies and the capacity to sustain for eight weeks without resupply.

Elena has her medical team staged. Four volunteer nurses, a retired paramedic, and a pharmacy tech who drove down from Waterloo at five this morning. They're organizing triage protocols for the intake that's about to start — our hub isn't the primary shelter, but it'll serve as overflow and medical staging for the Iowa City corridor.

My phone rings. A number I don't recognize.

"Is this — are you the emergency preparedness people? My neighbor gave me this number, she said—"

"Where are you?"

"Coralville. I have my kids, I don't know what's happening with the sky, the news isn't saying anything—"

"Go to the Coralville Recreation Center on Fifth Street. Tell them Sophie sent you. Bring water if you have it, but don't wait to pack. Just go now."

"Is it — what is it? What's happening?"

"Go now. They'll explain when you get there."

I hang up. Three more calls come in over the next ten minutes. The informal network — neighbors telling neighbors, the word-of-mouth layer that no communication system can replicate. People calling the number someone gave them at a community workshop six months ago, a number they saved in their phone and forgot about until the sky turned the wrong color on a Tuesday morning in June.

---

The national emergency broadcast hits at 11:47 AM.

I'm in the hub's main room when it cuts through every active channel — the familiar three-tone alert, the synthetic voice that sounds like it was designed to be calming and instead sounds like a machine trying not to scream.

*This is a national emergency broadcast. Unusual atmospheric conditions have been detected over the continental United States and globally. Citizens are advised to remain indoors. Close all windows and doors. Await further instructions from local authorities.*

That's it. That's all they have. *Unusual atmospheric conditions.* Like a weather advisory. Like a pollen alert.

The broadcast repeats twice, then cuts out. Static. Then silence.

In the first timeline, I was sitting on my apartment floor in Waterloo when that broadcast aired. I remember the silence after. I remember the way the static sounded like the world clearing its throat before delivering bad news. I remember opening a bottle of Jim Beam because it was the only response I had.

Now I'm standing in a room full of people who are looking at me, waiting.

"Keep working," I say. "We're two hours ahead of this. Stay on mission."

They stay on mission.

---

The vessels become visible at 12:34 PM.

I'm outside, checking the backup generator, when Sophie comes through the door with a look on her face I've never seen — and I've known Sophie since she was eleven years old, since she was a kid who read too many books and asked too many questions and once told me at Thanksgiving dinner that she wanted to be "the person who knows things before other people know things." She grew up to be exactly that. But right now she's not the person who knows things. She's a twenty-nine-year-old woman who just saw something in the sky that her education and her intelligence and her entire framework for understanding reality cannot accommodate.

"Nate," she says. "You need to come look."

I follow her around the side of the building to the parking lot, where four members of our team are standing in a loose cluster, all facing southwest. I follow their sight line.

They're there.

Not dramatic. That's the thing nobody will get right in the retellings, the news footage, the documentaries that will be made if there's anyone left to make documentaries. The Kessari vessels are not dramatic. They are not sleek or angular or glowing. They are vast and matte and dark, the color of wet concrete, and they hang in the sky with the indifference of weather. They are industrial. Functional. They have no windows, no lights, no markings. They look like someone built a factory and forgot to put it on the ground.

There are three visible from where we're standing. More, I know, are elsewhere — over every major landmass, positioned at intervals that have nothing to do with nations or borders or military installations and everything to do with agricultural yield and biomass density. They're not here to fight. They're not here to conquer. They're here to harvest. They will strip the photosynthetic capacity of this planet like a combine moving through wheat, and when they're done, they'll leave, and what remains will be a world that has to relearn how to feed itself.

The distinction between invasion and harvest is worse than any war. War implies an opponent. Harvest implies a crop.

Sophie is staring. Everyone is staring.

"Come inside," I say. "There's nothing to see out here that changes what we need to do."

"Nate." Sophie's voice is tight. "What *are* they?"

"They're called Kessari. That's the name they'll eventually give us for themselves, or the name we'll decode from their transmissions. It doesn't matter right now. What matters is that the atmospheric particulate they've released will collapse photosynthetic function in most plant species within the next six to twelve hours. That's the immediate threat. Not the ships. The air."

She looks at me. "You've known. This whole time."

"Yes."

"How?"

"I'll tell you later. I promise. Right now I need you on communications. We have ninety-six nodes to coordinate and the cell networks are going to get unreliable within the hour."

She stares at me for three more seconds. Then she goes inside. Because Sophie, like everyone I've spent eighteen years carefully, deliberately, painstakingly surrounding myself with, is the kind of person who does the work first and asks the questions after.

---

By two PM, the plant die-off is visible.

I'm standing at the window of the hub, watching the strip of grass between our building and the road. It's browning. Not the slow brown of drought or the patchy brown of disease. A uniform darkening, like a bruise spreading under skin, the green leaching out in real time. If I watch for thirty seconds without blinking, I can see it happening — the color draining eastward, blade by blade, as the particulate density reaches the threshold where chloroplasts simply stop functioning.

Across the street, a young oak tree drops three leaves. Then five. Then a dozen. They fall with no wind, in the dead still air, spiraling down with an almost gentle precision. I've seen this before. In the first timeline, I watched a maple tree outside my apartment window shed its entire canopy in forty-five minutes. The leaves curled as they fell — edges folding inward, like fingers making a fist. Like the tree was trying to hold onto something and couldn't.

The oak across the street is doing the same thing now.

Behind me, someone — one of the volunteers, a college kid named Travis who signed up for NEORAN because his girlfriend thought it was cool — says, "Oh God." Very quietly. Like a prayer he didn't mean to say out loud.

"Stay focused," I say, without turning from the window. "This is expected. This is what we prepared for."

"But the *trees*—"

"The trees are dying. Yes. The food supply chain built on photosynthesis is collapsing. That's why we have stored supplies, seed banks, and hydroponic plans. That's why you spent three weekends last month loading canned goods into a basement in North Liberty. This moment right here is why."

Travis doesn't say anything else. I hear him sit down and pick up his radio.

I keep watching the oak tree. Leaf by leaf. Fist by fist.

---

The calls from other cities start coming in around three.

Cedar Rapids: overwhelmed. The emergency shelters opened forty minutes ago, two hours after ours, and they're already over capacity. People are panicking. There was a fight at a gas station on First Avenue. Someone drove a truck through the front window of a Walmart.

Des Moines: our NEORAN node is operational, holding steady, but the city around it is fracturing. Interstate 80 is gridlocked with people trying to drive — somewhere. Anywhere. The universal human impulse to flee, even when there's nowhere to go.

Chicago: bad. We don't have a strong node there. Sophie gets a call from a contact at Northwestern who says the lakefront is packed with people staring at the sky and the National Guard has been deployed to three neighborhoods and there aren't enough of them.

Kansas City: worse.

I listen to each report. I write the essentials on the board. I don't editorialize.

This is the contrast. This is the argument the last eighteen years have been building toward, and I don't need to articulate it because the evidence is doing the work. In places with preparation — with networks, with relationships, with shelters opened before the broadcast, with people who know each other's names and have practiced this and trust the plan — there is fear, but there is function. In places without it, there is chaos. The difference is not technology. It's not money. It's not intelligence or foresight or strategic genius. The difference is that someone showed up, year after year, and said *let's be ready together,* and enough people said yes.

Preparation is a relationship. It always was.

Elena comes over at three-fifteen. She's been in the medical staging area for nine straight hours, and she looks it — the skin under her eyes is dark, her scrubs have a coffee stain on the left thigh, her hair is escaping its tie in a way she'd normally fix but can't be bothered to now.

"Three anxiety attacks, one asthma exacerbation from the particulate, one laceration from a guy who cut himself loading supplies," she reports. "Nothing critical. The intake is slowing — I think everyone who's coming is here."

"How are you?"

She considers the question. Not performing consideration — actually weighing an honest answer. "Terrified," she says. "Also weirdly calm, because we're doing the thing. It's not theoretical anymore. We're doing the thing and it's working." She pauses. "You?"

"The same."

"Liar."

"Okay. Terrified, calm, and something else I don't have a word for. The feeling of watching something happen for the second time and it being completely different."

She puts her hand on my chest again. The heartbeat check. I know it's high. She knows it's high. Neither of us says the number.

"I'll be in triage," she says, and goes back.

---

Derek Holt finds me outside the shelter at ten minutes after four.

I've stepped out for air — bad air, particulate-heavy air that tastes like copper and chalk, but air nonetheless. My lungs need to expand. My chest needs space. I've been in the hub for twelve hours and the walls have started to feel like they're leaning inward, and I know it's not claustrophobia, it's the accumulated weight of being the person everyone looks to, the man with the plan, the one who knew.

The parking lot is quiet. The sky is a uniform gray — no sun visible, no blue, no variation. Just gray, from horizon to horizon, like someone pulled a tarp over the world. The oak across the street is bare. In June. The grass is the color of old rope.

I hear footsteps on gravel. I turn.

Derek looks wrong. Not disheveled — Derek wouldn't be disheveled if you pulled him from a car wreck. He's wearing the same kind of clothes he always wears: good jeans, a quarter-zip, shoes that cost more than my first car. His hair is where it's supposed to be. But his face. His face has come loose from its usual architecture. The angles that normally project confidence and control have slackened into something I've never seen on him before.

He's afraid. Derek Holt, who built a portfolio that survived three market crashes, who plays every human interaction like a chess game, who has never in his life encountered a problem that couldn't be solved with leverage or capital or the right phone call — Derek Holt is afraid.

"Nate."

"Derek."

He stops eight feet away. Like he's not sure whether to come closer. Like the distance is a decision he's making and unmaking and making again.

"My phone doesn't work," he says. "The network went down an hour ago. I drove here from my house because I didn't know where else to go." He stops. Recalibrates. I can see him recalibrating — the old machinery trying to engage, the transactional calculus trying to find a frame for this situation. *What do I have. What do they need. How do I position.* But the machinery is grinding on nothing. He has nothing anyone needs. His money is a number on a screen connected to servers that may or may not be running. His real estate portfolio is buildings sitting under a gray sky full of alien particulate. His network of investors and politicians and power brokers is a phone tree with no phones.

"You can come in," I say. "There's space. Food. Medical if you need it."

He shakes his head. Not refusing — processing. "You did this," he says. "This — shelter, this operation. This is yours."

"It's not mine. It's a lot of people's."

"But you built it."

"I helped build it. Yes."

He takes two steps closer. His voice drops, not to a whisper but to something private, something meant only for the space between us.

"You *knew.*" Not a question. "You knew this was coming. For eighteen years."

"Yes."

"And you spent that time — what? Making amends? Building shelters in Iowa?"

"Yes."

He stares at me. The fear on his face is evolving into something more complex — not understanding, not quite, but the raw material of understanding. The preliminary demolition before construction.

"You could have been a king," he says.

The word lands in the gray air between us and stays there. King. The word Derek would use, because Derek's framework sorts the world into those who rule and those who serve and he has never questioned which category a mind like mine belongs in. He's right, in his way. Eighteen years of foreknowledge. Perfect market predictions. Political leverage. Technological advantage. The ability to be first, to be right, to position yourself above every other human being on the planet. I could have been richer than anyone alive. I could have owned governments. I could have built a bunker so deep and so stocked that I'd survive the Kessari harvest in absolute comfort while the world above burned.

I did that. In the first timeline, I did exactly that — not all of it, but enough. Enough to know the shape of it. Enough to know where that road leads.

"I was," I say. "In the first version. It wasn't worth it."

Derek's mouth opens slightly. Closes. He looks at me like I've spoken in a language he almost recognizes — like the words are English but the grammar is from somewhere he's never been.

He can't comprehend it. I see him trying. I see the wheels turn and catch and slip. He is a man who has optimized every decision in his life for advantage, and I'm telling him I had the ultimate advantage and spent it on community shelters and canned food and relationships with people in small Iowa towns. I'm telling him the crown was real and I put it down. I'm telling him the throne room was empty.

He looks at the shelter. The lights are on inside — warm, yellow, visible through the windows. People moving. The sound of a child crying, a radio crackling, someone laughing at something, the low hum of collective human survival.

"Can I—" He pauses. "Can I come in?"

"I already said you could."

He nods. Doesn't move. Then: "What can I do? Inside. What can I do?"

This is the question. Not *what can I get.* Not *what's in it for me.* For the first time in the twenty years I've known Derek Holt, he is asking what he can contribute to something that will not return a profit.

"We need someone to organize the supply inventory in the back room. It's boring. It's physical. It requires counting things accurately."

"I can count," he says.

He walks past me. Through the door. Into the light.

I stand in the parking lot for another minute, alone, breathing the copper-chalk air, looking at the dead grass and the bare trees and the gray sky that will not clear. A vessel is visible in the southwest — a dark shape, impossibly large, hanging in the sky like a building that forgot how to fall.

I go back inside.

---

Frank arrives at 5:20 PM.

No call. No warning. Just the sound of a truck engine in the parking lot — the same 2004 F-150 he's been driving for twenty-two years, the one Diane keeps telling him to replace, the one he keeps fixing because "it runs fine, Di, leave my truck alone."

I'm at the communications desk when Sophie says, "There's a truck outside. Older guy. He's unloading something."

I go to the door.

Frank is standing at the tailgate. The truck bed is packed: blankets — heavy wool ones, the kind Frank buys at farm auctions because "they don't make them like this anymore." Cases of canned goods. Two five-gallon water jugs. A toolbox. A first-aid kit. A propane camp stove. A bag of dog food, even though Frank doesn't have a dog, because someone at the shelter might.

He's sixty-five. His back went out in March. He's lifting a case of green beans off the tailgate with the careful, stubborn movement of a man whose body has started filing objections that his will refuses to acknowledge.

"Frank."

He looks up. "Hey, Nate. Figured you could use some supplies."

"You drove down from Cedar Falls?"

"Wasn't bad. Roads are pretty empty. Everyone's staying put." He sets the case down. Reaches for another. "Diane's at the shelter up there. She's handing out blankets. Told her I'd bring the rest of the stock down to you."

Nobody called Frank. Nobody activated him. He's not in the NEORAN phone tree — he was never in the phone tree because he never signed up for anything officially, because Frank doesn't sign up for things. He just shows up. He's been showing up since the day I met him at the Cedar Falls hardware store eighteen years ago, a seventeen-year-old kid with haunted eyes buying weatherstripping, and Frank said, "You look like you could use a hand."

He's been showing up ever since.

I grab a case from the truck bed and carry it inside. We work in silence for three trips, unloading. On the fourth trip, Frank stops, leans against the tailgate, and looks at the sky.

"Ugly day," he says.

"Yeah."

"We'll manage."

He picks up the last box and walks inside.

---

Night.

The transition happens slowly — the gray sky darkening by degrees, from dishwater to slate to charcoal to the deep, featureless black of a sky with no stars. The particulate blocks everything. No moon. No constellations. No visual confirmation that there's a universe beyond the gray. Just black, and the vessels somewhere up there, and the faint wrongness of air that smells like metal.

Inside the hub, the lights are on.

Elena is still in triage, but the pace has slowed. She's sitting with an elderly woman — someone's grandmother, brought in from the south side — holding the woman's hand and explaining in calm, clear terms what the particulate is and isn't, what it will and won't do to human lungs, what the next twenty-four hours will look like. Elena's voice carries across the room like a frequency designed to reduce fear.

Sophie is at the communications desk, tracking reports from the network. Forty-three of our forty-seven nodes are operational and reporting stable conditions. Three went dark when the cell towers failed. One — the western Nebraska node — sent a final status update at six PM and hasn't been heard from since. Sophie logs each report with the same meticulous precision she's brought to everything since she was eleven years old. She'll worry about the silent nodes later. Right now, she's working.

Chen is still on satellite link from Fort Collins, monitoring the atmospheric data with the quiet relentlessness of a man who believes that understanding a catastrophe is the first step toward surviving it. His updates come every thirty minutes: particulate density, rate of photosynthetic collapse, estimated timeline for atmospheric stabilization. The numbers are bad. They're going to keep being bad for a long time. But the numbers are *known,* and knowing is its own kind of shelter.

Marcus called from Grinnell at seven PM. The shelter is holding. Priya and Amara are safe. Amara — three years old, Marcus's daughter, my goddaughter — asked why the sky was gray, and Priya told her it was sleeping.

Derek is in the back room, counting cans. He's been counting cans for four hours. I checked on him once and found him sitting on the floor surrounded by organized rows of kidney beans and diced tomatoes and chicken broth, a legal pad in his lap with tidy columns of numbers, doing the most menial work of his life with a focus that bordered on devotion. He didn't look up when I came in. I didn't interrupt.

Frank is in the main room, handing out blankets. Moving between cots with the unhurried steadiness of a man for whom this is not a crisis response but simply the next thing to do. A woman wrapped in one of his wool blankets — one of the heavy farm-auction ones — is asleep on a cot near the wall. A child is eating crackers from a box Frank brought. A teenage boy is wearing a flannel jacket that I'm pretty sure is Frank's, because Frank is now in his undershirt, and it's cold.

This is the family. Not the word — the fact of it. Biological and chosen. Built over eighteen years with a patience that sometimes felt insane, that sometimes felt futile, that sometimes felt like building a sandcastle below the tide line and trusting that the sand would hold. Elena. Marcus. Sophie. Chen. Frank. Diane, up in Cedar Falls, handing out blankets in the gymnasium. Derek, in the back room, counting cans and encountering humility for the first time.

None of them knew the full truth. Some of them knew pieces. Elena knew the most — not the mechanism, not the timeline, but the shape of the knowledge, the weight of it, the reason I wake up at three AM and stare at the ceiling. She chose to stay anyway. She chose to be here, in this room, on this day, holding a stranger's hand and explaining atmospheric particulate with a calm she built from scratch because the man she loves couldn't tell her everything but told her enough.

That's the cost. Eighteen years of partial truth. Eighteen years of asking people to trust me without giving them the reason. The shelters are real. The network is real. The supplies are real. But the foundation of all of it is a story I can never fully tell — a first timeline, a first life, a version of me who saw the Kessari come and responded with selfishness and watched everything burn and was given, somehow, inexplicably, a chance to do it again.

I did it again.

---

At eleven PM, I step outside one more time.

The parking lot is dark. No streetlights — power grid failed at nine, but our generators are running. The light from the hub's windows spills out in yellow rectangles onto the dead grass. Above, the sky is black and starless and absolute.

It's quiet. Not peaceful — there's no peace in this. But quiet. The kind of quiet that follows a detonation, when the sound has finished and the air is still ringing at a frequency too low to hear but too present to ignore.

In the first timeline, this night was the worst of my life. Alone in Waterloo. Drunk. The power out, the sky black, the apartment dark, the bottle half-empty, the knowledge that the world had changed and I had done nothing about it sitting in my chest like a stone I couldn't cough up. I remember lying on the kitchen floor — actual linoleum, sticky with something I'd spilled days ago — and staring at the ceiling and thinking: *I knew. I could have done something. I did nothing.*

That was the first night of the long countdown. The beginning of the eighteen years I've been living since — the years of building, of connecting, of showing up in small towns and saying *let's get ready.* The years of Elena and Marcus and Sophie and Frank. The years of NEORAN, of shelters and supply caches and phone trees and the patient, grinding, unglamorous work of convincing people to prepare for something they couldn't see.

The preparation didn't stop the Kessari. Nothing could stop the Kessari. The vessels are still up there, doing what they came to do, and the plants are still dying, and the sky will stay gray for — I don't know how long. Months. Maybe longer. The world has changed, and the world will keep changing, and the loss is real and vast and I can feel it pressing against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

But the lights are on.

In Cedar Falls, the lights are on. In Iowa City, the lights are on. In Grinnell, where Marcus is holding his daughter, the lights are on. In forty-three communities across eleven states, the lights are on because someone showed up and did the work.

A sound from inside — laughter. Brief, startled, the kind that escapes before you can catch it. Someone laughing at something. On the worst night of the world, someone in the shelter is laughing, and the sound carries through the window and into the dead air of the parking lot like proof of something I spent eighteen years trying to believe.

That this version was worth it. That the choice was right. That the crown was heavy and the throne was empty and the kingdom was a room full of one, and this — this shelter, these people, this ugly beautiful imperfect network of human beings who showed up for each other — is the thing that was always supposed to be built instead.

The sky is gray. The sky will be gray tomorrow, and the day after, and for a long time after that. The world has ended for the second time.

In Cedar Falls, Iowa, in the place where I rebuilt myself, the lights are on.
