The thing about trust is that it's not a binary. It's not a switch you flip, on or off, believe or don't. Trust is a spectrum, and most of the interesting human drama happens in the gray middle where you trust someone enough to show up but not enough to stop watching the door.

Chen trusts me enough to answer the phone at eleven at night. She trusts me enough to run three independent analyses on a signal I told her to look for. She trusts me enough to list her name next to mine on a nonprofit application.

She does not trust me enough to stop asking how.

---

Her office at the University of Iowa is smaller than you'd expect for someone of her reputation. Two desks pushed together to form one large workspace, both buried under printouts and journals and the particular organized chaos of a mind that processes information faster than it can file it. Boltzmann occupies the only clear surface — the corner of the left desk — and watches me with the serene indifference of a creature who has never once doubted his own place in the universe.

It's a Tuesday afternoon in October. The leaves outside her window have gone the color of old pennies. Chen is sitting cross-legged in her office chair, which she does when she's about to say something she's been thinking about for days. I've learned her tells. She's learned mine. This is what four months of weekly meetings will do to two people who are fundamentally incapable of small talk.

"The scientific community dismissed it," she says. Not angry. Factual. The way you'd say the sky is blue or water is wet. "Ramirez at Caltech called it instrument noise. Whitfield at MIT didn't even respond to my email. Davidson responded, but only to suggest I recalibrate." She scratches Boltzmann behind the ears. He closes his eyes. "They're wrong."

"I know."

"You know." She studies me over the top of her glasses. Chen wears reading glasses now, thin silver frames that make her look like a librarian who could also dismantle your argument at the molecular level. "You know because you predicted it. You told me the frequency. You told me the coordinates. You told me the approximate amplitude. You were off by less than two percent on all three parameters."

"Yes."

"For a signal that no theoretical framework predicts."

"That's correct."

She uncrosses her legs. Crosses them the other way. Boltzmann adjusts without opening his eyes.

"Nate." She says my name like it's the first line of a proof she hasn't finished yet. "I've spent four months trying to find any possible way you could have derived that prediction from existing data. Any way at all. A model I haven't considered, a paper I haven't read, a dataset I don't have access to. I've gone through everything. ArXiv, JSTOR, internal preprints from three different research groups. I called in a favor from a colleague at CERN. Nothing. There is no path from publicly available information to the prediction you gave me."

"I know."

"Stop saying 'I know.' It's not helpful."

"Fair enough."

She takes off her glasses. Sets them on the desk. Boltzmann opens one eye, decides this doesn't concern him, closes it again.

"You're not guessing," she says. "You've never been guessing."

The room is quiet except for the hum of her desktop computer and the distant sound of undergraduates in the hallway, their voices carrying the particular carelessness of people who think the future is a long way off.

"No," I say. "I'm not guessing."

"Then what are you doing?"

I've rehearsed this. I've gone over it in my head dozens of times, standing in the shower, lying in bed, driving the forty minutes between Cedar Falls and Iowa City with the radio off and the window down. I've tried a hundred different phrasings. None of them are good enough for a physicist. None of them would be.

"I have foreknowledge of events I cannot explain the source of."

She stares at me.

"I know things that are going to happen. Some things. Not everything — there are gaps, inconsistencies, details I'm certain about and others I'm less sure of. But the signal was one of the certain ones. And I need you to trust the data, not the mechanism."

Chen doesn't move. Her face does something I've never seen it do before — it goes completely blank. Not angry, not skeptical, not curious. Just blank. The face of a woman whose entire framework for understanding reality has just been handed a variable it can't solve for.

"That," she says slowly, "is the opposite of science."

"I know."

"I told you to stop saying that."

"Sorry. But it's true. I know it's the opposite of science. I know it violates everything you believe about how knowledge works. I know you need mechanism, and I can't give it to you, and I know how maddening that is for someone who has spent her entire career insisting that the universe is knowable if you ask the right questions."

"Don't tell me what I believe."

"Okay."

"Don't tell me what I need, either."

"Okay."

She picks her glasses back up. Puts them on. Takes them off again. Boltzmann, sensing the disturbance in his immediate environment, stands up, turns in a circle, and lies back down facing the other direction.

"The data is clean," she says. "I've verified it three times. The signal is real. It corresponds exactly to what you described. And there is no conventional explanation for how you could have known it was there."

"That's all true."

"So either you have access to classified information from a source you can't reveal, which would make you some kind of intelligence asset, which frankly seems unlikely given that you're a twenty-year-old emergency management major from Cedar Falls—"

"Thanks."

"—or you're telling the truth, and you have foreknowledge of events through a mechanism neither of us understands. Which is, to put it mildly, not a hypothesis I can publish."

"I'm not asking you to publish it."

"Then what are you asking?"

"I'm asking you to keep working with me. To trust that the predictions I give you will continue to be accurate. To help me build something that can use that accuracy to save lives. I can't give you the why. I can give you the what."

She's quiet for a long time. Outside, the light has shifted. The pennies on the trees have gone copper, then bronze. Somewhere in the building, a door closes.

"I'm going to need more data points," she says.

"I can give you more data points."

"And I'm going to keep looking for the mechanism."

"I wouldn't expect anything less."

She reaches down and picks up Boltzmann, settling him in her lap like a gray, disinterested pillow. He tolerates this with the long-suffering patience of a cat who knows he is essential to his human's cognitive process.

"The thing that bothers me," she says, stroking Boltzmann's back, "is not that I can't explain it. I've spent my career working on problems I can't explain. That's the job. The thing that bothers me is that you're not surprised. You told me about a signal no one has ever detected, I confirmed it exists, and you were not surprised. Not even a little."

"No."

"Most people, if they made a prediction that extraordinary and it came true, would be... something. Excited. Scared. Vindicated. You were none of those things. You were relieved."

She's right. She's exactly right. When she called that night in June — eleven PM, her voice tight with something that wasn't quite excitement and wasn't quite fear — the feeling that went through me was the feeling of a load redistributed. Not lighter. Just shared.

"I've been carrying this alone for a long time," I say. "Having someone else see it — even one piece of it — that matters more than I can explain."

Chen looks at me over Boltzmann's head. Her expression has changed. The blankness is gone, replaced by something more complicated. The look of a woman who is choosing, deliberately and with full awareness of the cost, to step off solid epistemological ground.

"I don't trust you," she says. "I want to be clear about that. I don't trust people who withhold information. It's a professional hazard."

"That's fair."

"But I trust the data. And right now, the data says you're worth listening to. So I'll keep listening. But Nate—" She holds up one finger. Boltzmann bats at it. "If you're wrong about something, even once, in a way that suggests you've been fabricating or manipulating, we're done. Not angry done. Just done. I'll walk away and I won't look back."

"I understand."

"Good." She sets Boltzmann on the floor. He lands without sound and immediately begins grooming himself. "Now. You mentioned a nonprofit."

The thing about Frank Callahan is that he prepares.

He doesn't announce. He doesn't explain. He doesn't sit you down and say, "Son, I think we should spend some quality time together because I've been reflecting on our relationship and I believe there are things that need to be said." That's not how Frank operates. Frank operates by packing the truck the night before.

I come downstairs on a Saturday morning in late October and the cooler is already by the front door. The rods are in the bed of the truck, strapped down with bungee cords. There are sandwiches in a paper bag — ham and Swiss on white, mustard, no lettuce, because Frank considers lettuce on a fishing sandwich to be an affectation. The thermos is full. The truck is gassed up. He's sitting at the kitchen table in his canvas jacket, drinking coffee, reading the Courier, and he looks up when I come in and says, "Cedar River?"

Not "Do you want to go?" Not "I was thinking maybe we could." Just the destination. As if the decision has already been made and we're past the point of discussing it.

"Sure," I say.

"Coffee's on," he says.

I pour a cup. We don't say anything else. We don't need to.

---

The Cedar River at dawn in late October is a study in gray. Gray water, gray sky, gray mist that sits on the surface like something alive and breathing. The trees along the bank are mostly bare, their branches like cracks in the sky, and the few leaves that remain are the dull rust of things that have given up being beautiful and are just waiting to fall.

Frank backs the truck to within twenty feet of the bank, a spot he's been using since before I was born. There's a flattened patch of grass that might be natural and might be the accumulated memory of a hundred truck tires over thirty years. He kills the engine and we sit for a moment in the cab, listening to the metal tick as it cools, watching the mist curl above the water.

"Good morning for it," he says.

He means the overcast. He means the temperature — low forties, cold enough to keep the bugs down, not cold enough to make your fingers useless. He means the river is running at the right speed, which he can tell from the sound, which is a skill I've watched him exercise my entire life without ever fully understanding how it works.

We unload without talking. It's a choreography we've been performing since I was six years old — he takes the rods, I take the cooler and the tackle box, we both ignore the camp chairs because Frank considers sitting while fishing to be only slightly less offensive than lettuce on a sandwich. We stand. We've always stood.

He rigs my rod before his own. He's done this since I was a kid and he's never stopped, even though I've been capable of rigging my own line since I was eight. I don't mention it. He doesn't either. It's one of those father-son rituals that would disintegrate if you looked at it directly, like trying to see a faint star by staring straight at it.

We cast. The lines arc out over the gray water and land with small, separate sounds. The current takes them. We stand about ten feet apart, which is the distance Frank has always maintained — close enough to talk, far enough to not have to.

For the first hour, we don't say much. A comment about the water level. An observation about a heron that lands upstream and stands motionless in the shallows, a gray thing in a gray world, patient beyond any human capacity for patience. Frank adjusts his line. I adjust mine. The mist thins but doesn't lift. The sky brightens from charcoal to pewter.

The sandwiches come out at eight. We eat standing up, rods propped in the notched branches Frank cut and stuck into the bank thirty minutes ago, another piece of the ritual. The ham is good. The mustard is the yellow kind, French's, the only brand Frank has ever purchased. The coffee from the thermos is strong enough to dissolve a spoon.

"Your mother wants to know if you're eating enough," he says.

"Tell her yes."

"I did. She doesn't believe me." He takes a bite of sandwich. Chews. Watches the water. "She says you look thin."

"I look the same as I've always looked."

"I know. But that's thin, so she's not wrong." He balls up his sandwich wrapper, puts it in his jacket pocket. Frank has never littered in his life. I genuinely believe it would cause him physical pain.

We go back to fishing. The heron is gone. The mist is gone. The sky stays gray but the light is different now — less like dawn and more like the world has simply decided this is as bright as it's going to get today and you can take it or leave it.

"I was thinking about my dad the other day," Frank says.

He says it the way he says everything — evenly, without preamble, as if this is a normal thing to bring up on a riverbank at eight-thirty on a Saturday morning. But I know it isn't. Frank has mentioned his father maybe a dozen times in my entire life, and most of those were single sentences delivered in passing, the way you'd mention a road you used to drive on that's been closed for years.

I don't say anything. I've learned that the best response to Frank beginning a thought is to let the silence do the work. If you jump in, he'll retreat. If you ask a question, he'll deflect. But if you just stand there and keep your line in the water and let the quiet stretch out like the river itself, he'll keep going. Sometimes.

"He wasn't bad," Frank says. "I know I've probably made him sound bad. The way I don't talk about him — that makes people think the worst. But he wasn't bad. He was just—" He reels in a few feet of line. Casts again. The motion is so practiced it looks involuntary, like breathing. "He didn't know how to be good. That's different from being bad. Bad is a choice. He never chose to be bad. He just never figured out how to be the other thing."

The river moves. A fish jumps somewhere upstream — a small flash of silver that's there and gone.

"He grew up on a farm outside Jesup. His dad — my grandfather — was a piece of work. I never met him. He died before I was born. But from what my dad said, and from what he didn't say, it wasn't a house where anyone learned how to be soft."

Frank looks at his line. Adjusts his grip on the rod. His hands are large, cracked from decades of mechanical work, and they hold the rod with a gentleness that always surprises me, as if the rod is something more fragile than graphite and fiberglass.

"He hit me once."

The words drop into the morning like a stone into still water.

"Once. I was fourteen. He'd been drinking — not falling-down drunk, but enough. Enough that whatever wall he kept between his feelings and his hands came down for a second. Open hand, across the face. Not hard enough to leave a mark. Hard enough."

I reel in. Cast again. The line whispers through the guides, a small bright sound against the quiet.

"And the thing I remember — the thing I've never forgotten — is what happened after. He looked at his hand like it belonged to someone else. Like he'd just watched a stranger do something terrible and he was trying to figure out how to intervene. And he sat down on the kitchen floor. Just sat right down on the linoleum. And he put his face in his hands and he stayed there."

Frank is quiet for a long time. A minute. Two minutes. The river doesn't care about human silence; it just keeps moving, carrying whatever it carries, taking it somewhere else.

"He never apologized. Not in words. But the next morning he made pancakes, which he had never done before in his life, and they were terrible — just awful, burned on the outside and raw in the middle — and he set them in front of me and he stood there at the stove with his back to me and neither of us ate and neither of us talked and that was his apology."

I feel something in my throat that isn't a word and isn't a sound. It's just pressure. The accumulated weight of knowing your father's pain and not being able to carry it for him.

"I decided something that day," Frank says. "Standing at the bus stop after those pancakes, waiting to go to school. I decided I would never raise my hand to my kids. Never. Not once. Not even close."

"You never did."

"No. But here's the part I didn't figure out until later." He looks at me. His eyes are the same gray as the river, the same gray as the sky, and for a moment the whole world is one color — the water and the air and my father's gaze all the same shade of not-quite-silver. "I couldn't just put away the hand. I had to put away the whole volume. You understand? I couldn't figure out how to be loud without being him. I couldn't figure out how to raise my voice about anything — anger, sure, but also the rest of it. The good stuff. I turned the whole thing down because I didn't trust myself to know where the line was."

A fish takes my line. I feel the tug, the small electrical urgency of something alive at the other end, and I let it go. I don't set the hook. The fish takes the bait and moves on and I don't care because my father is telling me why he is the way he is and I have been waiting for this my entire life without knowing I was waiting for it.

"So when I didn't — when I don't—" He stops. Reels in. Casts. The mechanical motion giving him somewhere to put his hands while his voice does something it doesn't know how to do. "I want you to know it isn't because the feeling isn't there. The feeling is there. It's always been there."

"I know, Dad."

"Okay." He nods. Once. That's it. The conversation is over, because Frank has reached the end of what he can do, and pushing further would be asking him to be someone he isn't, and I would never do that. Not to him. Not to the man who packed the truck the night before and made sandwiches and drove us out here in the dark because this is how he says the thing he can't say.

We fish. The morning gets as bright as it's going to get. The cooler sits between us on the bank, sweating faintly in the cool air. At one point Frank catches a smallmouth bass, maybe two pounds, and holds it up for me to see with a look on his face that is as close to delight as Frank Callahan gets, which is a slight widening of the eyes and a barely perceptible uptick at the corner of his mouth. He releases it. It flashes once in the gray water and is gone.

Around noon, he starts packing up. The choreography in reverse — I take the cooler, he takes the rods, we load the truck and he straps everything down with bungee cords, checking twice because Frank checks everything twice.

We're in the cab, engine running, heat on because the afternoon has gone cold, and he's about to put it in gear when he stops. Looks out the windshield at the river, which is still doing what it's been doing since long before either of us was born and will be doing long after we're both gone.

"This is nice, bud," he says.

Not *I love you*. But the meaning is identical.

I open my mouth to respond and nothing comes out because the lump in my throat has been building for hours and it has chosen this particular moment to make itself immovable. So I nod. And he puts the truck in gear. And we drive home with the radio off and the heater on and the smell of river water and mustard and coffee in the cab, and it is the best day I will have for a long time, and I know it, and I let it be what it is.

The State of Iowa makes you jump through hoops to establish a nonprofit. The federal government makes you jump through flaming hoops while blindfolded. The 501(c)(3) application is twenty-eight pages long, not counting attachments, and it asks questions like "Describe your organization's planned activities in detail" and "List your organization's sources of financial support" and "Provide a narrative description of anticipated receipts and planned expenditures for your first three years of operation."

I fill it out at the kitchen table in my apartment in Cedar Falls on a Sunday afternoon in April. The table is a card table I bought at Goodwill. The chair is a folding chair. The pen is a Bic. The glamour of world-saving, as it turns out, looks a lot like doing your taxes.

**Northeast Regional Awareness Network.**

Mission: To advance community disaster preparedness, resilience education, and emergency response coordination across the Midwest and northeastern United States.

Board of Directors: Nathan P. Callahan, President and Executive Director. Dr. Mei-Lin Chen, Scientific Advisor.

I called Chen two weeks ago to ask if she'd agree to be listed. She was quiet for long enough that I thought the call had dropped.

"Scientific advisor," she said finally. "To a disaster preparedness nonprofit."

"Yes."

"Founded by a twenty-one-year-old emergency management student who has unexplained foreknowledge of future events."

"That's a characterization, but yes."

"And this is going to — what? Train communities? Distribute information?"

"Among other things."

Another long pause. Boltzmann made a sound in the background — not a meow, exactly, more like a comment. An editorial observation.

"You're building infrastructure," she said. "That's what this is. You're building something that will already be in place when—" She stopped. "When what, Nate?"

"When it's needed."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the best one I have right now."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true."

She agreed. She agreed because the data was clean and the signal was real and because Mei-Lin Chen, whatever her reservations about my epistemological shortcomings, recognized that preparation has value independent of its justification. You don't need to understand why a dam might break to see the sense in building on high ground.

I list the registered agent as myself. The principal office as my apartment, which I will need to upgrade at some point, because operating the most important nonprofit on the planet out of a studio apartment above a laundromat is going to present logistical challenges eventually. The financial support section is mostly aspiration at this point — grant applications I haven't submitted yet, fundraising strategies that exist only in my notebook, the vague promise of "community partnerships" that I will need to actually forge.

I sign where it says to sign. Date where it says to date. April 15, 2012, which is also Tax Day, which feels appropriate given the amount of paperwork involved.

Northeast Regional Awareness Network. Boring. Institutional. The kind of name that slides right off your brain, that you'd scan past in a list of local organizations without pausing, that a journalist would have to look up twice to get right. Exactly what the world needs and will never notice until it matters.

I put the application in an envelope. I put the envelope by the door so I'll remember to mail it in the morning. I sit back down at the card table and look at the kitchen of my apartment, which is small and beige and contains nothing of significance except a folding chair, a Goodwill card table, and the twenty-one-year-old who just filed the paperwork that will, if everything goes according to plan — and nothing ever does, which is the whole reason for the paperwork in the first place — put a network in place that will save lives on a scale he can't tell anyone about yet.

I open my notebook. The blue one, the one I keep locked in my desk drawer, the one no one has ever read and no one ever will.

*Every relationship I have is built on a lie I'm telling for the right reasons. Is that better or worse than the lies I told for selfish ones? Chen trusts the data but not me. Frank trusts me but doesn't know what I'm doing. Mom trusts everything because that's who she is, and that's the one that keeps me up at night — being trusted completely by someone who doesn't know what they're trusting.*

*The fishing trip was the best day. The application was the most important day. I don't think those will ever be the same day. I think that's the cost.*

I close the notebook. Lock the drawer. Go to bed in a room that smells faintly of laundry detergent from the machines running downstairs, a sound like breathing, rhythmic and impersonal, the world turning its clothes over and over in warm water and calling it clean.
