Chapter Two: The Man in the Boy

The coffee maker gurgles like it's drowning.

It's a Mr. Coffee, the twelve-cup model with the cracked carafe handle that Diane has been meaning to replace since I was in middle school. She never will. It'll sit on this counter through two more presidential administrations, one kitchen renovation that stops at the cabinets, and a brief experiment with a Keurig that Frank will declare "a scam" after six days. The Mr. Coffee will outlast the marriage's worst year, Sophie's braces, and the family dog they haven't adopted yet. It will not outlast Frank.

But right now it's just a coffee maker, doing what coffee makers do, and the sound of it is so ordinary that I have to grip the doorframe to stay upright.

The kitchen is a crime scene of normalcy. Frank sits at the table with the Waterloo-Cedar Falls Courier spread in front of him, reading glasses low on his nose, spooning Cheerios with the mechanical rhythm of a man who has eaten the same breakfast for thirty years. He's wearing a gray Carhartt t-shirt, the one with the small hole near the hem that Diane keeps threatening to throw away. His hair is reddish-brown and thinning at the crown in a way I know embarrasses him, though he'd eat glass before admitting it. He's fifty-four years old and he's alive and I can smell Barbasol and sawdust from six feet away and the floor is tilting.

"There he is," Frank says without looking up. "Thought you were going to sleep through Tuesday."

Diane is at the counter, spreading peanut butter on white bread with the focus of a surgeon. She's in a green cardigan with pockets that I know contain at least two pens, a grocery list, and a coupon for something she'll forget to buy. Her reading glasses are on top of her head. She'll spend twenty minutes looking for them after she drops Sophie off.

"Do you want toast, honey? There's toast."

I open my mouth. Nothing comes out.

It's September 2008. The kitchen radio — the actual radio, the one that sits on the windowsill above the sink — is tuned to AM 1650, and some guy is talking about the Hawkeyes' secondary. Diane's lunch-packing station occupies the entire left counter: bread, turkey, those little bags of baby carrots she buys in bulk because she read an article about school nutrition. Sophie's lunchbox is purple. It has a scratch on the front from when she dropped it on the first day of seventh grade and cried in the car, then denied crying, then didn't speak for two hours.

I know this because I was there. I was seventeen and annoyed about being late for something I can't remember anymore, and I told her to stop being a baby, and she looked at me with an expression I would not understand for another decade.

"Nate?" Diane turns, butter knife in hand. "You okay?"

"Yeah." My voice cracks. Not from emotion — from the genuine physiological reality of being seventeen, of having a larynx that hasn't finished settling into itself. I'd forgotten about the voice cracks. I'd forgotten about a lot of things. "Yeah, sorry. Morning."

Frank turns a page. The paper rustles. It's the most important sound I've ever heard.

"Juice is in the fridge," he says. "Your mother bought that pomegranate stuff again."

"It's full of antioxidants, Frank."

"It's full of something."

I walk to the fridge. My body moves differently than I remember — there's a looseness to it, a spring-loaded quality, like I'm piloting a sports car after years in a sedan with a check-engine light. I'm taller than I expected and shorter than I should be. My hand closes around the juice carton and I stand there with the refrigerator door open, staring at the contents: Diane's yogurts, Frank's Miller Lites lined up on the bottom shelf, a Tupperware of something from Sunday, Sophie's string cheese.

The cold air hits my face and I think: *In eighteen years, every plant in the yard will die in real time. The sky will go the color of a bruise. Frank will already be fourteen years dead and I will be standing in an apartment in Waterloo, alone, watching the emergency broadcast system tell me nothing useful.*

"You're letting the cold out, bud."

I close the fridge. I pour pomegranate juice into a glass and sit at the table across from my father. He smells like Monday morning. He smells like the first half of my life.

"You look tired," Diane says. She comes up behind me, puts her hand on my shoulder. Her fingers are cold from the lunch meat. "Did you sleep okay?"

"Fine," I say. "Weird dreams."

This is technically true in the way that being hit by a freight train is technically an interruption to your commute.

Frank folds the sports section and sets it aside. He looks at me over his reading glasses, and there it is — that expression I spent years misreading as disinterest. It's not disinterest. It's the face of a man who was raised to believe that concern is best expressed through proximity, not conversation. Frank Callahan has never in his life asked "How are you feeling?" He has, roughly ten thousand times, simply been in the room.

"Big week?" he asks.

"What?"

"School. Big week coming up?"

I have absolutely no idea what's happening at Cedar Falls High School this week. I have a vague, waterlogged memory of September 2008 being early in the school year — first or second week, probably. AP History with Mr. Danaher. Precalculus. The rest is fog.

"Just the usual," I say.

Frank nods. This is a complete conversation by Frank Callahan standards.

Sophie appears in the doorway like she was conjured. She's all elbows and knees and a ponytail pulled so tight it looks structural. Her braces catch the overhead light when she yawns. She's wearing an oversized hoodie — mine, I realize, one I thought I'd lost — and she has the string in her mouth already, chewing with the dedication of someone training for a competition.

"Morning, sweetheart," Diane says. "Your lunch is almost ready. Do you want carrots or cucumber?"

"Carrots." Sophie slides into her chair and pours Cheerios without looking at the bowl. Half of them make it in. She doesn't acknowledge this. "Can I have five dollars?"

"For what?"

"Existing."

Frank snorts. It's almost a laugh. For Frank, it is a laugh.

I watch my thirteen-year-old sister eat cereal, and what I feel is so far beyond anything I have vocabulary for that I just sit there, holding my juice, breathing. She's thirteen. She hasn't decided yet that I'm a disappointment. She hasn't written me off as the brother who left and didn't call. Right now I'm just her annoying older brother who steals the bathroom and never helps with anything, and that's — 

I stand up. "Bathroom," I say, and I'm out of the kitchen before anyone can respond.

I make it to the downstairs half-bath, the one with the seashell soap dish Diane bought at a craft fair in 2004, and I close the door and sit on the lid of the toilet and put my face in my hands and I do not cry. I want to. Every cell in my body wants to. But if I start, I'm not sure the sound that comes out will be something I can explain, so I just sit there and breathe and press my palms against my eyes until I see colors.

Two minutes. Maybe three. Then I flush the toilet for cover, run the water, and go back to the kitchen.

"Wash your hands?" Diane asks.

"Yes, Mom."

She nods, satisfied. Sophie is looking at me. She has an expression I can't read, or maybe I can read it and I just don't want to. She goes back to her Cheerios.

Frank drives me to school. This was the routine, I remember now — he'd drop me on his way to the job site, and I'd complain about the country station he played too loud, and we'd sit in comfortable silence for the eight-minute drive, and I had no idea any of it mattered.

The truck is a 2004 Ford F-150, white, with a toolbox bolted to the bed and a pine air freshener that stopped working in 2006 but still hangs from the mirror like a talisman. The bench seat has a permanent indent on the driver's side. There's a coffee thermos in the cupholder, stainless steel, dented.

Frank drives with one hand on the wheel at twelve o'clock. He hums along with something on the radio. I watch the streets of Cedar Falls scroll past — the Hy-Vee, the Casey's, the houses with their September lawns still green, the trees that haven't started turning yet — and I catalog every detail like a man memorizing a painting before the museum burns.

"Supposed to rain later this week," Frank says.

"Yeah?"

"Thursday maybe. Might push the Millard job."

I have no idea what the Millard job is. "That sucks," I say, which is apparently the right response, because Frank nods and takes a drink of his coffee.

He pulls up to the school entrance. Cedar Falls High. The building looks exactly the same and also like a place I invented. There are kids everywhere — on the steps, on the lawn, leaning against cars in the lot — and they are so young. They are preposterously, offensively young. They're wearing low-rise jeans and Hollister t-shirts and carrying backpacks that look too big for their bodies, and they're laughing about things that are important and stupid and important because they're stupid, and I want to stand on the hood of the truck and scream at them to pay attention because they will never be this free again.

I don't do that.

"Have a good one, bud," Frank says.

"Thanks, Dad."

Something in my voice must land differently than I intend, because Frank looks at me — really looks, the way he does maybe four times a year — and there's a beat of something between us. Then he nods.

"Go on. You'll be late."

I go.

---

AP History with Mr. Danaher is third period, and it's the first time in eighteen years I've had to sit in a desk with an attached writing surface designed for someone with the wingspan of a heron.

Danaher is talking about the 2008 presidential election. He's drawing a map on the whiteboard — red states, blue states, the whole electoral math. He's a good teacher, I remember that now. Mid-forties, beard, the kind of guy who wears ties with historical figures on them and genuinely thinks it's charming. He's asking the class to predict outcomes. Who's going to win? What states are in play?

This is, for me, like being asked to predict whether the sun will come up.

I keep my mouth shut for the first twenty minutes. I sit in the back row and I watch my classmates — seventeen-year-olds who will vote for the first time next month, who think this election is the most consequential thing that's ever happened, who have no idea that they're right but not for the reasons they think — and I practice being invisible.

It doesn't work.

"Nate. You're awfully quiet back there." Danaher's looking at me with that teacher expression, the one that means *I know you know something.* "What's your read? Virginia — red or blue this cycle?"

Virginia. I know the answer. I know the exact margin. I know the county-level breakdown. I know that northern Virginia's demographic shift is going to be the story of the night, that Mark Warner is going to win the Senate race by thirty points, that this is the election that turns Virginia from a swing state into something else entirely.

"Blue," I say. "Northern Virginia suburbs. Demographic shift. Loudon and Fairfax counties are going to run up the score."

The room is quiet.

"That's..." Danaher tilts his head. "That's a very specific analysis."

"I read a lot."

"What are you reading? Because I want to assign it." He's half-smiling, genuinely intrigued. "Most of your classmates think Virginia is safe Republican territory."

*Shut up,* says the voice in my head. *Shut up shut up shut up.*

"I think the polling data is there if you look at off-year trends," I say, because apparently I am constitutionally incapable of shutting up. "The 2006 midterms already showed the movement. Webb beat Allen. You just have to extrapolate."

Danaher stares at me. Two rows up, a girl named Rachel Kovacs turns around and mouths *what the hell?*

"Well," Danaher says. "I think you might have a future in campaign strategy, Nate."

I spend the rest of the period staring at my notebook and writing *STOP TALKING* in the margin, over and over, like a kid in detention from a horror movie.

---

Lunch is in the cafeteria, which smells exactly the way school cafeterias have always smelled and will always smell — a proprietary blend of industrial cleaner, pizza grease, and the particular funk of three hundred teenagers in a room with inadequate ventilation.

Marcus is already at our table. Marcus Wade — six-three, dark brown skin, gap-toothed grin, wearing Nike basketball shoes that are too clean for someone who actually plays basketball in them. His watch is a silver Casio, and it is, as always, three minutes fast, a fact he has never acknowledged despite years of evidence.

"Yo." He slides over to make room. "Heard you went full pundit in Danaher's class."

Word travels fast in a building this small. "I just answered a question."

"Rachel said you sounded like you were running for office."

"Rachel exaggerates."

"Rachel said you predicted Virginia going blue and cited specific counties."

"I was making conversation."

Marcus looks at me. He has this way of looking at people — direct, open, completely without guile — that made him the best friend I ever had and the easiest person I ever took for granted. In the original timeline, I borrowed three thousand dollars from Marcus Wade in 2014 to cover rent and never paid it back. I told myself I'd get to it. I told myself he understood. I told myself a lot of things. He stopped calling. I let him.

"You good?" he asks. "You seem kind of... off."

"I'm fine. Weird morning."

"Yeah, I feel that." He opens his lunch — turkey sandwich, bag of Doritos, apple he won't eat. "My mom's on this thing where she makes me bring fruit. Like I'm going to eat an apple when there's a vending machine right there."

Jake Petersen and Cody Voss show up with their trays. Jake's talking about a girl. Cody's talking about Call of Duty. They're both talking at the same time, which is a communication strategy that only works when you're seventeen and everything you're saying is essentially the same temperature of important. I eat my sandwich and I watch them, and what I see — what I can't stop seeing — is children.

Not in a condescending way. In a heartbroken way. These are kids who think the worst thing that can happen is a bad grade or a breakup, and they're right, that is the worst thing, and they should protect that ignorance with everything they have because the world is going to take it from them one piece at a time until they're sitting in an apartment in Waterloo at thirty-five, watching the sky change color and thinking, *I had everything and I spent it all on being angry.*

Marcus is telling a story about his engineering teacher, Mr. Brekke, who apparently spent an entire class period arguing with a student about whether a bridge could be built out of Popsicle sticks. Marcus is animated, funny, doing voices. The table is laughing. I'm laughing too, because Marcus is genuinely funny and because laughter is a good place to hide.

"You should do engineering club," I say. "You'd be good at it."

Marcus shrugs. "Maybe. My dad wants me to do something practical. Like business."

In the original timeline, Marcus went to Iowa State for mechanical engineering, graduated with honors, and works at John Deere now. Or worked. Will work. The tenses of my life no longer function.

"Engineering is practical," I say. "Extremely practical. Do the engineering thing."

He looks at me funny. "Okay. Thanks, career counselor."

I eat my apple. I eat Marcus's apple too, because he's not going to, and because I'm seventeen and my metabolism is an open furnace and I'm hungry in a way I haven't been in a decade.

---

Sophie is in the living room when I get home, spread across the couch with a geometry textbook open on her stomach and an expression that suggests the textbook has personally wronged her. She's chewing a hoodie string. Her ponytail has migrated to the side of her head in a way that would be concerning on an adult but reads as aerodynamic on a thirteen-year-old.

I should go upstairs. I should go to my room and close the door and try to figure out the rules of whatever this is — time travel, psychotic break, afterlife, simulation. I should not interact with my sister more than necessary until I understand what's happening.

"That's an isosceles triangle," I say, looking over her shoulder. "The two equal sides mean the base angles are equal. So if you know one base angle is fifty-two degrees, the other one is too, and the vertex is whatever's left over from one-eighty."

Sophie looks up at me. Her expression cycles through several phases: confusion, suspicion, deeper confusion.

"I didn't ask you."

"I know."

"Since when do you know geometry?"

"I've always known geometry."

"You got a C in geometry."

This is true. Seventeen-year-old Nate Callahan got a C in geometry because seventeen-year-old Nate Callahan did not do homework and studied exclusively during the passing period before tests. Thirty-five-year-old Nate Callahan has used geometry exactly four times in his adult life and remembers it only because competence compounds in weird ways over two decades.

"The C was a lifestyle choice," I say. "The vertex angle is seventy-six degrees."

Sophie stares at me for a long time. She has Diane's eyes — dark, quick, missing nothing. Then she writes down seventy-six degrees and says, without looking up, "Okay, weirdo."

I go upstairs. I close my door. I sit on the bed and I look at the Dark Knight poster and I think about how Heath Ledger has been dead for eight months and how in eighteen years the world will end and how my sister just accepted geometry help from me without the universe collapsing, and I press my hands flat against my thighs and I breathe.

---

The panic attack comes on Thursday.

I've held it together for three days. Three days of smiling at the right times and saying the right things and not screaming in the hallway when I see a poster for the Homecoming dance, because I know the DJ will play "Low" by Flo Rida at least six times and Amanda Perkins will twist her ankle during the Cupid Shuffle and the whole thing will be exactly as wonderful and idiotic as being seventeen requires.

Three days of knowing that Lehman Brothers is going to collapse in nine days. That the stock market is about to crater. That Frank has a 401(k) that will lose a third of its value by Christmas and he'll sit at the kitchen table staring at the statement without saying anything, and Diane will find him there at midnight and neither of them will talk about it.

Three days of knowing that the sky will turn yellow-gray in 2026 and every plant in the yard will die and something called the Kessari will arrive and the emergency broadcasts will cut out one by one like candles in a wind.

It hits in the bathroom between fifth and sixth period. I'm washing my hands and I look up at the mirror and I see a seventeen-year-old kid looking back at me and the kid has my eyes but they're the wrong age, there's something behind them that doesn't fit, and the room starts to breathe.

Not spin. Breathe. The walls expand and contract, the fluorescent light buzzes at a frequency I can feel in my teeth, and my chest locks up like someone has reached inside and grabbed my lungs and squeezed.

I make it to a stall. I close the door. I sit on the floor, which is tile, which is cold, which is disgusting — I'm sitting on a high school bathroom floor and I'm thirty-five years old and I can't breathe. My hands are shaking. Not trembling, shaking, the kind of shaking that starts in your fingers and moves up your arms and into your shoulders until your whole body is vibrating at a frequency that doesn't have a name.

I put my head between my knees. I try to count. I try to breathe in for four and out for four and the air won't cooperate, it comes in shallow and fast and wet-sounding, and I can hear someone's shoes squeaking in the hallway outside and a locker slamming and laughter and the normal beautiful stupid sounds of a world that doesn't know it's ending.

Frank dies at sixty-eight. Heart attack. I find out two days late because we haven't spoken in four years because I said something at Thanksgiving that I can never take back. "Maybe you should've actually been there instead of just standing in the room." I said that. I said that to a man whose entire love language is standing in the room, and I knew exactly what I was doing, and I did it anyway.

The tile is cold against my palms. My breathing sounds like an engine failing.

I know who wins every election. I know which stocks to buy. I know that the iPhone is about to change everything and that social media is going to eat the country alive and that a pandemic is coming in 2020 that will kill over a million Americans. I know that Marcus will be fine. I know that Sophie will be fine. I know that Diane will sell the house after Frank dies and move to a condo in Waverly and learn to garden and never entirely recover.

I know everything and it is not a superpower. It is a weight. It is a sentence. It is sitting on a bathroom floor in a body that doesn't match, hyperventilating, knowing that the world is going to end and that the ending starts slow, so slow, with a fight at Thanksgiving and a phone call you don't make and a letter you don't answer, and then all at once with a gray-yellow sky and the smell of dying grass.

The bathroom door opens. Footsteps. Someone uses the urinal, washes their hands, leaves. I am invisible. I am a thirty-five-year-old man sitting on a high school bathroom floor with his head between his knees, and nobody knows, and the loneliness of that is so total that it's almost funny.

I breathe. Slowly. The count works on the ninth or tenth try. In for four. Out for four. The tile is cold. The light buzzes. My hands stop shaking.

I get up. I wash my face. I go to sixth period. Nobody noticed I was gone.

---

Friday evening. The porch.

Frank is in his chair — the Adirondack, the green one, paint peeling at the armrests. He's got a Miller Lite and the tail end of the sunset and he's not doing anything. Just sitting. Frank Callahan sits on this porch at least four evenings a week from May to October, and he doesn't read or listen to music or talk on the phone. He just sits. For years I thought this was boring. For years I thought a lot of stupid things.

I come out and sit in the other chair, the white one that wobbles because one leg is shorter. I don't say anything. I don't have anything to say. Or I have everything to say — eighteen years of everything — and none of it is speakable, so the result is the same.

Frank glances at me. He takes a drink of his beer. He looks back at the yard.

We sit.

The September air is warm enough that you don't need a jacket but cool enough that you notice it on your arms. The neighbor's sprinkler is going, that slow *tch-tch-tch-tch* sweep, and somewhere down the block a kid is bouncing a basketball, irregular, the sound of someone shooting alone. The light is doing that thing it does in Iowa in September, going gold and then amber and then a color that doesn't have a name, the color of the last hour of a day when nothing happened.

Frank doesn't ask me what's wrong. He doesn't ask me how my week was, or what I'm thinking about, or whether I want to talk. Frank Callahan does not do that. What Frank Callahan does is stay. He stays in the room. He stays on the porch. He stays in the truck for the eight-minute drive and he stays at the breakfast table and he stays married and he stays employed and he stays present in the only way he knows how, which is physically, consistently, without narration.

I used to think that wasn't enough.

Five minutes. The gold light moves across the yard. A car passes. The basketball sound stops and starts again.

Seven minutes. Frank finishes his beer, sets the bottle on the armrest. He doesn't get up to get another one.

Ten minutes. The light is amber now, almost gone. The sprinkler stops. The street goes quiet in that temporary way streets do, a held breath between one set of sounds and the next.

Frank stands up. He puts his hand on my shoulder — just for a second, just the weight of it, rough and warm — and then he picks up his bottle and goes inside. The screen door closes behind him with that spring-loaded slap that sounds like every summer night I've ever lived.

I sit on the porch. I don't move. The amber goes to gray and the streetlight on the corner comes on, the sodium-vapor kind that turns everything orange, and I think: *He stayed. He didn't know why I needed him to and he stayed.*

I think: *I have eighteen years. I have eighteen years to be worth the room he takes up in the world, and I will not waste this. I will not say the thing at Thanksgiving. I will not let the silence grow until it's structural. I will not find out two days late.*

The screen door opens again. I expect Frank, but it's Sophie. She comes out in her hoodie and her socks — no shoes, Diane would kill her — and she stands in the doorway with her arms crossed, looking at me with that expression she has. The one that's already too old for her face.

"Why are you being weird?" she asks.

I look at her. My thirteen-year-old sister, all elbows and braces and borrowed hoodies, who sees everything and says nothing until she says exactly the right thing.

I don't have an answer.

She didn't expect one.

She goes back inside, and I sit on the porch, and the streetlight buzzes, and the night comes in.
