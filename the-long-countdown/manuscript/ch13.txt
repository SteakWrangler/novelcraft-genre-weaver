Marcus calls from a gas station in Waterloo, twenty minutes out, and I can hear the grin through the phone.

"Bro, Iowa is flat. Like, aggressively flat. Like the land is making a point."

"You've been driving through Iowa for three hours. This is just occurring to you now?"

"I was in denial. I kept thinking there'd be a hill. Just one. A bump. Anything."

"There's a river valley when you hit Cedar Falls. That's the best I can offer."

"I'll take it." A pause, the sound of a gas pump clicking off. "Twenty minutes?"

"Twenty minutes. Sophie's already made a casserole."

"Sophie cooks now?"

"Sophie's fifteen. She does a lot of things now."

He pulls into the driveway in a green Civic with a cracked windshield and Iowa State plates. The car looks like it's been through something — road salt damage, a dent in the rear quarter panel, a parking sticker from an apartment complex in Ames. Marcus unfolds himself from the driver's seat, all six-three of him, and the gap-toothed smile hasn't changed in six years. The Nike high-tops are new — bright white, fresh out of the box — but the Casio on his wrist is the same one, still running three minutes fast.

"Callahan." He grabs me in a hug that lifts me off the ground. Marcus has always been stronger than he looks, which is saying something because he looks strong.

"Put me down."

"Not until you admit Iowa is flat."

"Iowa is flat. Now put me down."

He does. Steps back. Looks at me the way he does every time — cataloging changes, filing them away. "You look good, man. Healthy."

"You look like you haven't slept in a week."

"Finals." He pops the trunk, pulls out a duffel bag. "Structural dynamics, fluid mechanics, and a capstone project that's actively trying to kill me. But I'm here. For the whole weekend."

Frank comes out the front door wiping his hands on a dish towel. He's fifty-three now and moves a little slower than he did when I first met him, but he's still built like a man who's spent his life under houses and inside walls. "You must be Marcus."

"Yes, sir." Marcus extends his hand. Frank takes it, and I watch them measure each other the way men do — grip, eye contact, the whole silent negotiation. Whatever Frank sees, he likes.

"Nate talks about you. Come on in."

---

Dinner is Sophie's casserole — a chicken and rice thing she learned from a YouTube video — plus a salad Diane made and bread Frank picked up from the bakery on Main. Marcus eats two plates and compliments Sophie until she turns red, which takes commitment because Sophie does not embarrass easily at fifteen.

"This is genuinely good," Marcus says, and he means it. Sophie knows he means it, which is why she thaws.

"Nate said you're studying engineering?"

"Structural. So bridges, buildings, load-bearing walls. That kind of thing."

Frank leans forward. This is his territory. "You know anything about residential foundation work?"

And they're off. Marcus and Frank spend twenty minutes talking about soil compaction and frost lines and the specific challenges of building on Iowa clay, and I watch it happen — the way Marcus draws Frank out, asks the right questions, treats the knowledge of a plumber with the same respect he'd give a professor. It's not performance. Marcus is genuinely interested. He's always been genuinely interested.

"Your boy here helped me replace a sewer main last summer," Frank says, gesturing at me with his fork. "Dug the trench himself."

"Nate dug a trench?"

"Eight feet of it."

Marcus looks at me with open delight. "Nathaniel Callahan dug a trench."

"Don't make it a thing."

"It's a thing. It's absolutely a thing. The kid who used to calculate the optimal path across campus to avoid walking on grass — dug a trench."

Sophie grins. "He complained the whole time."

"I didn't complain."

"You said, and I'm quoting: 'I went to college for this.'"

"That's not complaining. That's observation."

Diane laughs — quiet, watching everything. She likes Marcus. I can tell because she brought out the good napkins, the cloth ones she saves for holidays and people she's decided matter. She hasn't said much tonight, but she put her hand on Marcus's shoulder when she set his plate down, and that's Diane's vocabulary. Touch. Proximity. The language of a woman who came back from a very dark edge and learned to say things without words.

After dinner, Sophie corners Marcus in the kitchen while he's helping clear plates. I'm in the hallway and I hear her say, low: "Are you actually his friend, or are you one of those people who just shows up when things are going well?"

Marcus, to his credit, doesn't flinch. "I've known your brother since he was seventeen and angry about everything. I've seen him at his worst. I'm still here."

Sophie studies him. Whatever test she's running, he passes.

"Okay," she says. "You can stay."

---

The back deck after dark. Cedar Falls in July, which means the air is thick and the crickets are running a shift rotation. The deck boards are pressure-treated pine, screwed down with stainless steel — Frank and I built this in the summer after I arrived, my first real project, and the wood has aged into a silver-gray that looks deliberate even though it's just weather.

Marcus stretches his legs out, a beer in his hand. I've got water. He noticed an hour ago but didn't say anything. Now, settling in, he looks at the glass.

"You stopped drinking."

"Yeah."

"Good."

He takes a pull from his beer. I drink my water. The crickets fill the silence and neither of us rushes to break it.

This is the thing about Marcus — the thing that six years has built. We don't need to narrate. We've passed the point where absence requires explanation or silence requires filling. He drove three hours to sit on a deck and drink a beer next to me, and that's enough. That's the whole thing.

"Priya wants to meet you," he says after a while.

"Bring her next time."

"She's convinced you're fictional. She says no one is as loyal as I describe you."

"You describe me as loyal?"

"I describe you as a pain in the ass who happens to also be loyal. There's a distinction." He picks at the label on his beer. "She's good, man. She's a mechanical engineer, which means she's smarter than me, which means I'm terrified all the time. But she's good."

"I'm glad."

"I want you to be there. When I propose."

I look at him. "You're proposing?"

"Not yet. Maybe next year. But when I do — I want you there. You'll be my best man, right? I mean, that's not even a question, that's a statement."

Something catches in my chest. Best man. The kind of thing people ask when they've decided you're permanent. "Yeah. Of course."

"Good." He grins. "You'll have to give a speech."

"I'll manage."

"A good speech. Not that thing you do where you're technically eloquent but emotionally evasive."

"I don't do that."

"Nate. You absolutely do that."

He's right. He's always right about me, which is both the comfort and the cost of being known.

We sit for a while longer. A car passes on the street. Frank's left the kitchen light on — the window throws a yellow rectangle across the lawn, and I can see Diane's shadow moving behind the glass, cleaning up, her movements steady and practiced. The Callahan house at night. The most ordinary thing in the world, and I have never stopped being grateful for it.

"You're different, man," Marcus says.

I wait.

"Focused. Reliable. Whatever you're building with NEORAN — it's real. I can see it." He pauses. The Casio glints on his wrist. "But there's something you're carrying. Something underneath."

The air doesn't change. The crickets don't stop. But the moment shifts, the way a load redistributes when you add weight to a beam — invisible unless you know what to look for.

"I don't need to know what it is," Marcus says. "You don't have to tell me. But I see it. I've seen it for a while."

Six years. He's been watching for six years, patient as bedrock, and now he's naming it. Not demanding. Not prying. Just laying the observation on the table between us and letting it sit there.

"When I can tell you," I say, "I will."

He nods. Files it. The promise is logged in whatever system Marcus uses to keep track of the people he loves — and he does love me, in the way men who grew up without easy affection learn to love: through presence, through return, through the accumulated evidence of years.

"Okay," he says.

He finishes his beer. I finish my water. We go inside.

---

Three months later, different room, different frequency entirely.

The Cedar Falls Community Development Board meets in the basement of the public library, which someone has tried to make professional with folding tables and a projector screen that keeps curling at the bottom corner. I'm here representing NEORAN — we've been tracking housing access data for the county, and tonight's agenda includes a proposal for a new residential development on the east side.

I know the developer's name before he walks in. Derek Holt. I've been reading the proposal documents for a week.

He enters the room and it recalibrates around him. He's wearing a charcoal suit that fits like it was cut for his body — which it probably was — and the Rolex catches the fluorescent light. His hair is shorter than I remember, sandy blond and styled with the kind of precision that's meant to look effortless. He's twenty-three and he moves like he's forty, like he's already arrived at some destination the rest of us are still traveling toward.

He scans the room, assessing sight lines and allegiances in one sweep, and when his eyes land on me, something sparks. Recognition. Not surprise — Derek doesn't get surprised — but interest. Genuine interest.

"Nate Callahan." He crosses the room and extends his hand. His grip is firm and calibrated. "I heard you were in Cedar Falls, but I assumed you'd be doing something boring."

"Community development nonprofits aren't boring?"

"They're usually boring. But you're here, so I'm reassessing."

The meeting unfolds. Derek presents his development plan — forty-eight single-family homes on a parcel east of the university. The renderings are polished, the financials are tight, and his delivery is flawless. He knows his audience: retirees, small business owners, a city planner who's been in the role since 1998. He speaks their language without condescending, references local concerns without pandering, and handles questions with the easy authority of someone who did his homework and enjoyed doing it.

He's good. That's the uncomfortable part. He's very good.

When it's my turn, I present NEORAN's analysis. The proposed development sits on a parcel that's currently zoned for mixed use, and the residential conversion would eliminate the possibility of affordable housing units that the east side actually needs. I have the data — income demographics, commute patterns, the gap between median rent and median wage. I present it clean. No rhetoric, just numbers.

Derek watches me present the way I watched him. Measuring. Cataloging. Filing away.

The board asks questions. Derek and I end up in a back-and-forth about density requirements and tax increment financing that goes four rounds deeper than anyone else in the room can follow. The city planner looks between us like he's watching tennis. We're both enjoying ourselves, and we both know the other one is enjoying it, and that shared awareness is its own kind of currency.

The board tables the decision for further review. Derek catches my eye as the room clears and tilts his head toward the door. An invitation.

---

There's a bar two blocks from the library — dark wood, brass fixtures, the kind of place that calls itself a pub and means it. Derek orders a bourbon neat. I order club soda.

He notices. He doesn't ask.

"Nonprofit housing advocacy," he says, settling into the booth. "That's your play?"

"It's not a play."

"Everything's a play, Callahan. The question is whether you know you're making one." He sips his bourbon. "You were good in there. The data was solid, the delivery was clean. You made me look like the guy who doesn't care about poor people, which is impressive since I actually do care about poor people. I just also care about making money."

"Those things are in tension more often than you'd think."

"They're always in tension. The question is whether you let the tension paralyze you or you use it." He leans back. Studies me. The blue eyes are the same ones I remember from Berkeley — sharp, assessing, constantly running calculations. "You went from burnout to nonprofit. That's a hell of a rebrand."

"It wasn't a rebrand."

"It was a pivot, then. A strategic repositioning. You left tech — which, for someone with your brain, was the obvious move — and you landed here. Cedar Falls, Iowa. Running a nonprofit that barely has funding. And yet you're the most interesting person in that room by a factor of ten. Why?"

"You're also in that room."

He grins. Takes the compliment like a man who's used to receiving them. "I am. Which makes it even more interesting. Two people from the same program, same cohort, same IQ bracket — and we ended up on opposite sides of a zoning dispute in Cedar Falls. The universe has a sense of humor."

I drink my club soda. Derek drinks his bourbon. The jukebox in the corner plays something by Springsteen — the quiet stuff, not the anthems.

"Tell me about the real estate," I say.

"Residential development. I've got three projects in the pipeline — Cedar Falls, Waterloo, and one in Des Moines that's going to make me a lot of money." He says it without apology, without performance. He's just stating facts. "My dad lost everything when I was eighteen. You remember."

"I remember."

"So I learned the lesson. You build assets. You build equity. You build things that can't be taken away by a market correction or a bad quarter."

"Your dad didn't lose everything because of a market correction."

"No. He lost everything because he trusted people and they screwed him. Which is a different lesson but the same result." He turns the bourbon glass in his fingers. The Rolex — his grandfather's, the only thing that survived the ruin — glints in the low light. "I build things, Nate. That's what I do. I look at a piece of land and I see what it could be, and then I make it that. You do the same thing, except your raw material is communities instead of real estate."

"Those aren't the same."

"They're more similar than you want them to be."

He's not wrong about that either, which is the problem with Derek. He's not wrong often enough to dismiss.

"What are you building?" I ask.

"Wealth." He takes a drink. "What else is there?"

"A lot of things."

"Name one that doesn't require wealth to sustain it."

I think about NEORAN. The grant applications. The conversations with foundations. The reality that every mission-driven organization runs on money, and the money has to come from somewhere, and the somewhere is usually people like Derek. He sees me thinking it.

"Exactly," he says.

"That doesn't make you right."

"It doesn't make me wrong, either."

We talk for another hour. I learn that Derek is meticulous about his projects — he knows every subcontractor, every permit timeline, every variance in the building code. He's not a speculator. He builds carefully and he builds well. I learn that he reads urban planning journals and follows housing policy debates, not because he cares about policy but because policy creates opportunity, and opportunity is his native language. I learn that he's lonely in a way he'll never say out loud — that the Rolex and the suit and the charcoal confidence are load-bearing walls, and behind them is a twenty-three-year-old who watched his father come apart and decided he'd never be that vulnerable.

None of which I'd say to him. Derek doesn't want to be understood. He wants to be respected.

"I heard you're dating someone," he says as we're closing out. "Elena something. Runs a water engineering firm?"

"How do you know that?"

"Cedar Falls is a small town and I pay attention. Is it serious?"

"Getting there."

"Good. You need someone who can keep up with you." He finishes his bourbon. Sets the glass down with precision. "Most people can't."

"Including you?"

He laughs. Real, genuine, surprised. "Including me. But I keep up differently. I keep up because I see the machine, same as you." He stands. Puts on his coat — dark wool, well-fitted. "You know what I like about you, Callahan?"

I wait.

"You're the only person I've met who's as strategic as me but pretends not to be."

The words land in the space between us and sit there. I want to deny it. I want to say he's wrong, that what I do isn't strategy, that the choices I've made are driven by conviction and not calculation. But the denial won't come. Because there's a version of what he said that's true — the version where every careful step I've taken, every relationship I've built, every skill I've developed has been in service of something planned. Something I haven't told anyone about.

Marcus sees the weight. Derek sees the architecture. They're both looking at the same thing from different angles.

"That's not a compliment," I say.

"It's an observation. Compliments are for people who need validation." He buttons his coat. "I'll see you at the next board meeting. Bring better data — you almost had me tonight."

"I did have you. The board tabled it."

"Tabling isn't winning. Tabling is overtime." He grins. "Goodnight, Callahan."

He walks out into the Cedar Falls night — October cold, early frost — and I sit in the booth with my empty club soda and the residue of a conversation that left marks.

---

I drive home. The house is dark except for the kitchen light, which Frank always leaves on. A habit from his years of early morning service calls — the light meant someone was awake, or at least that the house was expecting you back.

I sit in the driveway for a minute. The engine ticks as it cools.

Two men looked at me this year and told me what they saw. Marcus saw something carried. Derek saw something calculated. The difference between them is that Marcus said it because he cares about me, and Derek said it because he recognized himself.

Both of them are right.

I go inside. Lock the door. Check on Sophie — asleep, headphones still in, laptop open to a chemistry textbook. I close the laptop, remove the headphones, pull the blanket up. Fifteen years old and already smarter than most adults I've met. She doesn't need me to do any of this. I do it anyway.

Frank's boots are by the back door. Diane's reading glasses are on the kitchen counter, folded on top of a novel — she's been reading again, which is something she couldn't do a year ago, when the concentration wouldn't hold. The house holds its small evidence of the people living in it, and I move through these artifacts like a curator, noting each one, filing it under proof.

Proof of what?

That this is real. That the family I chose chose me back. That whatever Derek saw — the strategy, the calculation, the architecture beneath the surface — it doesn't negate what Marcus saw. The weight I carry isn't a performance. It's a cost. And the fact that I carry it strategically doesn't make it lighter.

Elena texts at eleven: *How was your week? I missed you.*

I type back: *Complicated. Two old friends. Very different conversations.*

*Tell me about them.*

I start to. I describe Marcus on the back deck, the comfortable silence, the beer and the water. I describe the board meeting, the bourbon, the verbal chess match with someone who reads the room the way I do. I don't name the thing that both of them noticed — the gap between what I show and what I carry — but Elena is sharp enough to read the outline of it in what I don't say.

*You're going to tell me eventually,* she writes. She's said this before. It's becoming a refrain.

*I know.*

*I'm patient. But I'm also paying attention.*

I set the phone down. Lie in the dark. The ceiling of my room in the Callahan house, the same ceiling I've stared at since I was eighteen, and I catalog the people who are waiting for me to be ready.

Marcus, who sees the weight and trusts me to carry it until I can set it down.

Elena, who sees the gap and trusts me to close it when I'm able.

Derek, who sees the strategy and doesn't care about the rest.

Frank. Diane. Sophie. Who see a son and brother and don't need to see anything else.

I'm twenty-three years old, and the countdown I started when I was seventeen is still running. The number changes every year — the deadline shifts as the plan sharpens — but the fundamental architecture remains. I'm building something. Multiple things. The nonprofit is real. The relationships are real. The person I've become in Cedar Falls is real.

But underneath all of it, structural and silent, is the thing I haven't said.

Derek is right. I am strategic. The question — the one that keeps me up at night, the one that Marcus almost asked and Elena keeps circling — is whether the strategy serves the people I love or only serves the plan.

I don't have the answer yet.

The kitchen light stays on. Frank's habit, my inheritance. A house that waits up for you, even when you're already home.
