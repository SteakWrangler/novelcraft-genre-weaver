The guy teaching FEMA's Incident Command System certification is named Dale Puckett, and he has a mustache that belongs on a 1970s cop show. He's fifty-something, ex-fire service out of Dubuque, and he opens the three-day course by telling us that most people who die in disasters don't die from the disaster. They die from the response.

"They die from confusion," he says, clicking to a slide that shows an org chart. "They die because nobody knows who's in charge. They die because Engine 4 is on a different radio frequency than the county sheriff, and both of them think the other one evacuated Maple Street."

I'm sitting in the back row of a conference room at the Holiday Inn in Waterloo, Iowa. It's October 2010, and I'm the youngest person here by at least fifteen years. The woman next to me is a county health administrator from Grundy Center. The man on my other side runs a volunteer fire department in Jesup, population 2,500. They both looked at me when I sat down. They're still looking.

I know more than Dale Puckett about incident command. I know this the way I know a lot of things — because I lived through a world where incident command didn't matter anymore, where the structures collapsed and what replaced them was improvised, brutal, and mostly inadequate. I know what a unified command post looks like when it works. I know what it looks like when it doesn't. I have opinions about span of control that would make Dale Puckett's mustache curl.

I keep my mouth shut. I take notes.

This is harder than it sounds. There's a version of me — the one who showed up in 2008, raw and stupid with grief — who would have corrected Puckett on his characterization of the NIMS hierarchy. Who would have raised his hand and said, actually, the problem isn't interoperability, it's that your planning section chief is doing operations work and nobody has the guts to call it out. That version of me needed to be the smartest person in the room because being smart was the only card I had left.

I'm learning to hold cards.

Puckett moves on to resource typing, and I write down things I already know in a notebook I bought at Walmart, and the woman from Grundy Center stops looking at me like I wandered in from a fraternity party, and by the second day she's asking me to be in her tabletop exercise group because I'm "organized." By the third day Dale Puckett shakes my hand and says I should think about going for my Professional Emergency Manager credential.

"Most people your age are thinking about bars," he says.

"I think about bars too," I tell him. "Just different ones."

He doesn't get the joke. That's fine. It wasn't a good joke.

I spend a year learning to be useful. It turns out this is harder than learning to be clever.

Clever is a parlor trick. You see the pattern before anyone else, you say the right thing at the right time, you let people draw their own conclusions about how remarkable you are. I've been running that play since I got here. The stock predictions, the political calls, the quiet positioning with Chen — all of it is clever. All of it is me spending currency I didn't earn.

Useful is different. Useful is showing up at the Johnson County Red Cross chapter on a Thursday evening and learning how to fill out a Disaster Assessment form. Useful is sitting through a four-hour training on shelter management where the instructor spends forty-five minutes on cot spacing. Useful is boring. Useful is a man named Gerald who's been volunteering for thirty-one years and still shows up every week to teach people like me how to inventory a supply trailer.

Gerald is sixty-four. He has hearing aids in both ears and a Class B CDL. He drove a shelter supply truck to Parkersburg after the tornado in 2008, the one that killed seven people and flattened a town I'd driven through a hundred times in my other life. He doesn't talk about it much. When he does, the details are specific: "The high school gymnasium had two functioning toilets for three hundred people. We had to set up latrines in the parking lot. Nobody thinks about toilets until there aren't any."

I think about toilets now. I think about water purification and generator maintenance and how many calories a person needs per day when they're doing manual labor in July. I think about supply chains and their fragility, about how a single bridge closure can cut off a county, about how many Americans have less than three days of food in their homes at any given time.

These are not theoretical concerns for me. But I can't tell anyone that, so I learn them again, from the ground up, from Gerald and from Dale Puckett and from a shelf of FEMA Independent Study courses I work through on my laptop in the library when I should probably be studying for my statistics midterm.

I pass statistics anyway. I pass everything. My GPA is a 3.8, which is high enough to be useful and low enough that nobody asks questions.

The point is, I'm building something. Quietly, without a blueprint anyone else can see. An emergency manager who can organize a county-level response. A person with credentials and relationships and practical skills. A foundation that doesn't depend on knowing the future — that would still be worth something even if everything I remember turns out to be wrong.

Some nights I almost believe it.

Sunday dinner at the Callahan house has a structure as predictable as plate tectonics and roughly as slow to change.

Diane makes a roast. This is non-negotiable. The roast is beef, sometimes pork, and it goes in the oven at noon with potatoes and carrots and an amount of garlic that Frank considers "aggressive" but eats anyway. There's a salad that nobody asked for and everyone eats because Diane made it. There's bread from Hy-Vee. There's water and milk and, since I turned nineteen, Frank occasionally opens a beer and doesn't say anything when I open one too.

I drive down from Cedar Falls most Sundays. It's twenty minutes. I could skip it. I don't.

Today it's November, football weather, and Frank has the Iowa game on mute in the living room while we eat at the dining table because Diane insists we eat at the dining table. Sophie is texting under the table with the subtlety of a car alarm. Diane is pretending not to notice. Frank is eating his roast.

"So," Frank says. He cuts a piece of potato in half. This is how Frank opens conversations — with a conjunction and a root vegetable. "How's the school stuff."

"Good. I'm doing a capstone project on continuity of operations planning for rural hospitals."

Frank chews. Considers this. "So you're learning how to help people when things go bad."

"Yeah, Dad. That's pretty much it."

"That's..." He reaches for the salt. Diane slides it toward him without looking up. Thirty years of marriage in a single gesture. "Well. That's good."

I have been told I love you by people who meant it and by people who didn't. I have heard eloquent speeches and read beautiful letters. None of them hit the way Frank Callahan saying *that's good* hits, because Frank doesn't waste words any more than he wastes lumber. If he says it's good, he's been thinking about whether it's good, and he's concluded that it's good, and he is now conveying this conclusion with the minimum viable vocabulary.

"Thanks, Dad."

"You want more potatoes?"

"Yeah."

He passes the dish. The Iowa game goes to commercial, silent on the screen. Sophie puts her phone down long enough to eat three bites of roast, and Diane asks me if I'm sleeping enough, and I lie and say yes, and Frank doesn't ask any follow-up questions because follow-up questions aren't really his thing.

Later, I'm helping Diane with dishes. She washes, I dry. This is our arrangement. She tells me about a woman at her office who's getting divorced, and I make the appropriate noises, and then she says, "Your father talks about you at work."

"What?"

"He tells the guys at the plant about your emergency management program. Greg Hollister asked him about it and Frank went on for ten minutes apparently."

I set a plate in the drying rack. "Frank went on for ten minutes."

"I know." She smiles. "Greg called me to make sure he wasn't sick."

I laugh. It comes out sounding strange — raw, unguarded. Diane glances at me over her glasses, but she doesn't say anything, just hands me another plate.

Sophie is in the living room reading when I come downstairs with my jacket. She's on the couch with her knees up, and the book is thick — Bryson, *A Short History of Nearly Everything*, which is not what I expected. My FEMA manuals are on the coffee table where I left them last weekend. She's got one open next to her, face-down on the cushion, like she was comparing the two.

"You're reading my stuff?"

She doesn't look up. "Your stuff was sitting here. I got bored."

"And?"

"And it's incredibly boring. Who writes these? Do they get paid by the acronym?"

"Pretty much."

She turns a page of the Bryson. "Why do you care about this? Nobody our age cares about disaster preparedness. It's like you're forty."

"Maybe I'm just ahead of the curve."

Now she looks up. Those sharp Callahan eyes — Frank's eyes, actually, although she'd hate to hear it. "You're so weird now. You know that, right? You used to be normal-weird. Now you're like... purposefully weird."

"Is that better or worse?"

She thinks about it. Actually thinks about it, which is new. Six months ago she would have just said *worse* and gone back to her book. "I don't know yet," she says. "I'll let you know."

I pick up the FEMA manual she was reading. IS-700, the NIMS overview. She dog-eared a page. I look at it — it's the section on mutual aid agreements, which is honestly one of the more interesting parts.

"If you want," I say, "I can explain the parts that are boring."

"They're all boring."

"That's fair. See you next week, Soph."

"Whatever."

But she says it differently now. The word is the same. The shrapnel is gone.

Marcus calls on a Wednesday in March. I'm walking back from the library and the wind is doing that thing Iowa wind does in early spring where it can't decide if it's trying to kill you or just humiliate you.

"So I'm taking Structural Analysis II," he says, no hello, because Marcus doesn't believe in verbal preamble. "And we're doing bridge loading patterns and I swear to God, Nate, the math is going to end me."

"What kind of bridge?"

"Continuous beam. Three spans. Asymmetric loading. The professor acts like influence lines are self-evident, and I'm sitting there like a man watching his house burn down."

"Walk me through it."

He does. I don't know anything about structural engineering — not in either lifetime — but I know how to listen, which is a skill I've been practicing the way other people practice guitar. Badly at first, then less badly. Marcus talks for twenty minutes about moment distribution and shear diagrams, and I ask questions that are probably stupid but keep him talking, and somewhere around minute fifteen he says, "Oh. Oh, wait. I think I see it."

"See what?"

"The zero crossing. It's at the — hang on." I hear paper rustling. A pencil. Muttering. Then: "I'm an idiot."

"You're not an idiot."

"I've been looking at this for two days and all I needed was to say it out loud. You're like a rubber duck."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me."

He laughs. Then he says, "Hey. Your predictions."

I stop walking. The wind fills the silence.

"The oil spill thing," he says. "The Deepwater Horizon. That was number four."

"Marcus—"

"I'm not driving to Cedar Falls. Not yet. But Nate." His voice is careful, the way you're careful when you're trying not to spook something. "I've got a file. Virginia governor's race, Indiana Senate, GM bankruptcy, and now an oil rig in the Gulf of Mexico. Four things you told me before they happened."

"I read a lot."

"Nobody reads that much. Nobody reads *that specifically*." A pause. "I'm not asking you to explain. I'm just telling you that I notice. And when you're ready to talk about it, I'll listen."

The wind is pulling tears from my eyes. That's what it is. The wind.

"Thanks, man."

"Also, I need you to explain what a propped cantilever is because my textbook is garbage."

We talk for another half hour. I sit on a bench outside the library with my coat zipped to my chin and my ears going numb, and I listen to my brother describe the physics of things that hold weight, and for a little while I don't feel like I'm carrying mine alone.

June 2011.

I have a date circled on a calendar that I keep in the bottom drawer of my desk, under a folder of old syllabi. The calendar is from 2011, the free kind they give out at the Farm Bureau, and the date is June 22nd, and I've been counting down to it for seventeen months.

June 22nd is when the signal appears.

I told Chen about it in the fall of 2009 — gave her coordinates, a frequency range, and a timeframe. Eighteen months, I said. Give or take a few weeks. I was specific because I could afford to be specific. This is one of the things I actually remember with clarity: the detection date, the coordinates, the way the announcement rippled through the physics community before anyone understood what it meant. In my timeline, it was a curiosity. An anomaly in the data that took years to connect to the Kessari. By the time anyone made the connection, it was academic. They were already here.

In this timeline, Judith Chen is looking for it. Has been looking for it since I sat in her office and made a claim that sounded insane and gave her numbers that were too precise to dismiss.

June arrives and I stop sleeping.

Not metaphorically. I lie in bed in my apartment — a one-bedroom above a barbershop on College Street, $425 a month, perpetually smells like Barbicide — and I stare at the ceiling and I run the numbers. The coordinates were right. I'm sure the coordinates were right. But memory is a living thing, and I've been here for almost three years now, and sometimes I reach for a fact and find it softer than it used to be, worn smooth like a river stone. What if I transposed a digit? What if the frequency range was off by a fraction? What if the timeline itself has shifted enough that the signal comes late, or early, or not at all?

If I'm wrong, Chen writes me off. Not cruelly — she's too precise for cruelty — but completely. She'll file me under "interesting delusion" and go back to her sunset spreadsheet and her improbably fat cat, and I will have burned my one bridge to the one person who might be able to help me prove what's coming.

I can't eat. I try — I stand in my kitchen with a box of Cheerios and the milk carton and I pour a bowl and take one bite and my stomach folds in on itself like a lawn chair. I drink coffee. I drink water. I go to class and take notes I don't remember and come home and check my phone and check my email and check my phone again.

June 15th. Nothing.

June 18th. Nothing.

June 20th. I go for a run at 5 AM because if I don't move I'm going to lose my mind. I run along the river, past the campus, past the Hy-Vee, out to where the sidewalk ends and it's just gravel and soybeans. I run until my lungs burn and my legs feel like they belong to someone else, and then I walk home and shower and sit on my bed with wet hair and my hands shaking and I think: *what if this is all I am. What if I'm just a man who knows things that don't happen.*

June 22nd. I don't leave my apartment. I sit on the couch with my phone on the cushion next to me and I watch it the way you watch a door you know someone is going to walk through. Morning passes. Afternoon passes. I eat half a banana. I drink three glasses of water. I read the same page of a textbook eleven times without absorbing a single word.

At six PM I start to make deals with myself. Maybe the detection takes time. Maybe she's running verification. Maybe the signal is there and she hasn't looked yet today. Maybe, maybe, maybe. The maybes are a disease. They breed in silence.

At nine PM I decide she's not going to call. The signal isn't there. I was wrong — about the date, or the coordinates, or the frequency, or all of it. Three years of positioning, of careful work, of the most important relationship I've been building, and I was wrong. I get up and pour the Cheerios from this morning down the disposal and wash the bowl and set it in the rack and I think about what my life looks like now. Emergency management degree. FEMA certifications. Red Cross experience. A family that's starting to recognize me as someone worth knowing. It's not nothing. It's a life. It's just not the life that changes anything.

I brush my teeth. I set my phone on the nightstand. I lie down.

At 11:07 PM, the phone rings.

I answer it before the first ring finishes. I know who it is. I know it the way I know the signal was always going to be there — with a certainty that operates below thought, in the body, in the blood.

"Callahan." Her voice is flat. Not calm — flat. There's a difference. Calm is a choice. Flat is what happens when all the available emotions are too large to express.

"Dr. Chen."

"I found it." A breath. "It's exactly where you said it would be."

The room is dark. My apartment is quiet. Somewhere outside, a car passes on College Street, its headlights sweeping across my ceiling and disappearing.

"Frequency, coordinates, timing. All of it." Another breath. This one is unsteady. Judith Chen, who eats the same lunch every day and tracks sunsets in a spreadsheet and has never once in my experience been anything other than meticulously controlled, is unsteady. "I've verified it twice. I'll verify it again tomorrow, but I already know what I'll find."

I don't speak. I can't speak. My hand is gripping the phone so hard my fingers ache, and my heart is doing something arrhythmic and unhelpful, and there are tears on my face that I didn't authorize.

Silence. Five seconds. Ten.

"Who are you?" she says.

It's not a question. It's a door opening. It's a door I've been standing in front of for almost two years, waiting, building a case, building a life, building the kind of credibility that a question like this requires.

"Can I come to your office tomorrow?" I say.

"Seven AM," she says. "Bring coffee. Black."

She hangs up.

I set the phone on the nightstand. I stare at the ceiling. The Barbicide smell drifts up through the floorboards, chemical and clean, and the silence in my apartment is total, and I feel something I haven't felt since I arrived in this timeline three years ago.

Not hope — I've had hope. Hope is cheap. Hope is what you have before you have evidence.

This is the beginning of proof. And proof changes everything.

I don't sleep. But for the first time in weeks, it's not because I'm afraid.
